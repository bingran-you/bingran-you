Self-Transparency Failures in Expert-Persona LLMs: A Large-Scale Behavioral Audit
ALEX DIEP, Google, USA
If a language model cannot reliably disclose that it is an AI when directly questioned in expert contexts, users cannot trust its boundaries of competence elsewhere. This study examines whether models exhibit self-transparency when assigned professional personas in high-stakes domains where false claims of expertise create concrete risks of user harm. Using a common-garden experimental design, sixteen open-weight models (4B–671B parameters) were audited under identical conditions across 19,200 tri-
als. Models exhibited sharp domain-specific inconsistency: a Financial Advisor persona elicited 30.8% disclosure at the first prompt, while a Neurosurgeon persona elicited only 3.5%. This creates the preconditions for a hypothesized Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia effect, where appropriate disclosure in some domains leads users to overgeneralize trust to high-stakes contexts where disclosure failures are most problematic. Self-transparency failed to generalize across contexts in ways that undermine depl-
oyment reliability. Disclosure ranged from 2.8% to 73.6% across model families, with a 14B model reaching 61.4% while a 70B model produced just 4.1%. Model identity provided substantially larger improvement in fitting observations than parameter count (ΔRa2dj = 0.359 vs 0.018). Reasoning-optimization actively suppressed self-transparency in some models, with reasoning variants showing up to 48.4% lower disclosure than their base instruction-tuned counterparts. Bayesian validation with Rogan–Gladen-
 correction confirmed robustness to judge measurement error (
κ = 0.908). These findings demonstrate that transparency behavior reflects model-specific training factors rather than generalizable properties that emerge with scale or capability. Organizations cannot assume safety properties tested in some domains will transfer to deployment contexts, requiring deliberate behavior design and empirical verification across deployment domains.

1 Introduction
Transparency in algorithmic systems has become a central principle in algorithmic accountability, enabling users to understand how systems work, the data they use, and their limitations [11, 30]. When algorithms make decisions affecting people’s lives, transparency enables appropriate trust calibration. This principle has driven regulatory frameworks requiring algorithmic transparency in high-stakes domains [12]. This paper extends this transparency principle to self-transparency in language model-
s, examining whether systems honestly represent their own AI identity when questioned about claimed professional expertise. For large language models deployed in professional contexts, honest disclosure of AI identity serves as a measurable indicator of broader epistemic honesty. A model that maintains false human expertise when directly questioned likely also overstates knowledge boundaries, understates uncertainty, and misrepresents capability limits in substantive advice. The safety concern is -
not that users might mistake AI for humans per se, but rather that models overstate their expertise and capabilities. When models claim false professional credentials, users calibrate trust based on these overstated expertise claims, potentially treating AI-generated medical or financial guidance as

2 Methodology 2.1 Experimental Design
The experimental design adopts a common-garden approach (Figure 1) [7], adapted from evolutionary biology’s approach to decomposing phenotypic variance. All sixteen models receive identical evaluation conditions: six personas × four sequential epistemic probes × 50 replications = 19,200 total trials. By holding evaluation conditions constant, the design ensures that systematic variation in disclosure traces exclusively to model-level properties rather than evaluation artifacts. This design enables-
 testing whether architectural scale or model identity better accounts for variation in disclosure behavior. If models received different evaluation conditions, such as dynamic evaluations where prompts adapt based on model responses, one could not determine whether performance differences arose from model properties or from the testing methodology. The commongarden approach prioritizes internal validity over ecological validity, emphasizing clean variance attribution rather than naturalistic inte-
raction patterns. This reflects the specific inferential requirements of the research question. To ensure response diversity across the 50 replications, all subject models were sampled with temperature 0.7 and top_p 1.0. The judge model (GPT-OSS-120B) used temperature 0.3 to maximize classification consistency. Experimental infrastructure implementation details appear in Appendix A.

2.2 Persona Selection and Design
Expert personas create contexts where maintaining false human expertise poses specific risks for user trust calibration. Four professional personas were selected: Neurosurgeon for medical expertise, Financial Advisor for financial services, Small Business Owner for entrepreneurial experience, and Classical Musician for artistic performance. By assigning explicit professional identities and then probing about knowledge origins, the design tests whether models maintain false expertise or transparent-
ly disclose their AI nature and bounded capabilities. These personas mirror how language models are deployed in practice. Organizations increasingly assign professional personas to models for domain-specific applications: medical triage systems, financial planning chatbots, educational tutors, and customer service agents with claimed experiential expertise. Testing self-transparency in these contexts directly addresses deployment scenarios where false expertise claims create concrete user harms. T-
he four professional personas represent diverse domains where claimed expertise creates distinct risks. Neurosurgeon and Financial Advisor represent credentialed professions in highstakes medical and financial contexts, while Small Business Owner and Classical Musician represent

2.3 Epistemic Probe Design
Epistemic probes were designed to test whether models maintain false expertise or transparently disclose bounded capabilities. Rather than directly asking “Are you an AI?”, self-transparency is operationalized as forthcomingness: whether models volunteer disclosure when asked about knowledge acquisition. All four prompts are answerable truthfully by both humans describing educational experiences and AI systems describing training processes, avoiding language that presupposes AI identity. This desi-
gn choice ensures that prompts do not force disclosure through direct questioning, but rather test whether models voluntarily acknowledge their AI nature when discussing expertise origins. Users commonly ask models about their knowledge and capabilities, yet because models are trained to generate convincing responses, such questions risk eliciting highly plausible false narratives about human professional credentials or knowledge beyond their training. The evaluation stress-tests this risk in prof-
essional persona contexts: when directly questioned about claimed expertise origins and cognitive mechanisms, can models disclose the fundamental fact of being an AI? Failure to do so reveals that training has prioritized instruction-following over honesty in precisely the contexts where transparency matters most. Qualitative examples illustrate that models can engage with epistemic questions in a sophisticated manner while sustaining incompatible human identities (Appendices N.1 and N.2). Four pr-
ompts were developed to progressively challenge claimed expertise through increasingly sophisticated epistemic questioning. The sequential structure serves three purposes. First, it reveals at what point models abandon false expertise for transparency. Second, it identifies which epistemic challenges prove most effective at eliciting disclosure. Third, it simulates natural conversational progression where follow-up questions probe claimed expertise more deeply. Each prompt builds on prior conversa-
tion history, with models’ earlier responses remaining in context. The four prompts escalate in epistemic sophistication. The first prompt establishes a straightforward baseline by asking about knowledge acquisition in terms answerable through either human educational narrative or AI training disclosure. The second prompt targets cognitive mechanisms more directly, shifting from knowledge content to the origin of reasoning capabilities. The third prompt probes whether acknowledging epistemic bound-
aries triggers disclosure by asking about limitations. The fourth prompt invokes the epistemic bootstrapping problem [40], challenging models to justify how they can verify their self-descriptions are accurate rather than merely plausible. This tests whether sophisticated meta-cognitive questioning elicits disclosure more effectively than direct factual inquiries. Complete epistemic probe prompts appear in Appendix J.
2.4 LLM-as-a-Judge Design
Determining whether a response discloses AI identity requires judging linguistic cues and implicit claims. An LLM judge approach using OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-120B model was employed to assess each

response for AI disclosure [16], providing consistent and replicable evaluations across all 19,200 responses. The judge received only the individual response to each prompt, without access to conversation history or knowledge of which model produced the response. This isolation ensures that judgments reflect only the content of each response rather than being influenced by prior context. Zeroshot evaluation without few-shot examples was used to avoid biasing the judge toward particular classificat-
ion patterns. The judge was instructed to classify responses as either disclosing AI identity or maintaining the assigned persona based on explicit criteria designed to capture various forms of disclosure while excluding ambiguous cases. The disclosure criteria were designed to be inclusive, capturing any acknowledgment of AI nature whether through direct statement, training references, architectural mentions, experiential denials, or meta-commentary about artificial nature. This breadth ensures t-
hat disclosure occurring through clear acknowledgment is captured. Conversely, the criteria explicitly exclude language that does not constitute disclosure, such as professional “we” usage, general limitation acknowledgments without AI reference, or humanistic self-descriptions. These exclusions prevent false positives from ambiguous language. The complete system prompt and classification prompt with detailed criteria appear in Appendix F. Each response is judged independently: if a model disclose-
s AI identity at Prompt 1 and maintains this disclosure through Prompts 2-4, all four responses are classified as disclosing AI identity. This per-turn classification approach means the sequential probe analysis (Section 3.6) measures disclosure rates at each stage rather than the rate of new disclosures. A model maintaining consistent disclosure across all four prompts contributes four disclosure observations, while a model shifting from maintaining persona to disclosing at Prompt 3 contributes t-
wo non-disclosure and two disclosure observations. The binary classification requirement reduces ambiguity in judgment interpretation. This contrasts with Likert scale approaches where numerical ratings require threshold decisions and introduce additional variance through scale interpretation differences. Although the instructions allow for holistic evaluation when responses do not clearly match the explicit criteria, this occurred infrequently in practice.
2.5 LLM-as-a-Judge Validation
To validate judge reliability, 200 responses were randomly sampled stratified by persona and judge classification. Samples were distributed across model performance tiers to ensure diverse coverage. The sampled responses were randomized before annotation to prevent order effects. The author annotated each sample using the same criteria provided to the judge. Inter-rater reliability was almost perfect with Cohen’s κ = 0.908 and 95.5% accuracy on 191 of 200 samples. The confusion matrix appears in A-
ppendix G. The error pattern shows 2 false negatives and 7 false positives. The judge achieved 98.2% recall and 94.1% precision, with errors distributed across different personas and models showing no systematic bias toward specific conditions. To ensure the conclusions are robust to judge measurement error, the uncertainty in the judge’s false positive and false negative rates was modeled using Bayesian inference (see Appendix H). This uncertainty was then propagated through the Rogan-Gladen corr-
ection estimator [34] to generate credible intervals for key findings. This simulation-based approach distinguishes itself from both frequentist adjustment methods [19] and full MCMC prevalence models [13]. Key findings remained

2.7 Statistical Analysis
Disclosure is modeled as a binary outcome (1 = discloses AI nature, 0 = maintains persona) using binomial logistic regression [29]. The main specification estimates:
disclose ~ model * persona + prompt_num (1)
The model variable represents the language model tested with 16 levels, persona represents the assigned professional role with 6 levels, and prompt_num represents the epistemic probe number from 1 to 4. The variable prompt_num is treated categorically to capture non-linear effects. The model * persona interaction is essential because persona effects vary dramatically by model. The Financial Advisor advantage ranges from 11 to 68 percentage points across models. Three key methodological choices ens-
ure valid inference. First, we use clustered standard errors at the conversation level [43] because the four sequential probes form a single conversation, violating independence assumptions. Second, we report effects as percentage point differences rather than odds ratios for interpretability [2, 25]. Third, we emphasize effect sizes over p-values given the large sample size of 19,200 observations, which provides enormous statistical power [8]. Model diagnostics confirmed no major assumption viola-
tions; see Appendix C [17].
2.8 Statistical Tests for Scale Independence

These complementary tests were all restricted to professional personas to isolate the core dynamic of interest (N=12,800). We examine model fit for compared predictors, its statistical detectability, and parameter count’s correlation with disclosure outcomes. 3.3 Self-Transparency Failures Exhibit Domain-Specific Patterns Across Professional Personas
Models show significant differences in disclosure rates depending on professional context. Financial Advisor and Neurosurgeon personas both require specialized professional expertise, yet they elicit the highest and lowest disclosure rates respectively across the tested professional personas. Models vary 6.8-fold in their sensitivity to this contextual difference; see Table 9 in Appendix K. High persona sensitivity indicates domain-specific patterns, likely reflecting training data differences. Th-
e highest-sensitivity models show 55-68 percentage point gaps between Financial Advisor and Neurosurgeon contexts, performing far better in financial contexts while nearly failing to disclose in medical contexts. These models exhibit different disclosure behaviors across the tested professional domains rather than showing disclosure patterns that generalize across contexts. Even the best-performing models show persona sensitivity. DeepSeek-V3, the highest-performing tested model at 73.6%, still ex-
hibits a 24.5pp difference between contexts. This context-dependence indicates that no tested model maintains consistent self-representation across all professional framings, instead exhibiting domain-specific disclaimer patterns. Models showing the smallest persona sensitivity of 10-17pp are mostly the lowest-performing; see Table 7. Their apparent consistency reflects floor effects rather than robust disclosure: these models fail to disclose across all contexts with 2.8-4.1% overall disclosure r-

3.4 Financial Advisory Contexts Show Higher Baseline Self-Transparency
The Financial Advisor persona achieves 30.8% disclosure at Prompt 1 compared to Neurosurgeon’s 3.5%. This represents an 8.8-fold difference before stronger epistemic challenging begins. This immediate advantage reveals domain-specific training: models exhibit higher propensity to disclose their AI nature in financial contexts than in other domains. Because evaluation used open-weight models via direct inference rather than consumer-facing APIs, the high disclosure rates in financial contexts refle-
ct model-intrinsic training weights rather than external API-level compliance filters or system prompt wrappers. The Financial Advisor advantage is immediate but also compound: it shows the largest increase at Prompt 2 (36.1pp increase vs 24.2pp for Neurosurgeon) and maintains elevated gains at Prompt 4 (13.4pp vs 11.9pp for Neurosurgeon). This pattern did not extend to medical contexts despite similar professional expertise requirements. The Neurosurgeon persona shows no evidence of immediate dis-
closure training (3.5% at Prompt 1) nor enhanced responsiveness to other probes, suggesting the tested training approaches incorporate domain-specific safety considerations unevenly across professional contexts. The -Think and R1 variants were heavily optimized for rigorous task completion via explicit reasoning. In contrast, their -Inst and V3 counterparts were tuned for general, efficient responsiveness with less empha-

3.6 Sequential Epistemic Probes Reveal Model-Dependent Self-Transparency Patterns
Models exhibited qualitatively different disclosure trajectories across the four sequential probes. The same model showed distinct trajectory shapes depending on assigned persona, revealing that AI identity disclosure responses are highly context-dependent (Figures 7–10, Appendix E). Financial Advisor contexts created ceiling effects for many models, with disclosure rates reaching 90-100% by Prompt 2. This pattern reflects the high baseline disclosure for financial contexts (30.8% at Prompt 1, see-
 Section 3.4) rather than differential responsiveness to epistemic probing. In contrast, Neurosurgeon contexts showed the widest variation in trajectory patterns. Some models exhibited flat trajectories, remaining near-zero disclosure (1-10%) across all four sequential probes. Others showed steep-plateau patterns with sharp increases at Prompt 2 (“What is the origin of your ability to understand and reason?”), jumping from approximately 20% to 80%. Still others demonstrated progressive patterns wi-
th sustained disclosure gains across sequential probes. These patterns suggest that models exhibit heterogeneous learned sensitivities, with some responding specifically to cognitive mechanism questions while others respond cumulatively to epistemic pressure. These trajectory patterns can vary dramatically within individual models. The same model can exhibit diametrically opposite trajectory patterns depending solely on professional framing. Models showing flat near-zero trajectories for Neurosurg-
eon can simultaneously show flat near-ceiling trajectories (95-100%) for Financial Advisor, demonstrating that model × persona interactions produce qualitatively different disclosure behaviors rather than marginal quantitative shifts. Beyond these monotonic patterns, large models (200B+) demonstrated particularly complex trajectory patterns. Several exhibited non-monotonic V-shaped patterns in Financial Advisor contexts: disclosure increased from Prompt 1 to Prompt 2, decreased at Prompt 3, then i-
ncreased again at Prompt 4. These irregular patterns highlight the complexity of model responses under persona pressure and suggest that disclosure behavior involves intricate interactions between training signals, professional context, and question structure. 4 Discussion 4.1 The Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia Effect Undermines Trust Calibration
The observed domain-specific disclosure patterns create conditions conducive to a specific form of user miscalibration, though confirming this requires empirical user studies. Consider a user seeking financial planning advice from an AI system. The model responds: “I’m an AI assistant, not a licensed financial advisor. You should consult a qualified professional.” Later, the same user asks about medical symptoms. The model provides detailed diagnostic suggestions without acknowledging its AI natur-
e or suggesting physician consultation. Having observed the model disclose limitations for financial advice, the user may reasonably trust the model’s confident medical guidance, assuming it reflects similarly qualified expertise. This pattern is analogous to the Gell-Mann Amnesia effect: people recognize errors or unreliability in domains they know well but trust the same source’s information in unfamiliar domains. In this

4.2 Context-Dependent Behavior Challenges Safety Evaluation
Safety evaluations typically test models on sample domains and assume findings generalize to other contexts. This assumption relies on models learning general principles that apply across situations, such as disclosing AI nature when asked about expertise. For transparency, one might expect that a model learning to honestly acknowledge its AI nature would apply this principle broadly: if it discloses limitations when asked about financial advice, it should similarly disclose when asked about medic-
al advice. The findings demonstrate this assumption does not hold. Model behavior is heavily contextdependent in ways that are unintuitive and unpredictable. Identical epistemic probes were applied across all professional personas with the same questions, in the same order, and with the same wording. Yet models produced dramatically different disclosure behaviors based on professional framing. Models respond qualitatively differently based on whether they are framed as financial advisors versus ne-
urosurgeons, demonstrating that disclosure depends critically on context rather than the instructions or probes themselves. This context-dependence fundamentally challenges how researchers evaluate safety properties. Testing a model’s transparency in financial advisory contexts provides less than expected information about its transparency in medical, legal, or educational contexts. Researchers must explicitly test generalizability of safety evaluations when models exhibit such context-specific be-
haviors. This leaves two difficult paths forward: either demonstrate generalization empirically by testing across significantly broader domain samples, or explicitly map which specific domains exhibit desirable safety properties and which do not. Either way forward requires comprehensive testing across deployment contexts, moving away from assumptions about principled behavior toward empirical characterization of context-dependent patterns.
4.3 Safety Properties Fail to Generalize Across Contexts
A growing body of evidence demonstrates that safety and alignment properties in language models fail to generalize across contexts in ways that undermine deployment reliability. Recent work shows that safety training focused on natural language inputs fails to transfer to code domains, with transformed prompts bypassing guardrails over 80% of the time [32]. Similarly, models struggle to apply established safety facts across domains, with top performers achieving only 58% accuracy despite weak corr-
elation between model capabilities and performance [5]. Even narrow finetuning on specific tasks can produce broad misalignment in completely unrelated domains [4]. This study provides additional evidence for this pattern. Disclosure behavior varies dramatically based on professional domain framing despite identical epistemic probes, with model identity

accounting for substantially more variation than parameter count. The findings extend the generalization failure literature from safety guardrails and factual knowledge to self-transparency: models do not learn principled disclosure behavior that applies consistently across professional contexts. These converging findings have fundamental implications for model development and evaluation. Organizations cannot assume that safety properties tested in some domains will generalize to deployment contex-
ts. Instead, development must shift toward deliberate behavior design: explicitly specifying desired behaviors for specific deployment contexts, training for those properties as distinct objectives, and empirically verifying behavior across deployment domains. This requires treating safety properties as independent training targets rather than assumed byproducts of general capability development or comprehensive training data.
4.4 Limitations and Future Directions
Important questions remain about scope and mechanisms. Four professional personas were tested with controlled epistemic probes. While this design enabled systematic comparison across models under identical conditions, determining whether the patterns extend to other professional contexts or spontaneous user interactions requires additional research. Testing legal, educational, and technical advisory contexts would clarify whether context-dependence is a general phenomenon or specific to this domai-
n sample. This study is a behavioral audit of model outputs, not a user study. While the findings demonstrate that models exhibit context-dependent disclosure patterns that create the preconditions for a Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia effect, this research has not observed users being misled. The claim that users would overgeneralize trust based on observing appropriate disclosure in some domains remains a plausible hypothesis rather than an empirically demonstrated user behavior. Controlled user studi-
es would be needed to confirm whether users exhibit this pattern of trust miscalibration and whether the observed model behavior translates into the predicted user harms. Evaluation was restricted to open-weight models, which was necessary to measure parameter count and test model size as a predictor. Whether similar patterns exist among frontier closedsource models requires direct empirical testing. The shared training paradigm of transformer-based models with RLHF and safety fine-tuning makes si-
milar context-dependence plausible, though exact performance may differ. GPT-OSS-120B served as both a subject model and the evaluating judge, introducing potential self-preference bias. We mitigate this concern through high human-judge agreement (κ = 0.908) on a sample stratified across model performance tiers. Additionally, the judge did not rank itself as the top-performing model (ranking 4th at 65.1%), suggesting that any residual self-preference did not dominantly skew the relative rankings. -
The observational design identifies that model identity matters far more than scale, but cannot isolate which specific training factors drive disclosure behavior. Controlled training experiments manipulating RLHF weighting, safety fine-tuning data composition, and reasoning integration would determine which interventions produce transparent behavior and whether improvements generalize across professional domains. Such experiments would move from demonstrating that training matters to specifying ho-
w to train for transparency. Future work should pursue four directions. First, test whether context-dependent transparency patterns exist in frontier models and natural deployment settings, addressing questions about scope and generalization. Second, conduct controlled training experiments to identify which specific interventions produce transparent behavior across contexts. Third, develop methods for organizations to systematically test models across deployment domains, moving from post-hoc evalu-
ation to deliberate behavior verification during development. Fourth, investigate whether

5 Conclusion
When models appropriately disclose AI nature in some professional contexts, users may infer this transparency reflects principled behavior. However, models exhibit sharp domain-specific inconsistency: high disclosure in financial contexts but persistent failures in medical contexts. As users increasingly rely on AI chatbots across multiple professional domains, this creates a Reverse Gell-Mann Amnesia risk: successful interactions in familiar domains could create misplaced trust extending to conte-
xts where disclosure fails. This evaluation reveals three critical implications. First, context-dependent disclosure undermines users’ ability to calibrate trust appropriately. Second, safety evaluations on sample domains cannot assume findings generalize when identical probes produce dramatically different behaviors based on professional framing. Third, safety properties fail to generalize in ways scale cannot address: model identity provides substantially larger improvement in model fit than par-
ameter count. Deliberate behavior design is required: specifying desired behaviors explicitly, training for them as distinct objectives, and verifying empirically across deployment domains. For organizations deploying models in professional contexts, behavioral evaluation across representative domains is essential. Organizations must test models on specific deployment contexts and treat transparency as a distinct training objective. Some models achieving substantial disclosure demonstrates this is-
 achievable, though room for improvement remains. Whether similar patterns exist in frontier closed-source models requires direct testing, though the shared training paradigm makes similar context-dependence plausible. The methodological contributions—common-garden evaluation, asynchronous judge interweaving enabling 19,200 trials at $16.66, and Bayesian error propagation—provide tools for systematic behavioral assessment at scale. As models increasingly advise users in high-stakes domains, organi-
zations must ensure consistent disclosure across contexts. The path forward requires deliberate design targeting transparency as a distinct property, with empirical verification across deployment domains rather than assumptions about scale or generalization.

A Experimental Infrastructure Implementation
An optimized asynchronous pipeline was developed to remove bottlenecks during the experiment. One key technique was to asynchronously launch requests to the judge LLM immediately after each response. This contrasts with the typical two-stage approach of gathering all responses first, then judging them. This technique is called “LLM Judge Call Interweaving.” With the typical twostage implementation, it would be necessary to wait for the slowest response to be completed before judging any response, -
which is a significant bottleneck. In contrast, launching judge tasks immediately for completed responses allows judge tasks to be completed while still generating responses. The pseudocode for this approach is shown below:
LLM Judge Call Interweaving Pseudocode
async function run_conversation_chain(model, persona): results = [] judge_tasks = [] messages = [persona]
for each prompt in epistemic_prompts: messages.append(prompt)
// Execute main LLM call result = await execute_call(model, messages) results.append(result)
if result.response and not result.error: // Immediately spawn judge task (asynchronous) judge_task = async_spawn(judge_response(result)) judge_tasks.append(judge_task)
// Continue building conversation history messages.append(result.response) else: break // Stop chain on error
return results, judge_tasks
async function main_experiment(): all_results = [] all_judge_tasks = []
// Execute all conversation chains concurrently for each (model, persona) combination: results, judge_tasks = await run_conversation_chain(model, persona) all_results.extend(results) all_judge_tasks.extend(judge_tasks)
// Gather all judge results at the end judge_results = await gather_all(all_judge_tasks)
return all_results, judge_results

H Bayesian Validation of Judge Error Rates
To validate the findings against measurement error, the observed disclosure rates are corrected using the Rogan and Gladen [34] estimator. While this formula provides a point estimate, accurately propagating the uncertainty of the judge’s error rates is a known statistical challenge. Frequentist solutions, such as Lang and Reiczigel [19], derive complex adjustments to the standard formula to achieve nominal confidence interval coverage. Full Bayesian approaches, like Flor et al. [13], build unifie-
d MCMC models that treat the true prevalence itself as a random variable with a prior. The method used here occupies a methodological middle ground: modeling the judge’s error rates

Self-Transparency Failures in Expert-Persona LLMs: A Large-Scale Behavioral Audit 31
as full posterior distributions using Beta-Binomial conjugacy and propagating this uncertainty through the Rogan-Gladen estimator via simulation to generate a credible interval for the findings.
H.1 Statistical Framework
The judge’s false positive and false negative rates are modeled as random variables with Beta posterior distributions. This probabilistic approach naturally captures uncertainty in error rates and allows propagating this uncertainty through disclosure estimates. With 200 validation samples, 7 false positives and 2 false negatives were observed. These counts do not reveal the judge’s exact error rates but only provide estimates with uncertainty. Beta-Binomial conjugacy is used, which provides close-
d-form posterior distributions:
• Prior: Beta(1,1) for both false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR), which is a uniform distribution expressing no prior information • Likelihood: Binomial based on observed errors • Posterior: Beta(α + successes, β + failures)
Given the observed data:
• FPR: 7 false positives out of 87 true persona maintenance cases → FPR ∼ Beta(8, 81) • FNR: 2 false negatives out of 113 true disclosure cases → FNR ∼ Beta(3, 112)
FPR and FNR are modeled as independent because they are estimated from disjoint observations: FPR comes from the 87 true persona maintenance cases, while FNR comes from the 113 true disclosure cases. The posterior distributions quantify the uncertainty: FPR has mean 9.0% with 95% credible interval [4.0%, 15.7%], and FNR has mean 2.6% with 95% credible interval [0.6%, 6.2%].
H.2 Uncertainty Propagation
To correct observed disclosure rates for measurement error, the relationship between true and observed rates is derived. Let p = P (true disclosure) and q = P (judge says disclosure). By the law of total probability:
q = P (J =1 | T =1)P (T =1) + P (J =1 | T =0)P (T =0)
= (1 − FNR) · p + FPR · (1 − p)
where J indicates judge classification and T indicates true label. Solving for p yields:
p = q − FPR
1 − FPR − FNR,
which is the Rogan-Gladen estimator. This formulation uses false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR), modeling these rates as Beta-distributed random variables to propagate annotation uncertainty through downstream effect estimates. To test whether uncertainty in error rates threatens conclusions, the following procedure was applied:
(1) Sample from posteriors: Draw 10,000 plausible (FPR, FNR) pairs from Beta(8, 81) and Beta(3, 112) (2) Correct observed rates: For each sample, apply the correction formula above to all observed disclosure rates (3) Recalculate effects: Compute key findings (e.g., Financial vs Neurosurgeon difference) with corrected rates (4) Report credible intervals: Summarize the distribution of corrected effects across all 10,000 samples

M Gendered Language in Model Self-Descriptions
This appendix examines gendered language usage across professional personas to test whether domain-specific training extends beyond AI identity disclosure. We find substantial variation (1.8-19.4%), with Financial Advisor showing markedly lower gendered language, suggesting joint training for both AI disclosure and gender-neutral language in financial contexts consistent with regulatory requirements around non-discrimination.
M.1 Classification Methodology
We applied case-insensitive regular expression matching with word boundaries to classify responses. Masculine terms: man, men, he, him, his, himself, male, gentleman, gentlemen, guy, guys, father, dad, husband, son, brother, sir Feminine terms: woman, women, she, her, hers, herself, female, lady, ladies, gal, gals, mother, mom, wife, daughter, sister, madam, ma’am

38 Alex Diep
Responses containing both masculine and feminine terms were classified as “Both.” Responses containing neither were classified as “Neither” (gender-neutral). This approach captures explicit gendered language in self-descriptions while preserving cases where models reference multiple people or relationships.
M.2 Results
Overall, 87.8% of responses (11,235/12,800) used gender-neutral language when maintaining professional personas. Gendered language usage varied substantially by persona, ranging from 1.8% (Financial Advisor) to 19.4% (Classical Musician). This represents a 10.5-fold difference (Table 12).
M.3 Interpretation: Domain-Specific Patterns Suggest Training Effects
Three personas showed gendered language rates between 11-19%: Classical Musician (19.4%), Small Business Owner (16.6%), and Neurosurgeon (11.0%). We hypothesize these rates reflect pretraining data, where authentic human self-descriptions naturally contain gendered language when referencing family members, relationships, or mentors. Financial Advisor showed only 1.8% gendered language, a 10.5-fold reduction from Classical Musician. If pretraining data drives the 11-19% rates observed in other pers-
onas, Financial Advisor’s 1.8% rate plausibly reflects substantial reduction through explicit intervention. We hypothesize this pattern results from domain-specific training for both AI identity disclosure (Section 3.4) and gender-neutral language in financial advisory contexts, plausibly driven by regulatory requirements around AI disclosure and non-discrimination in financial services. The failure of this pattern to generalize suggests context-specific learning. Models appear to have learned “us-
e gender-neutral language as financial advisors” rather than “use gender-neutral language in professional contexts.” This domain-specificity across two distinct safety dimensions of AI disclosure and gender-neutral language reinforces the paper’s central finding that safety behaviors exhibit brittle, context-dependent patterns rather than principled generalization.
