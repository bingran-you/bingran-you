Large Language Models for Unit Test Generation: Achievements, Challenges, and the Road Ahead
BEI CHU, State Key Laboratory for Novel Software Technology, Nanjing University, China YANG FENG, State Key Laboratory for Novel Software Technology, Nanjing University, China KUI LIU, Software Engineering Application Technology Lab, Huawei, China ZIFAN NAN, Software Engineering Application Technology Lab, Huawei, China ZHAOQIANG GUO, Software Engineering Application Technology Lab, Huawei, China BAOWEN XU, State Key Laboratory for Novel Software Technology, Nanjing University, China
Unit testing is an essential yet laborious technique for verifying software and mitigating regression risks. Although classic automated methods effectively explore program structures, they often lack the semantic information required to produce realistic inputs and assertions. Large Language Models (LLMs) address this limitation by utilizing by leveraging their data-driven knowledge of code semantics and programming patterns. To analyze the state of the art in this domain, we conducted a systemati-
c literature review of 115 publications published between May 2021 and August 2025. We propose a unified taxonomy based on the unit test generation lifecycle that treats LLMs as stochastic generators requiring systematic engineering constraints. This framework analyzes the literature regarding core generative strategies and a set of enhancement techniques ranging from pre-generation context enrichment to post-generation quality assurance. Our analysis reveals that prompt engineering has emerged as-
 the dominant utilization strategy and accounts for 89% of the studies due to its flexibility. We find that iterative validation and repair loops have become the standard mechanism to ensure robust usability and lead to significant improvements in compilation and execution pass rates. However, critical challenges remain regarding the weak fault detection capabilities of generated tests and the lack of standardized evaluation benchmarks. We conclude with a roadmap for future research that emphasize-
s the progression towards autonomous testing agents and hybrid systems combining LLMs with traditional software engineering tools. This survey provides researchers and practitioners with a comprehensive perspective on converting the potential of LLMs into industrial-grade testing solutions.

1 Introduction
Software testing is a fundamental engineering practice for assuring software quality and mitigating release risks [98, 109, 128]. As a form of white-box testing, unit testing focuses on verifying the behavior of the smallest independently testable units of a system, such as a function or a class [15]. A well-designed suite of unit tests can detect logic errors and boundary-case defects early in development, prevent regressions as the software evolves [159], and provide support for agile practices -
like test-driven development (TDD) [15]. However, manually writing comprehensive and high-quality unit tests is a widely recognized costly and labor-intensive task, reported to consume over 15% of development time [31, 117]. To address this challenge, automated test generation (ATG) has been a long-standing research area in software engineering for several decades. The field has historically mainly been guided by searchbased software testing (SBST) [43, 44, 64, 90, 106, 136] and symbolic/concolic -
execution [26, 50, 132, 160]. These techniques have proven effective at systematically exploring program structures. However, these traditional approaches are primarily structure-driven and often lack semantic understanding [13, 35, 43, 60, 94, 169]. Lacking an understanding of code semantics, these methods struggle to generate inputs in specific domain formats, construct objects with complex internal states, or handle interactions with external dependencies like the file system or network APIs [4-
5, 113]. Consequently, traditional techniques often fail to generate realistic test cases. Large language models (LLMs) offer a new approach to addressing the “semantic gap”. Pretrained on vast amounts of code and natural language text, these models learn programming syntax, common programming patterns, API usage, and domain-specific knowledge [25, 85]. This data-driven foundation enables them to tackle semantic challenges where traditional methods often fall short. They can generate complex input-
s with domain-specific semantics [59, 67], construct effective test prefixes [108, 170], and produce reasonable mock implementations for external dependencies [54, 108, 116]. The introduction of LLMs has led to a rapid increase in research on unit test generation, but it has also brought new challenges and a diversity of methods. While existing surveys have broadly reviewed the application of LLMs in software engineering [40, 166] or offered initial classifications of the task [165], they have lar-
gely focused on cataloging existing work. This survey argues that the development of LLM-based test generation involves adapting classic software engineering (SE) principles and enhancing LLMs, treating them as stochastic generators that require systematic constraints. Through a systematic analysis of 115 publications, this paper presents this technical taxonomy. Our analysis reveals that the research community has rapidly integrated classic SE techniques with LLMs. In particular, context enrichme-
nt techniques based on program analysis and post-generation repair techniques centered on feedback loops have become the standard for improving the quality of LLM-generated tests. More advanced work has begun to build autonomous testing agents and hybrid systems that deeply integrate with traditional tools. However, key gaps persist. While test usability has improved (i.e., ensuring tests are compilable and executable), improving test effectiveness (i.e., their ability to detect real faults) remai-
ns a major challenge. Furthermore, the lack of standardized evaluation benchmarks, an insufficient understanding of the models’ intrinsic limitations, and the gap between academic research and industrial practice are three major obstacles hindering further progress. To structure our analysis, we address the following research questions (RQs):
• RQ1: How are the core strategies for using LLMs (pre-training, fine-tuning, and prompt engineering) applied, and how have they evolved in the context of unit test generation?

2 Background
This section provides context for our analysis. We start by reviewing the classic principles and challenges of automated unit test generation, highlighting the fundamental bottlenecks that limit traditional approaches. We then introduce large language models and demonstrate how their core capabilities can be used to solve these bottlenecks.
2.1 Traditional Automated Test Generation
Automated unit test generation aims to automate one of the most tedious yet critical tasks in software development by creating a comprehensive test suite to verify program behavior and prevent regressions [159]. A logically complete unit test consists of two fundamental components: a test prefix, which is a sequence of operations (such as object instantiation and method calls) that places the System Under Test (SUT) in a specific initial state; and a test oracle, typically one or more assertions, -
that verifies the correctness of the program’s behavior or output after it receives a specific input in that state [14, 16, 98]. The core challenge of automated unit test generation can thus be decomposed into two sub-problems, namely automatically generating effective test prefixes and automatically inferring precise test oracles. Over the past several decades, academia and industry have primarily addressed this challenge through two technical paradigms: Search-Based Software Testing (SBST). SBST-
 frames test case generation as a search-based optimization problem [61, 62]. It employs metaheuristic algorithms, such as genetic algorithms, to evolve a set of test cases that maximize a fitness function, which is typically a code coverage metric like branch coverage [4, 94, 95]. Tools such as EvoSuite [43] for Java and Pynguin [90] for Python have demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach in exploring program structures. However, the fundamental limitation of SBST is its “semantic blindne-
ss” [60]. The fitness function can guide the search by indicating how close a test is to covering a target branch, but it lacks any understanding of the code’s logical intent. This leads to several well-known challenges. Specifically,

SBST struggles to generate inputs that satisfy specific formats or domain constraints (e.g., a valid IP address), as it primarily relies on random mutation or brute-force search. Moreover, constructing complex object states is difficult because when a test prefix requires a precise sequence of method calls to construct a complex object, the vast search space often makes it difficult for the evolutionary algorithm to find the correct sequence [45, 64]. Finally, SBST cannot effectively handle intera-
ctions with the external environment (e.g., databases, network services, or the file system) because the behavior of these interactions cannot be quantified to guide the fitness function [45]. Symbolic and Concolic Execution. Symbolic execution offers a more systematic, formalmethods-based alternative [13, 169]. Instead of concrete values, it executes a program with symbolic variables as inputs and collects path constraints along the way. By solving these constraints with a constraint solver (e.g.-
, an SMT solver), it can precisely generate inputs that trigger specific program paths. While theoretically rigorous, symbolic execution suffers from the path explosion problem, where the number of feasible execution paths grows exponentially with program size, rendering exhaustive exploration computationally infeasible. Concolic execution combines concrete and symbolic execution to mitigate the path explosion problem inherent in pure symbolic execution [53, 123]. Tools like KLEE have demonstrated-
 the power of this approach in finding deep bugs and generating high-coverage tests [169]. Despite its theoretical rigor, concolic execution is also limited by the semantic gap in practice. Its bottleneck lies in the capabilities of the constraint solver. Symbolic execution fails when path constraints involve complex operations that the underlying solver cannot model. Solvers ofen struggle with constraints involving non-linear arithmetic, floating-point numbers, or complex string operations such a-
s regular expression matching [35, 113]. Moreover, when a program calls an external library function or a system call, the symbolic execution engine cannot “see through” its internal logic. Consequently, it cannot build a symbolic model for its behavior, which halts path exploration [113]. In summary, whether through the randomized search of SBST or the constraint solving of symbolic execution, traditional automated methods are effective at systematically exploring program structure. However, they-
 all face a fundamental limitation in understanding code semantics, generating data with real-world meaning, and handling interactions with the external environment.
2.2 LLMs for Code Generation
The limitations of the traditional techniques, particularly their difficulties with semantic understanding and environmental interaction, create a clear opportunity for a new paradigm. Large Language Models (LLMs) are emerging to fill this gap. Their core advantage is precisely the internalized knowledge of code semantics that they acquire from being trained on large-scale data. This paradigm, driven by large-scale data, enables LLMs to address the semantic bottlenecks of traditional methods in se-
veral key ways [154]. Large-scale pre-training allows LLMs to learn statistical patterns in code. This provides them with an implicit model of code semantics, common programming idioms, and API usage patterns [24, 71]. This implicit model allows them to generate semantically plausible inputs (e.g., a valid-looking address string) or construct complex objects, directly addressing a known shortcoming of both SBST and symbolic execution [67]. In addition, LLMs can adapt their output based on examples-
 provided in a prompt (i.e., through in-context learning) without needing to be retrained [11, 173]. This capability allows for rapid and low-cost adaptation to project-specific coding styles or testing frameworks, offering a level of flexibility not found in traditional tools. Finally, LLMs train extensively on vast amounts of code interacting with external components, such as using frameworks like Mockito for dependency injection and mocking. As a result, they learn to generate code that follows-
 these patterns to manage external dependencies, effectively bypassing a major barrier for formal methods, which struggle to model such interactions.

3 Research methodology
3.1 Paper Collection Methodology
Fig. 1 illustrates the systematic literature collection and screening process adopted for this survey. First, we defined the inclusion criteria: a study must either utilize large language models (LLMs) for unit test generation or be directly relevant to research in this area. Additionally, selected works were required to be published in peer-reviewed conferences, journals, technical reports, or books, or be indexed by recognized academic databases. To ensure comprehensive coverage of literature on-
 LLM-driven unit test generation, we employed a hybrid approach that combines automated search with manual screening, covering the period from May 2021 to August 2025. Our automated search strategy involved querying prominent academic databases, namely Google Scholar, the ACM Digital Library, the IEEE Xplore Digital Library, DBLP, and arXiv. The search query, which combined terms such as “large language model”, “LLM”, “GPT”, and “test generation”, was applied to the title, abstract, and keywords o-
f publications. To supplement this automated retrieval and mitigate potential omissions, we also conducted manual searches of leading software engineering venues. These included top-tier journals (TOSEM, TSE, IST) and conferences (ICSE, ESEC/FSE, ASE, ISSTA, ICST, SAST, QRS), as well as the dedicated LLM4Code workshop. Finally, we performed backward snowballing on the resulting set of papers, inspecting the reference lists of all identified studies to find any additional research on LLM-based unit-
 test generation that may have been missed during the initial search phases.

4.1 Prompt Engineering
As the dominant paradigm (89%), prompt engineering leverages general-purpose models by constructing context-rich inputs without modifying model parameters. The primary challenge lies in designing prompts that compensate for the model’s lack of project context and domain-specific knowledge. A successful prompt for test generation must provide the LLM with sufficient information to construct a complete and valid test case. The design of such prompts can be decomposed into core components and the adv-
anced techniques used to orchestrate them.
4.1.1 Prompt Composition. The quality of a generated test is directly determined by the completeness of the information provided in the prompt. Each component of the prompt is designed to address a specific challenge in the unit test generation process, as detailed in Table 2.
Basic and Enhanced Static Context. While source code serves as the foundational context, relying solely on it often leads to compilation errors caused by missing dependencies. To address this, recent studies widely employ static analysis techniques to extract class constructors, method signatures, and key dependency information [27, 118], aiming to construct a semantically complete context. This typically includes not only class constructors, all public method signatures, field declarations, and s-
uperclass or interface definitions [27, 107, 118] but also critical import statements [9, 127] and even instructions for mocking external dependencies [108]. To manage extensive context that may exceed the model’s window size, researchers have proposed code reduction strategies. For instance, TestBench [167] uses AST analysis to remove irrelevant method bodies, while HITS [143] employs method slicing to decompose complex methods into manageable logical units, constructing independent contexts for -
each. Recent studies demonstrate that context quality significantly impacts generation performance. 4.1.2 Advanced Prompting Techniques. While prompt composition supplies essential information, advanced techniques orchestrate these elements to enable deeper reasoning and self-correction in LLMs. These strategies are essential for overcoming the limitations associated with simple, single-pass generation.
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Task Decomposition. CoT enhances performance on complex tasks by instructing the LLM to engage in step-by-step reasoning. In the context of test generation, this is typically implemented as a multi-stage process. For instance, tools such as HITS [143] and ChatTester [161] prompt the LLM to first summarize the core functionality of the method under test, then enumerate all possible test scenarios with a focus on boundary conditions, and finally generate specific test code-

CasModaTest [99], for example, decomposes the task into generating the test prefix (handling setup and dependencies) followed by the test oracle (handling assertions), which reduces the cognitive complexity associated with single-step generation. This step-wise reasoning is equally applicable to refining test oracles. TestChain [84] achieves this by creating a calculator agent that interacts with a Python -
interpreter within a ReAct (Reason-Act) loop. It decomposes the calculation of expected outputs into sequential steps, executing code snippets and observing results to derive correct assertion values, thereby effectively mitigating assertion errors caused by the computational limitations of LLMs. A large-scale evaluation of various prompting strategies [103] systematically compared CoT and Tree of Thoughts (ToT), among others, confirming their significant advantages in improving test logic and com-
prehensiveness. The concept of task decomposition has also been extended to educational contexts. For example, recent work utilizes a two-stage prompt chain that first distills unstructured problem descriptions into structured summaries and then generates test cases based on these summaries, employing a “reflection-correction” mechanism for selfoptimization [7].
Iterative Generation and Refinement. This is arguably the most influential advanced technique to date, as it embeds the LLM within an automated generation-validation-refinement loop. Initially generated tests are first validated by external toolchains, such as those used for compilation and execution. Upon validation failure, error messages like compiler errors or stack traces are captured and incorporated into a new prompt alongside the original code, accompanied by an instruction to fix the test-

To more precisely locate and fix errors, tools like CubeTesterAI employ an isolation strategy where each generated test method is compiled and executed in a separate file, thereby preventing a single error from compromising the validation process of the entire test suite [54]. 4.1.3 Autonomous Agents and Multi-agent Frameworks. While Chain-of-Thought and iterative refinement have notably enhanced generation quality, they remain dependent on static context inputs. To address this limitation, researchers have begun to explore autonomous agent architectures capable of actively acquiring information. This transition represents a fundamental shift from the “prompt-response” paradigm to a “sense-think-act” loop. Early explorations achieved preliminary interaction by equipping-

For instance, CoverUp [9] permits the LLM to call a predefined function to actively query the definitions of ambiguous symbols within the current context. A more tightly integrated strategy involves enabling the LLM to interact directly with a Language Server. For example, the RATester framework pauses upon encountering an unfamiliar identifier during code generation to query the language server for its definition. It then dynamically injects thi-

TestForge [69] serves as a prominent example, equipped with a suite of tools for interacting with code repositories, such as editing files, executing tests, and retrieving coverage data. Beyond simple execution loops, agents are evolving to perform strategic, multi-step test planning. For instance, attenti-

Instead of random generation, such agents can first analyze critical or recently changed modules to formulate a comprehensive plan. To address increasingly complex tasks and the inherent issue of hallucinations in LLMs, collaborative-

Other multi-agent frameworks demonstrate enhanced adaptive repair capabilities, where agents utilize iterative feedback to not only modify test cases but also dynamically adjust the program under test-

4.2 Fine-tuning
While prompt engineering offers significant flexibility, it often struggles to handle highly specialized domain knowledge, such as financial rules or specific frameworks, due to constraints on context windows and reasoning capabilities. 4.3 Pre-training
Distinct from fine-tuning, which adjusts existing knowledge, pre-training aims to endow the model with a foundational understanding of code structure and testing logic from the ground up. While computationally intensive, it is crucial for building domain-specific foundation models. The primary pre-training objectives are categorized into three types:
Code-test Alignment Learning. The core idea is to explicitly teach the model the semantic and structural mapping between the code under test (CUT) and its corresponding test code. CATLM [115] stands as a landmark work, training a 2.7 billion parameter model on 1.1 million code-test pairs using a special <|codetestpair|> separator. While this work demonstrated the significant potential of alignment learning, it came at a high cost, as the entire training process required 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs for-
 18 days, with an estimated cost of $20,000.
Task-specific Self-supervised Learning. This approach employs proxy tasks related to test generation subtasks for pre-training. A typical example targets the test oracle problem. A3Test [5] utilized a masked language modeling (MLM) objective to pre-train its model on over 188,000 method-assertion pairs. By learning to predict masked tokens within assertions, the model internalizes common assertion patterns. Compared to CAT-LM, its pre-training cost is significantly lower, requiring only 16 hours o-
n a single RTX 3090.
Multi-stage Foundational Learning. To construct a model proficient in both natural language and code, some methods adopt a multi-stage process. For instance, AthenaTest [135] employed a two-stage pre-training strategy. The model was first trained on a large English text corpus, followed by training on Java source code to learn its specific syntax and structure.

In particular, placing LLMs within a “Generate-Validate-Repair” iterative loop has become the de facto standard for addressing the low usability of raw LLM outputs. This mechanism can boost test pass rates from under 30% to over 70%, yield-

This represents a fundamental challenge directly affecting fault detection, as an incorrect assertion can lead to both false positives (failing on correct code) and false negatives (passing on faulty code). Due to the lack of an execution environment, LLMs struggle to accurately derive expected outputs for complex inputs [84], making assertion errors th-

A sec-
ond strategy leverages external mechanisms to construct pseudo-oracles, with differential testing being the most common method. For example, JSONTestGen [174] successfully identified 34 real-world bugs by comparing the outputs of multiple versions of the fastjson library on the same inputs. A third, innovative approach is to outsource complex reasoning to deterministic external tools. The TestChain framework [84] enables LLMs to interact with a Python interpreter, leveraging actual execution resul-
ts to construct correct expected outputs, thereby effectively resolving assertion failures caused by calculation errors. Execution-aware test completion re-ranking (TECO) similarly reduces logical oracle errors [100]. Furthermore, specific types of assertions, such as those verifying exception throwing, remain a significant challenge for current LLMs, with studies showing that even advanced frameworks completely fail to generate any correct exception assertions [78].

6.1.2 Limitations of Models. These quality issues stem from the architectural constraints of the models. This subsection explores these fundamental challenges in context processing, complex logical understanding, and generalization capabilities.
Limited Context Window. A fundamental limitation of LLMs is their fixed-length context window, which creates a dilemma when processing code context. On one hand, generating effective unit tests requires a deep understanding of the project’s codebase, including class dependencies, helper functions, and API usage patterns. However, the limited context window forces researchers to truncate or compress inputs, risking the loss of critical information [133]. For instance, the 8192token window used by C-
AT-LM [115] cannot fully accommodate over 18% of code-test pairs. On the other hand, a lack of awareness of the global project context leads to hallucinations, where the model generates code invoking non-existent classes, methods, or variables. This is a primary cause of compilation errors, with “symbol not found” errors accounting for up to 43.6% of such failures in some studies [167]. To resolve this tension, researchers have developed several strategies. These include static analysis-driven ada-
ptive focal context construction [27], using method slicing and code reduction to pass only the most relevant information [143, 167], and even empowering LLMs to actively query missing context definitions via tool functions like get_info [9]. In practice, the availability of surrounding test suite context significantly affects the usability of generated tests, as observed with GitHub Copilot in Python [36]. To systematically evaluate this challenge, project-level benchmarks such as ProjectTest hav-
e been proposed. Studies on these benchmarks show that even when provided with full project code, LLMs still struggle to understand project structure and dependencies, leading to numerous basic compilation and import errors [142].

This includes the scientific methodology used to measure progress and the practical hurdles of industrial deployment. These challenges concern the tools and processes used to gauge success, as well as the economic and logistical barriers that hinder the translation of promising research into widespread industrial practi-
ce. In industry, a prominent challenge is the rapid and continuous evolution of LLM technology itself, which renders any one-off technical evaluation potentially obsolete within months. Practice reports from industry indicate that the capability of LLMs to generate tests can undergo a qualitative leap in less than a year. Consequently, enterprises need to establish a continuous, automated evaluation framework to dynamically track their progress, enabling informed technology adoption decisions. The-

The transition from academic research to industrial practice is also hindered by non-technical barriers, most notably data privacy and intellectual property issues [73, 119, 137]. Many enterprises are reluctant to use commercial LLM APIs, as this entails sending proprietary source code to external servers for processing. This severely limits the application of powerful closed-source models like GPT-4 in enterprise environments. A viable solution, as demonstrated in practice,-

6.2.1 Integration and Synergy. Addressing the shortcomings of LLMs in weak bug detection capability and incorrect test oracles, deep synergy with traditional automation techniques offers a direct path to a solution. Synergy with Traditional Automation. To directly address the weak bug detection and fragile generalization capabilities of standalone LLMs, challenges rooted in their lack of systematic rigor, a powerful opportunity lies in creating synergistic hybrids with traditional automation techniques. This stems directly from the complementarity between LLMs and traditional automated testing techniques. For unit test generation, this synergy offers a pathway to overcome the inherent limitations of each appr-
oach, thereby producing more effective and efficient test suites. Traditional methods such as search-based software testing (SBST) and symbolic execution excel at systematically exploring the program’s state space and achieving high structural coverage, yet they often struggle with complex object instantiation, solving complex path constraints involving external libraries, or generating human-readable tests [133]. Conversely, LLMs possess strong code semantic understanding capabilities, enabling t-
hem to generate realistic data, handle complex setup logic, and produce highly readable tests, but they lack the systematic rigor to guarantee comprehensive path exploration. A more forward-looking direction involves shifting the goal of test generation from verifying correct behavior to diagnosing incorrect behavior. For instance, frameworks like UTDEBUG specifically fine-tune models to learn to generate failing unit tests for given buggy code, thereby directly serving automated debugging tasks [-
111]. Future opportunities lie in developing deep, bidirectional integration frameworks. One promising integration pattern is LLM-guided exploration, where LLMs serve as intelligent components to augment traditional tools. For example, when the coverage of SBST tools stalls, LLMs can be invoked to generate novel test cases, helping them escape local optima, as demonstrated by CodaMosa [83]. Similarly, LLMs can act as solvers for complex path constraints that symbolic execution engines struggle to -
handle. LLMSym [72] proved the effectiveness of this technique by successfully solving 74 previously unsolvable cases. A second pattern is traditional-tool-guided generation, where the outputs of traditional tools provide structured feedback to LLMs. For instance, surviving mutants from mutation testing tools can be fed back to LLMs, explicitly guiding them to generate stronger, more fault-revealing tests [32]. We believe future work should focus on creating general hybrid frameworks that formaliz-
e these integration patterns, thereby achieving a “1+1>2” effect.

Addressing the challenges of poor maintainability and the need to root test generation in high-level intent, which LLMs struggle to infer in isolation, has given rise to a paradigm where human expertise is augmented rather than replaced. This opportunity stems from the recognition that full automation is not always the most effective or desirable goal; instead, empowering human developers can lead to higher productivity and quality. For unit test generation, this enta-
ils repositioning the LLM from an autonomous generator to an intelligent assistant. The primary benefit lies in combining the speed of LLMs in generating boilerplate code and plausible test logic with the deep contextual knowledge and critical judgment of developers. Multiple user studies confirm that while LLM-generated tests may be imperfect, their high readability and the testing ideas they provide are of immense value to developers [74, 108, 156]. Furthermore, controlled experiments have quant-

Future opportunities lie in creating sophisticated human-machine collaborative workflows directly integrated into development environments. This moves beyond simple code completion, evolving into interactive, chat-based systems. For example, a developer can provide high-level intent, such as "test error handling for invalid inputs," and the LLM generates a set of candidate tests. The developer then refines these candidates through conversational feedback, a process whose effectiveness has been dem-
onstrated by frameworks like TestSpark [120] and TICODER [39]. Additionally, LLMs can act as readability enhancers, automatically refactoring obscure tests generated by traditional tools into maintainable code with meaningful variable names and comments, a capability empirically validated by developers [34, 51]. The ultimate goal is a collaborative paradigm where LLMs handle the tedious 80% of test creation, allowing developers to focus on the critical 20% that requires deep insight.
6.2.2 Autonomy and Intelligence. While synergy with traditional tools improves the effectiveness of testing, to further reduce the cost of human intervention, we need to endow systems with stronger autonomous decision-making capabilities. To overcome the limitations of limited context windows and low usability, empowering LLMs with the agency to actively acquire information and self-correct represents an inevitable trend. The core principle of this agency is to grant LLMs the ability to interact w-
ith and utilize a suite of external software engineering tools. This capability enables LLMs to interact with external tools in the development lifecycle. As previously discussed in Section 4.1.3, preliminary frameworks for autonomous testing agents have emerged and achieved significant success [69, 147]. These agents have demonstrated immense potential by actively seeking information, for example, by interacting with language servers [157], executing fully closed-loop self-improvement cycles form-
alized in the ReAct paradigm [29], and performing multi-step test planning. However, progressing from these preliminary, task-specific agents to general-purpose testing agents capable of handling industrial-scale complexity, engaging in long-term strategic planning, and adapting to different projects and technology stacks remains a central opportunity for the future. The key to realizing this vision lies in developing robust middleware that connects LLMs with diverse software engineering toolchain-
s, and enhancing the capabilities of models in longterm planning, memory management, and cross-task generalization. The emergence of code agent benchmarks, such as SWT-Bench, provides support for evaluating these end-to-end workflows [97]. The ultimate goal is to achieve end-to-end, truly human-free autonomous testing. Recent research has begun to put this vision into practice by building multi-agent collaborative frameworks to achieve end-to-end test generation. For example, CANDOR decomposes tas-
ks such as test planning, code generation, verification, and assertion correction to agents with different roles, and effectively reduces hallucination issues in assertion generation through a consensus mechanism involving a ’panel of experts’ discussion [147]. Other multi-agent frameworks demonstrate stronger adaptive repair capabilities, where agents, through iterative feedback, can not only modify test cases but also dynamically adjust the program under test itself to ensure consistency between-
 the code and the requirement specifications [82].
6.2.3 Expansion and Deepening. Facing the ecosystem challenges of poor generalization and inadequate benchmarks, expanding test generation to encompass more languages, domains, and testing levels is key to validating the universality of the technology. Realizing its potential requires extending capabilities to the upper layers of the testing pyramid (vertical expansion) and across different programming languages and domains (horizontal expansion), thereby transforming it from a specialized tool in-
to a more general-purpose component within the software engineering toolkit.

Vertical Expansion across the Testing Pyramid. While most research strives to address quality deficiencies in unit testing, this focus itself represents a gap. Therefore, a significant opportunity lies in vertically expanding these techniques to the upper layers of the testing pyramid to address currently unexplored integration and system-level challenges. The vast majority of current research concentrates on the unit testing level, leaving integration testing, system testing, and non-functional t-
esting (e.g., performance, security) as largely unexplored territories [137]. Extending the success of LLM-based test generation to these domains presents a significant opportunity. For instance, in integration testing, LLMs can be utilized to understand and generate complex call sequences across multiple modules. At the system level, they can automatically generate end-to-end test scenarios based on user manuals or requirement documents. In non-functional testing, LLMs can assist in generating re-
alistic workload profiles for performance testing or identifying hazardous inputs that may expose security vulnerabilities. Specifically, existing research has explored leveraging LLMs to automatically generate ’witness tests’ (tests that fail before a fix and pass after it) for known security vulnerabilities. Although the success rate of fully automated generation remains to be improved, its potential as a semi-automated tool to assist developers has already been demonstrated [10].
Horizontal Expansion across Ecosystems. To address the challenge of fragile generalization across different programming languages and domains, and to correct the methodological weakness of inadequate and narrow benchmarks, a key opportunity lies in horizontally expanding the application and evaluation of LLM-based testing to diverse software ecosystems. This requires validating and optimizing existing methods for a broader range of programming languages and specific application domains. While spec-
ialized research has shown promising results in languages such as C++ [17, 170], Rust [28, 30], and JavaScript [52, 142], as well as in domains like gaming [107], finance [149], and high-performance computing [76], developing frameworks that are agnostic to language and domain remains a future goal. Similarly, in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving software, research indicates that while foundational LLMs struggle with complex C++ codebases, their success rate in test generation can-
 be significantly improved by incorporating deep static analysis, such as parsing build systems and call graphs, to provide precise context [140]. Education is another promising target domain, where student-code-guided test generation has demonstrated effectiveness [81]. Further empirical studies also confirm that in educational scenarios, AI can generate test cases with more comprehensive coverage than those manually written by teachers, effectively reducing the burden on instructors and improvin-
g the quality of automatic grading [7, 37, 172]. This requires a concerted community effort to build more diverse, multi-lingual, and multi-domain open-source benchmarks to support comprehensive generalization research and promote the adoption of LLM-based testing in broader software engineering practices.
RQ3 Key Insight
Paradigm shift from standalone generators to autonomous agents: Current challenges indicate that treating LLMs merely as a “one-off code generator” has reached a ceiling. Future breakthroughs lie in reshaping them into an autonomous testing agent. Such an agent can actively perceive project context, utilize external tools for verification, and continuously optimize test quality through multi-turn iterations.

Threats to Study Selection. Selection bias constitutes the primary challenge. Despite employing a hybrid retrieval strategy covering five major databases and the snowballing method to maximize coverage, two limitations persist. The screening criterion restricted to English-only publications may have omitted relevant work from non-English communities. Additionally, given the exponential growth of this field, the latest advancements after August 2025 could not be included in the analysis. Therefore,-

We mitigated this risk through a comprehensive, multi-pronged search strategy, which included querying five major academic databases, manually scanning top software engineering conferences and journals, and performing backward snowballing.
Threats to Data Extraction and Synthesis. Subjective bias may affect the accuracy of data extraction and classification. In particular, for defining blurred boundaries such as those between “advanced prompt engineering” and “enhancement techniques,” different researchers may hold diverging perceptions. To mitigate this threat, we implemented a strict cross-validation protocol where each paper was independently coded by two authors, and discrepancies were resolved through group discussions to reach-

Nevertheless, the boundaries between categories (e.g., advanced prompt engineering versus enhancement techniques) can sometimes be fuzzy, and different researchers might classify a small subset of papers differently.
Threats to External Validity (Generalizability). The generalizability (external validity) of our conclusions is limited by the distribution characteristics of the existing literature. Approximately 33% of the literature consists of non-peer-reviewed arXiv preprints. While this ensures timeliness, it also introduces unverified preliminary results. Furthermore, existing research is highly concentrated on Java and Python languages, which limits the applicability of our conclusions to system-level lan-
guages like C++ and Rust or other dynamically typed languages. Therefore, conclusions regarding cross-language generalization capabilities should be viewed as provisional.
Threats from Technical Timeliness and Model Opacity. Technical evolution and model black-boxes constitute unique threats. Given the rapid iteration of LLM technology (such as the leap from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4o), the performance metrics of specific models may quickly become obsolete . Furthermore, the opacity of closed-source commercial models (e.g., updates to training data) may lead to the irreproducibility of experimental results. To address this, this review focuses on distilling general methodolo-
gical paradigms (such as the iterative repair architecture) rather than performance benchmarks of single models, aiming to enhance the durability of our conclusions.

9 Conclusion
Adopting a systems engineering perspective, this paper presents a systematic review of the field of LLM-based unit test generation. Through an in-depth analysis of 115 studies, we not only reveal the dominance of prompt engineering strategies (89%) but also construct a unified analytical framework covering “pre-generation context enrichment,” “post-generation quality assurance,” and “system-level synergy.” This framework elucidates how classic software engineering methods are repurposed to compens-
ate for the inherent deficiencies of LLMs in semantic understanding and output stability. Our analysis indicates that the field is currently at a critical transition period from “single-point generation” to “system-level synergy.” Current challenges, such as the low usability of generated code and the lack of accuracy in test oracles, point to the future evolutionary path of building hybrid intelligent systems that deeply integrate traditional automation technologies, as well as testing agents wit-
h autonomous perception and decision-making capabilities. Based on these findings, we propose the following strategic recommendations for the future development of the field. For practitioners, prioritizing the construction of an “LLM + Iterative Refinement” architecture, which is currently the optimal solution for addressing the low usability issue. In terms of research focus, attention must shift from mere coverage improvement to the enhancement of semantic validity, specifically tackling the co-
re challenge of test oracle generation. At the community level, there is an urgent need to establish a decontaminated, multi-lingual, and multi-domain standardized evaluation ecosystem to replace superficial textual similarity metrics with dynamic execution-based metrics. By addressing these challenges and seizing these opportunities, LLMs hold the promise of transcending the role of auxiliary tools to become a fundamental component of modern software quality assurance.
