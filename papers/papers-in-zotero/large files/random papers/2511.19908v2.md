Probing superhydrophobic surface topography using droplet adhesion
Pawan Kumar∗ and Joseph D. Berry†
Department of Chemical Engineering, The University of Melbourne, Parkville, Melbourne Victoria 3010, Australia
Marta Krasowska
Future Industries Institute, The University of South Australia, Mawson Lakes 5095, Australia (Dated: November 27, 2025)
Understanding contact line dynamics on superhydrophobic surfaces with microscopic structures is essential for designing materials with reduced drag, anti-icing, self-cleaning, and anti-fouling properties. Using numerical simulations, we demonstrate that forces on droplets receding over structured surfaces are governed by microscale deformations near the contact line. We present and experimentally validate an expression demonstrating that adhesion force increases logarithmically with pillar area fr-
action at constant droplet volume and pillar surface chemistry. Furthermore, we establish that the average tensile force measured in direct force measurements provides a more reliable indicator of surface structure than the commonly used maximum force. This newfound insight enables precise quantification of superhydrophobic surface structure using a droplet probe.
Surfaces that can resist wetting by liquids and offer low friction to rolling droplets are critical for low-drag, anti-icing, self-cleaning and anti-fouling applications [14]. Surfaces with microscopic pillars coated with a hydrophobic chemical layer are known to have remarkable superhydrophobicity, characterized by a high advancing contact angle (>150°) and very low contact angle hysteresis (<10°). The wetting behavior of such surfaces depends on the local density of the pillars, also known as th-
e pillar area fraction (φ) [5–13]. Parameters such as the advancing and receding contact angles and the adhesion and friction forces between a droplet and the surface depend on φ. It is therefore imperative to have methods that can estimate the local area fraction on such surfaces. Contact angle goniometry (CAG) is the most commonly used technique for surface characterization due to its simplicity and utility [14, 15]. By measuring advancing (θa) and receding (θr) contact angles, other droplet par-
ameters such as adhesion and friction forces can be indirectly inferred. However, measurement precision is highly dependent on the precise identification of the baseline and the choice of the fitting algorithm used to trace the contour of the droplet [16–22]. Obtaining a perfect droplet contour becomes extremely challenging for surfaces with extreme wettability, such as highly hydrophobic (θa >150°[23]) or highly hydrophilic (θa <40°[24]) surfaces. In addition, if surface roughness is comparable t-
o droplet size, identifying a precise baseline is difficult, and contact angle measurements are incorrect [22]. Additionally, CAG only gives qualitative information on the local surface topography. Direct force measurement techniques such as drop probe microscopy [25–28] and atomic force microscopy [29–31] have emerged as a promising alternative to CAG. These techniques are well suited for directly measuring

friction and adhesion forces on surfaces with complex topography and have been used for detailed qualitative surface characterization [26, 29]. However, previous studies in the literature using direct force measurement to characterise surfaces have failed to yield a quantitative prediction of area fraction from force measurements. For example, Paxson and Varanasi measured the detachment force between a droplet and a superhydrophobic surface with microscopic square pillars, and observed a monotonic-
 increase in the detachment force with φ [32]. In contrast, Jiang et al. measured the maximum adhesion force between a droplet and superhydrophobic surfaces with cylindrical pillars and observed a non-monotonous increase in the maximum adhesion force with φ [33]. In this Letter, using numerical simulations, we demonstrate that microscale interfacial deformation near the contact line governs the force acting on a droplet during retraction from the surface. We present an experimentally validated exp-
ression relating adhesion force to pillar area fraction that can be used to quantitatively characterize a surface from just the value of the maximum adhesion force exerted by a droplet in the Cassie-Baxter wetting regime.
The net vertical force on a droplet in contact with a surface is F = Fp − Fσ where Fp is the pressure force due to the Laplace pressure difference (∆p) and Fσ is the surface tension (or pinning) force acting at the contact line (CL). Fp acts downward, pushing the droplet against the surface, while Fσ exerts a pulling force, acting to detach the droplet. A surface with a higher pinning strength can pin the CL for a longer duration of time, during which the interface near the CL continuously deforms-
 as the droplet is gradually pulled away from the surface. The relationship between force and interfacial deformation is crucial to developing a universal model for the adhesion force as a function of the surface topography. Microscopic imaging of moving CL is difficult, and it is not possible to capture multiple CL jump events that occur simultaneously at different points around the circumference of the droplet [32, 34–40].

To better understand the relationship between adhesion forces and surface topography, we employed the numerical technique presented in the accompanying paper [41] to simulate the microscale interfacial dynamics during a typical adhesion force measurement experiment. Fig. 1 shows the typical variation in droplet parameters as it is pulled away from the surface during the recede stage [41]. Due to the CL pinning, the meridional curvature (κ1, Fig. 1a in [41]) decreases (Fig. 1a), and the Laplace pre-
ssure inside the droplet (∆p) gradually decreases as the droplet is stretched (Fig. 1b). The CL remains pinned until κ1 reduces to a value at which the interface cannot exist in equilibrium, (ie. a constant mean curvature with Young’s angle boundary condition at the CL), at which the CL executes a jump (Fig. 1a). The CL
will pin again at a new location if the conditions for the droplet equilibrium are satisfied. During the CL jump, κ1 increases slightly, depending on the distance the CL moves during the jump. This results in a slight increase in ∆p, explaining the sawtooth variation in the Laplace pressure. During this stage, when the CL is pinned, the kinematics of the interface dictates a gradual decrease in θm (measured at the apparent CL at a height ∆z above the pillars, see Fig. 2a). A decreasing ∆p and θm r-
esults in an increasing magnitude of the force F , again leading to a sawtooth variation observed experimentally by Liimatainen et al. [26]. The force reaches a maximum (Fmax) just before the droplet detaches from the surface at a smaller force Fdetach (Fig. 1d, see end matter A). The droplet base radius (R), measured as the radius of the apparent CL at ∆z = 20 μm, moves in a step-like manner due to the CL pinning followed by occasional jumps (Fig. 1e). The change in force due to a jump is higher -
at locations corresponding to i and iii in comparison to v, because these jumps occur in the direction of the surface structure, ie. in the x (or y) axis direction. When a jump is aligned with surface structure the interface dissipates a greater magnitude of energy compared to when it jumps in the direction 45° to the surface structure [42], eg. for v → vi (Fig. 1f). To confirm this experimentally, we measured the adhesion force on superhydrophobic surfaces with cylindrical pi-
llars of diameter 10 μm and height 20 μm (etched onto a silicon wafer using a dry etching technique [54]). Before
force measurement, thin layers of chromium (5 nm) and gold (25 nm) were deposited by physical vapour deposition, and then the surfaces were hydrophobized with a monolayer of octadecanethiol from a 1 mM ethanol solution ([54]). The advancing and receding contact angles on flat hydrophobized surfaces were measured to be 111.2° and 98.7°, respectively ([54]). A Dataphysics DCAT-21 tensiometer was used to measure the adhesion between the surface and a 1.6 μL droplet of ultra pure DI water (less than 4-

It is also clear that the fit of Eq. 3 to Favg is better than the fit to Fmax, and is therefore better representative of the surface topography.
An important point to note here is that, in all the experiments conducted in this study, droplet evaporation was negligible due to the relatively high droplet receding velocity (the stage upon which the surface is placed was moved away from the droplet at a speed 25 μm/s). To determine the role of evaporation in the measurement, we conducted experiments measuring the adhesion force between a droplet 1.6 μL and pillared surfaces with area fractions ranging from 0.016 to 0.30. The droplet was allowe-
d to evaporate and the stage was kept stationary. The Fmax values are shown in Fig. 8 as open squares. We observed a general increase in Fmax with φ, but without any universal relationship between them. Similar observations were made by Jiang et al. [33]. We also conducted numerical simulations that mimic droplet evaporation (see §IV C in [41]). The simulation results are shown in Fig. 8 as triangles and show a monotonous increase with φ that can be predicted by Eq. (3) which is shown as a solid r-
ed curve. The discrepancy between the simulation results and the experimental data is due to the change in droplet weight as a result of evaporation. The tensiometer apparatus is usually calibrated with the initial weight of the droplet, and any change in it due to evaporation results in under-prediction of Fmax (see §IV D in [41]). To incorporate the difference in droplet weight due to evaporation, we plot Fmax + ∆W with φ in Fig. 8 as circles. Here, ∆W is the change in droplet weight during the -
recede stage. The maximum force considering the change in droplet weight due to evaporation during recede shows a monotonous increase with φ that can be represented by Eq. (3) (green curve in Fig. 8) and is in better agreement with the experimental data. However, the simulation assumes negligible evaporation of the droplet during the advance stage, which may not be true for the experiments, and explains the discrepancy between the two. In addition, the numerical results form an upper bound to the -
experimental data, re-affirming the fact that droplet evaporation results in a reduction in Fmax. Therefore, an important criterion for employing the direct force measurement method as a surface characterization tool is to minimize the effect of droplet evaporation. This also explains the different observations made by Paxson and Varanasi [32] where the evaporation

To conclude, we have shown that the average pulling force exerted by a droplet for a given droplet volume, pillar geometry, and inherent receding angle during typical droplet probe microscopy when evaporation is negligible, is a function of pillar area fraction only and can be represented by Eq. (3). The coefficients in the fitting equation can be fully predicted using the numerical technique presented i-
n the accompanying paper [41]. The experimentally measured average force along with the numerically predicted coefficients in Eq. (3) are sufficient to quanti
tatively characterize a superhydrophobic pillared surface.

Appendix A: Variation in pressure and surface tension forces during the recede stage
Fig. 5 shows the variation in pressure (Fp), surface tension (Fσ) and total force (Fp + Fσ) with time (in simulation units). Initially, Fp is relatively higher than Fσ. Up to t = t2, the CL is pinned and Fσ and Fp show an increasing and decreasing trend, respectively, due to a decreasing trend θm and ∆p. At t = t1, Fσ and Fp are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, and therefore the net force (F = Fp + Fσ) is zero. Further stretching of the droplet results in a negative net vertical force-
 or a tensile (pulling) force on the surface. The tensile region is shown as the shaded area in the figure. The CL starts to recede at t = t2, executing a jump which results in a drop in both Fp and Fσ. Now, since Fp varies with the square of the base radius, it drops faster than Fσ, which depends linearly on the base radius. This gives the force curve its peculiar form in which the maximum force (F = Fmax at t = t3) is achieved before the droplet detaches from the surface at a comparatively small-
er force (F = Fdetach at t = t4).

Appendix C: Direct force Measurement
Direct force measurement experiments were conducted using the DCAT-21 tensiometer (DataPhysics). First, a superhydrophobic pillared surface, which was a 1.5 × 1.5 cm2 piece, is gently placed on top of a movable stage. A 0.91 mm outer diameter needle is fixed to clamp attached to the tensiometer, for force measurement. In the present study, a droplet of precise volume (1.6 μL) of DI water (Milli-Q) was generated using a syringe pump, which was then attached to the tip of the needle. The needle tip -
was tightly packed with Blu Tack to avoid water entrainment. During the advance stage, the stage is moved at a speed of 50 μm/s until the droplet contacts the surface and the force increases to approximately 32 μN. Once the maximum compression force is reached, the stage is moved downward at a speed of 25 μm/s until the droplet detaches from the surface. The tensiometer measures the force between the droplet and the surface at a frequency of 50 Hz. The droplet was imaged simultaneously using a Din-
oLite camera (Edge Plus AM8917MZTL) to record the contact angle of the droplet and the diameter of the base (see Fig. 7a). A second set of experiments was also conducted to see the effect of droplet evaporation. In these experiments, the stage was moved upwards at a speed of 10 μm/s until a compression force of 32 μN was reached. After this, the stage was kept stationary and the droplet was allowed to evaporate. The force between the droplet and the surface during this was measured by the tensiome-
