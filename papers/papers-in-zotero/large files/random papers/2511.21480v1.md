1 Introduction
Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surfaces are canonical models of random planar geometry, originally motivated by conformal field theory and string theory [Pol81]. They possess a rich mathematical structure, have deep connections to probability, geometry, combinatorics and theoretical physics [KPZ88, DS11, DKRV16], and remain the subject of many open problems; see [BP24] for an extensive exposition. A central conjecture is that LQG surfaces, which form a one-parameter family indexed by γ P p0, 2s, -
arise as scaling limits of discrete random surfaces known as random planar maps. The best known results in this direction concern models where face degrees are suitably controlled. Progress culminated in the works of Le Gall [LG13], Miermont [Mie13], Miller–Sheffield [MS21] and Holden–Sun [HS23], which establish convergence in the uniform bounded-degree case (γ “
a8{3). It has been predicted in physics, see for example [DKRV16] for a precise conjecture, that a natural way to escape from the pure-gravity universality class is to couple the maps with critical statistical mechanics models. Proving rigorous convergence results in this general setting remains a major open problem, although spectacular progress has been made recently for the related model of Boltzmann planar maps with heavy-tailed face degree distributions [CMR25]. In this work, we consider the -
infinite-volume Fortuin–Kasteleyn (FK) decorated planar map model, which provides a natural setting for studying scaling limits outside of the pure-gravity regime. For q ě 0, a finite (self-dual) FKpqq-decorated planar map of size k is a random planar map Mk with k edges together with a distinguished subset of edges Pk. 2 Background and preliminary observations
We provide some background and definitions for the FK-weighted planar map model. We also comment on the existing literature, although some of our preliminary observations and constructions seem to be new.
2.1 Finite FK decorated random planar maps
2.1.1 Definition of the model
We start with some notation related to planar maps. Let Mk be the set of rooted planar maps with k ą 0 edges, that is, proper embeddings on the sphere of finite connected graphs with k edges and one distinguished, oriented root edge, defined up to continuous deformation. For m P Mk, let V pmq, Epmq and F pmq be the set of vertices, edges and faces of m respectively. We declare the root vertex of m to be the initial vertex of the oriented root edge, and the root face to be the face of m to the righ-
t of the oriented root edge. The degree of a face f P F pmq is the number of edges incident to this face, counting twice any cut edge lying strictly inside f. In this subsection we will describe the law of an FK planar map (of size k), which is a random pair pm, pq where m P Mk and p is a subset of Epmq called open edges. To do this, we will first explain how to construct a loop-decorated triangulation T pm, pq from pm, pq, called the Tutte map. See Figure 2 for an illustration. Given a pair pm, p-
q as above, we let m: be the dual map of m, so that each vertex v: P V pm:q corresponds to a face f P F pmq and two dual vertices are connected by a dual edge if the faces are adjacent. The root edge of m: is, by convention, the oriented edge that crosses the root edge of m from right to left, which means that the root vertex of m: corresponds to the root face of m. The edge set p induces a subset p: of Epm:q by declaring that e: P p: if and only if the primal edge e R p. We build a new planar map-
 Qpmq with vertex set V pmq Y V pm:q by connecting an edge between v: and every vertex v that is adjacent to f in m. Note that for each edge e P Epmq (and the corresponding e: P Epm:q), there are four edges connecting the endpoints of e and e:. Hence Qpmq is a rooted, planar quadrangulation (i.e. all faces have four incident edges), with the convention that the root edge points from the root vertex of m: to the root vertex of m. Adding edges in p and p: to Qpmq, we build a planar rooted triangulat-
ion T pm, pq, since every quadrangle is split into two triangles by exactly one edge in p Y p:. These triangles will be referred to as primal (resp. dual) triangles if they are produced by edges in p (resp. p:). For visualisation purposes we think of (primal) edges in p as being coloured blue and (dual) edges in p: as being coloured red. The root edge of T pm, pq is the same as that of Qpmq. On T pm, pq, we can start from any triangle and draw a unique path that only crosses the triangles through -
edges in Qpmq. This path will finally return to the starting triangle and turn out to be a loop. Repeating the procedure (starting from another arbitrary uncrossed triangle each time) until every triangle is crossed by a loop, we obtain a fully packed ensemble of loops Lpm, pq that separate the primal and the dual connected components created by pp, p:q. 2.1.2 The Mullin–Bernardi–Sheffield bijection
In this section we recall the bijective frameworks of Mullin [Mul67], Bernardi [Ber08] and Sheffield [She16], which relate random planar maps decorated by edge sets to words in a certain alphabet Θ (see below), which can be viewed as inventory accumulation processes.
Inventory accumulation model. Let Θ “ tc, h, C, H, Fu, called the alphabet of letters. A word w “ θ1θ2 . . . θk (θi P Θ) is a concatenation of letters. Denote by H the empty word. In the sequel, we will call the letter c (respectively h) a cheeseburger (respectively hamburger ), C (respectively H) a cheeseburger order (respectively hamburger order ) and F a flexible/freshest order. A word w is then viewed as an (ordered) sequence of events in a restaurant selling either hamburgers or cheeseburgers-
. For example, the word w “ hcFC corresponds to the restaurant producing first a hamburger, then a cheeseburger, and finally two customers ordering a “freshest” burger and then a cheeseburger. It is conceptually useful to view this string of letters as describing, from left to right, the evolution of a stack of burgers: new burgers are pushed onto the stack, and (fulfilled) orders remove a burger from it. Thus, the model can be seen as an inventory model at a LIFO (last-in, first-out) retailer wit-
h two products and three types of orders. The reduced word w is the word that results after matching burgers and orders in w, following the reduction rule that cC “ hH “ cF “ hF “ H and cH “ Hc, hC “ Ch.1 The rule is interpreted by fulfilling the orders – whenever possible – in the way they were placed (i.e. from left to right), as follows. A hamburger order (cheeseburger order) is matched with the first remaining hamburger (cheeseburger) discovered when tracing back along the sequence (reading th-
e word from right to left), while a freshest order is matched with the first remaining burger (no restriction on type) discovered when tracing back. 2.5 Proof outline
We now outline our approach to proving the scaling limit stated in Theorem 1.1. As discussed in Section 2.2.2, the law of the burger count S is independent of p and coincides with that of a simple symmetric random walk. The main challenge lies in controlling the discrepancy process D. For context and comparison, we start by briefly recalling Sheffield’s argument [She16] in the regime 0 ă q ă 4. The proof is elementary in that it relies solely on martingale techniques. At a high level, the argument-
 hinges upon two central ideas:
• Tightness. The first key step is to show that the discrepancy D remains of the same order as the burger count S (denoted C in Sheffield’s notation). To this end, Sheffield introduces a clever lattice-path encoding of burger stacks, known as embedded stacks, which has the nice feature of being monotone [She16, Section 3.4]. This monotonicity implies that D is at most of
the same order as the burger count S, leading to tightness in n for the pair pSn{?n, Dn{?nq.
• Identification of the limit. The second step is to show that, among the indices 1, . . . , n,
the number of F symbols matched to the left of 0 is negligible at scale ?n (these F symbols are called unmatched in this paper). Proving this fact is technically involved but relies again only on martingale techniques and a variance estimate. This observation allows one to decompose the discrepancy Dn at time n into an independent sum of k terms distributed as Dtn{ku, up
to an error term that is negligible at scale ?n. Hence, the scaling limit must be infinitely divisible, and one can further identify it as the process described in (2.7).
In the regime q “ 4, both arguments break down. Indeed, Dn is no longer of the same order as Sn, while we do not have precise enough control on the variance to ensure that the number of unmatched F symbols is negligible at the right scale – let alone to identify the limit. Instead, we use the planar map structure to obtain some preliminary information about the hamburger-cheeseburger walks. Our start point is to map the FKp4q model to the loop-Op2q model on triangulations, from which we derive bot-
h the exact expression and the asymptotic behaviour of the associated partition function Fl. This part, presented in Section 3, draws on ideas from analytic combinatorics (in particular the gasket decomposition introduced in [BBG12a]) and has a different flavour from the rest of the article. It leads to the first rigorous derivation of the loopOp2q partition function expression and asymptotic (Theorem 1.4), thereby confirming the physics prediction of Gaudin and Kostov [GK89]. Our strategy is then-
 to approximate the discrepancy using Markovian walks, rather than dealing with the discrepancy directly. To do this, we construct two explorations – one into the past and one into the future – as defined in Section 2.2.3. These explorations admit a natural interpretation in terms of certain peeling explorations in the infinite-volume FKp4q planar map. We relate in Section 4 some observables of the exploration into the past (namely, hitting times) to the partition function Fl,6 which, in particula-
r, implies the first two statements of Theorem 1.3. To the best of our knowledge, this connection appears to be novel. It goes through the notion of skeleton words that encode clusters through the Mullin–Bernardi–Sheffield bijection (Lemma 2.3). Using Tauberian theorems and regular variation techniques, it further enables us, in Section 5, to analyse the step distribution of the walk and to derive the scaling limit of the exploration into the past.
