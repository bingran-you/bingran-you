RAFAŁ GRUSZCZYŃSKI, PAULA MENCHÓN, AND WILLIAM ZULUAGA
Abstract. The ternary extended contact relation was introduced in (Ivanova, 2020) as a more expressive counterpart of the standard binary contact relation. The class of Boolean algebras expanded with the relation was named Extended Contact Algebras (ECAs). In this work, we take an algebraic perspective on ECAs, interpreting the ternary relation as a form of entailment. We introduce Pseudo-Inference Algebras, purely algebraic structures where the ternary relation is replaced by a monotone ternary o-
perator, capturing the logical character of extended contact. We show that the subclass of relational Pseudo-Inference Algebras corresponds precisely to ECAs and generates a subvariety of strict PSI-Algebras, which forms a discriminator variety. Furthermore, we extend Stone duality to this ternary context, introducing descriptive PSI-frames and establishing three interrelated dualities that differ in their morphisms while sharing the same class of topological objects. The framework developed in th-
e paper provides a unified relational semantics for Boolean algebras equipped with monotone ternary operators, connecting spatial and logical notions within a categorical and topological setting. Keywords: Extended Contact Algebras, extended contact relation, modal algebras, ternary relation, ternary operations, topological duality MSC: Primary 06E25; Secondary 03G05
1. Introduction
The class of Extended Contact Algebras (ECAs, for short) was introduced by Ivanova (2020). The idea was motivated by certain limitations related to the well-known Boolean Contact Algebras (BCAs), i.e., Boolean algebras expanded with a binary relation C of contact. BCAs were introduced in the early 2000s by Stell (2000) and Düntsch and Winter (2004) as an algebraic tool for representing and studying the phenomenon of the nearness of regions in space. From a certain point of view, they could be seen-
 as an abstract approach to proximity spaces in the sense of Thron (1973) (see, e.g., Düntsch and Vakarelov, 2007). The idea of BCAs proved both fruitful and useful, giving insight into the algebraic aspects of Region Connection Calculus and developing the so-called Region-based Theories of Space (also known as mereotopologies) that had been investigated at least since the times of de Laguna (1922) and Whitehead (1920; 1929). Among others, it turned out that BCAs have a considerable expressive pow-
er that lets express various topological properties by means of the contact relation. However, unsurprisingly, natural properties were discovered that could not be grasped within the framework of contact relations. One of these was the property of internal connectedness of regular closed subsets of topological spaces. Since the topological property of connectedness of regions—particularly important for mereotopology (Pratt and Schoop, 2000)—can be naturally expressed by means of the contact relati-
on, it could be expected that he similar situation holds for the property of internal connectedness. Contrary to this, Tatyana Ivanova (2020) proved that the property cannot be formulated by means of contact.1 At the same time, she put forward the idea of a ternary relation of extended contact, which strengthens the ordinary contact and allows for a definition of the internal connectedness of regular closed sets.

Formally, an extended contact algebra is a structure of the form
(A, ∨, ∧, ¬, 0, 1, ⊢),
where (A, ∨, ∧, ¬, 0, 1) is a non-degenerate Boolean algebra and ⊢ is a ternary relation on A such that, for all a, b, d, e, f ∈ R, the following conditions hold:
(ExtCA0) If (a, b) ⊢ f , then (a ∨ d, b) ⊢ d ∨ f .
(ExtCA1) If (a, b) ⊢ d, (a, b) ⊢ e and (d, e) ⊢ f , then (a, b) ⊢ f .
(ExtCA2) If a ≤ f , then (a, b) ⊢ f .
(ExtCA3) If (a, b) ⊢ f , then a ∧ b ≤ f .
(ExtCA4) If (a, b) ⊢ f , then (b, a) ⊢ f .
It is noteworthy that the relation ⊢ has a logical character (in a certain sense), since it behaves analogously to a consequence relation, capturing a form of inference or entailment between regions within the underlying Boolean framework. Moreover, this relational perspective—and its generalization from (Vakarelov, 2017) in the form of sequent algebrasoffers a new perspective on the region-based approach to theories of space, establishing a conceptual bridge between the spatial notion of nearness-
 and the logical notion of entailment.
In this paper, we adopt the entailment perspective as dominant and define and investigate a class of Pseudo-Inference Algebras that can be viewed as purely algebraic counterparts of Extended Contact Algebras introduced by Ivanova. Purely algebraic, since we replace the ternary relation by a ternary operator which is (almost) a possibility operator, and we translate the axioms for extended contact into constraints put upon the operator. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the notio-
n of Extended Contact Algebra and presents a simplified, yet equivalent, axiomatization of the system originally introduced in (Ivanova, 2020). This formulation yields a more transparent algebraic characterization of the associated operator while preserving the expressive power of the original relational framework. Finally, Section 5 develops the topological counterpart of the theory by extending Stone duality to the ternary context. We introduce the class of descriptive PSI-frames and establish three interrelated dualities which share the same class of topological objects-

Throughout the paper, we assume the reader is familiar with standard notions from category theory and universal algebra as presented in (Mac Lane, 1978) and (Burris and Sankappanavar, 2011), respectively.
2. Extended Contact Algebras
As we mentioned in the introduction, the first axiomatization of ECAs is due to Ivanova (2020). In our approach, we will use a slightly simplified set of constraints that is equivalent to the original one. Thanks to this, on the side of the operator associated with the relation, we obtain a simpler and easier to handle set of algebraic postulates. We will prove that an Extended Contact Algebra is equivalent to a structure of the form (A, ⊢), where A = (A, ∨, ∧, ¬, 0, 1) is a non-degenerate Boolean-
 algebra (whose elements are called regions) and ⊢ is a ternary relation on A such that, for all a, b, c, d, f ∈ A (≤ is the

standard binary Boolean order relation):
(EC0) If (a, b) ⊢ f , then (a ∨ d, b) ⊢ f ∨ d .
(EC1) If (a, b) ⊢ d, (a, b) ⊢ e and (d, e) ⊢ f , then (a, b) ⊢ f .
(EC2) (a, b) ⊢ a ∨ f .
(EC3) If (a, a) ⊢ f , then a ≤ f .
(EC4) If (a, b) ⊢ f , then (b, a) ⊢ f .
⊣ The constraints can be interpreted as expressing elementary facts about entailment (in the very special case when we have no more than two premises). To see this, let us interpret the Boolean order x ≤ y as x carries more information than or the same information as y. According to (EC0), if a and b entail f , then we can weaken premises and the conclusion simultaneously. (EC1) is an instance of the cut rule: if a and b entail both d and e, which entail f , then we can eliminate d and e. Accordin-
g to (EC2) we can always weaken the conclusion, and according to (EC3) the conclusion never carries more information than the premise. Finally, (EC4) says that the order of premises is not relevant for the entailment.
Proposition 2.1. If (EC1) and (EC2) hold in a structure (A, ⊢), we have that
(2.1) If (a, b) ⊢ c and c ≤ f , then (a, b) ⊢ f .
Proof. Indeed, suppose that (a, b) ⊢ c and c ≤ f . By (EC2) we have that (c, c) ⊢ c ∨ f , so (c, c) ⊢ f . By (EC1) it must be the case that (a, b) ⊢ f . □
Propositions 2.2 and 2.4 below show that our set of axioms is equivalent to the one originally introduced in (Ivanova, 2020).
Proposition 2.2. The axiom (EC2) is equivalent to (ExtCA2).
Proof. It is clear that (ExtCA2) entails (EC2). For the other direction, if a ≤ f , then we have that a ∨ f = f . But by (EC2) it must be the case that (a, b) ⊢ a ∨ f , so (a, b) ⊢ f . □
Since by (ExtCA2) we have that a ≤ f entails that (a, a) ⊢ f , then we can strengthen (EC3) to full equivalence
(2.2) (a, a) ⊢ f iff a ≤ f .
Proposition 2.3. If (EC1), (EC2) and (EC4) hold in a structure (A, ⊢), it is the case that
(2.3) If (a, b) ⊢ c and f ≤ a, then (f, b) ⊢ c.
Proof. Suppose that (a, b) ⊢ c and f ≤ a. Thus, by (EC2) we obtain that (f, b) ⊢ a and by (EC2) and (EC4) that (f, b) ⊢ b. From these, by (EC1), it follows that (f, b) ⊢ c. □
Proposition 2.4. Modulo remaining axioms, (EC3) is equivalent to (ExtCA3).
Proof. One direction is immediate. For the other, assume that (EC3) obtains and that (a, b) ⊢ f . Using (2.3) and (EC4) we conclude that (a ∧ b, a ∧ b) ⊢ f , and so a ∧ b ≤ f , by the assumption. □
The extended contact is a more expressive counterpart of the standard binary contact relation C. The latter can be defined by means of ⊢ as follows
a C b :←→ (a, b) ⊬ 0
but ⊢ cannot be captured in the standard language of Boolean Contact Algebras (see Ivanova, 2020, Propositions 2.1 and 3.1). Similarly, as in the case of the contact relation, the standard interpretation of extended contact is topological, that is for a topological space (X, τ ), any subalgebra S of the Boolean algebra RC(τ ) of regular closed subsets of X with the ternary relation ⊢τ given by
(A, B) ⊢τ C :←→ A ∩ B ⊆ C

The operation ∩ on the right side of the equivalence is the usual set-theoretical intersection, that, in general, is different from the meet operation of RC(τ ), which for any A, B is given by A∧B := Cl Int(A∩B), the closure of the interior of the intersection. Proposition 2.5 (Balbiani and Ivanova, 2021, Proposition 3). Let (A, ⊢) be an extended contact algebra. For all a, b, d, e ∈ A, the following conditions hold:
(2.4) (0, 1) ⊢ a.
(2.5) If (a, b) ⊢ c and (x, b) ⊢ c, then (a ∨ x, b) ⊢ c.
(2.6) If (a, b) ⊢ c and d ≤ a, then (d, b) ⊢ c.

We conclude this section by presenting some properties of the characteristic function of ⊢. In a sense that will be made explicit in the following section, this result reveals the algebraic behavior that underlies ECAs.
Lemma 2.6. Let (A ⊢) be an extended contact algebra and let us write χ⊢ for the characteristic function of ⊢. Then, for every a, b, c, x ∈ A the following hold:
(2.8) χ⊢(0, b, c) = χ⊢(a, 0, c) = χ⊢(a, b, 1) = 1 ,
(2.9) χ⊢(a ∨ x, b, c) = χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(x, b, c) ,
(2.10) χ⊢(a, b ∨ x, c) = χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(a, x, c) ,
(2.11) χ⊢(a, b, c ∧ x) ≤ χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(a, b, x) .
Proof. (2.8) Since 0 ≤ c, then, by (EC2), (0, b) ⊢ c, so χ⊢(0, b, c) = 1. Similarly, by (EC2) and (EC4), we obtain that χ⊢(a, 0, c) = 1. Finally, because a ≤ 1, from (EC2) again we obtain (a, b) ⊢ 1, and thus χ⊢(a, b, 1) = 1.
(2.9) For the left-to-right direction, let χ⊢(a ∨ x, b, c) = 1. Thus, (a ∨ x, b) ⊢ c, and from this by (2.6) applied twice we obtain that (a, b) ⊢ c and (x, b) ⊢ c. In consequence, χ⊢(a, b, c) = 1 and χ⊢(x, b, c) = 1. Thus, χ⊢(a ∨ x, b, c) ≤ χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(x, b, c).
For the right-to-left direction, suppose that χ⊢(a, b, c) = χ⊢(x, b, c) = 1. Then (a, b) ⊢ c and (x, b) ⊢ c. (2.5) implies that (a ∨ x, b) ⊢ c, so χ⊢(a ∨ x, b, c) = 1. Therefore χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(x, b, c) ≤ χ⊢(a ∨ x, b, c). Joining both directions, we obtain the equality.
(2.10) Observe that (EC4) implies that for every a, b, c ∈ A, χ⊢(a, b, c) = χ⊢(b, a, c). Then, by (2.9) we obtain
χ⊢(a, b ∨ x, c) = χ⊢(b ∨ x, a, c)
= χ⊢(b, a, c) ∧ χ⊢(x, a, c)
= χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(a, x, c).
(2.11) Suppose that χ⊢(a, b, c ∧ x) = 1, i.e., (a, b) ⊢ c ∧ x. By (2.7) we obtain (a, b) ⊢ c and (a, b) ⊢ x. Therefore, χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(a, b, x) = 1. Thus, χ⊢(a, b, c ∧ x) ≤ χ⊢(a, b, c) ∧ χ⊢(a, b, x).
This concludes the proof. □
3. Pseudo-Inference Algebras
In this section, we introduce the variety of Pseudo-Inference Algebras and show that there is a one-to-one correspondence between its subclass, consisting of the so-called relational algebras, and the class of ECAs.

Definition 3.1. A Boolean algebra with a ternary weakly normal monotonic operator (3BAMO for short), is a pair (A, ♢) where A = (A, ∨, ∧, ¬, 0, 1) is a Boolean algebra, and ♢ is a ternary operator on A such that for every a, b, c, x ∈ A the following hold:
(MO1) ♢(0, b, c) = ♢(a, 0, c) = ♢(a, b, 0) = 0 ,
(MO2) ♢(a ∨ x, b, c) = ♢(a, b, c) ∨ ♢(x, b, c) ,
(MO3) ♢(a, b ∨ x, c) = ♢(a, b, c) ∨ ♢(a, x, c) ,
(MO4) ♢(a, b, c) ∨ ♢(a, b, x) ≤ ♢(a, b, c ∨ x) .
⊣
As we see, ♢ behaves like the standard modal possibility operator in the first two coordinates, but is only monotonic in the third coordinate. Any 3BAMO is a special case of a Boolean algebra with n-ary monotonic operator (a BAMO) introduced in (Celani, 2009, Definition 1).
Example 3.2. The following is an example of a 3BAMO defined on the four-element Boolean algebra {0, a, b, 1}, with b = ¬a. We only exhibit the values of the operation ♢ that are different from zero. In all other cases, ♢(x, y, z) = 0.
♢(1, 1, 1) = 1, ♢(1, 1, a) = a, ♢(1, 1, b) = 1,
♢(1, a, 1) = 1, ♢(1, a, a) = a, ♢(1, b, 1) = 1,
♢(1, b, b) = 1, ♢(a, 1, 1) = 1, ♢(a, 1, a) = a,
♢(a, a, 1) = 1, ♢(a, a, a) = a, ♢(b, 1, 1) = 1,
♢(b, 1, b) = 1, ♢(b, b, 1) = 1, ♢(b, b, b) = 1.
Note that ♢(a, a, a) ∨ ♢(a, a, b) < ♢(a, a, 1). ⊣
To obtain the variety of Pseudo-Inference Algebras we extend the 3BAMO axioms with additional postulates corresponding to axioms (EC0)–(EC4).
Definition 3.3. A 3BAMO (A, ♢) is called a Pseudo-Inference Algebra (PSI-Algebra for short), if for every a, b, c, d, f ∈ B, the following conditions hold:
(PI1) ♢(a, b, f ) ≤ ♢(a, b, ¬d) ∨ ♢(a, b, ¬e) ∨ ♢(d, e, f ) ,
(PI2) ♢(a, b, ¬a) = 0 ,
(PI3) a ∧ f ≤ ♢(a, a, f ) ,
(PI4) ♢(a, b, f ) ≤ ♢(b, a, f ) .
⊣
The proof of the following result is a straightforward consequence of (MO3) and (MO4). The details are left to the reader.
Proposition 3.4. (PI2) is equivalent to the equation
(3.1) ♢(a, 1, ¬a) = 0
and to each of the two quasi-equations below
(3.2) a ∧ f = 0 ⇒ ♢(a, b, f ) = 0 ,
(3.3) a ∧ f = 0 ⇒ ♢(a, 1, f ) = 0 .
Example 3.5. Every Boolean algebra can be turned into a PSI-Algebra by defining
♢(a, b, c) := a ∧ b ∧ c .

♢(a, b, c) ∈ {0, 1}.

As shown in Lemma 2.6, the characteristic function of the extended contact relation ⊢ behaves similarly to the ternary operation ♢ of 3BAMOs. 4. Relational PSI-algebras
In this section we show that the class of relational PSI-Algebras generates the subvariety of strict PSI-Algebras, namely those PSI-Algebras that satisfy the following additional equations:
(R1) ♢(x, y, a) ∧ ¬♢(x, y, b) ≤ ♢(1, 1, a ∧ ¬b),
(R2) ♢(x, a, y) ∧ ¬♢(x, b, y) ≤ ♢(1, a ∧ ¬b, 1),
(S) ♢(a, b, c) ≤ μ(♢(a, b, c)),
where μ(z) := ¬♢(1, 1, ¬z) ∧ ¬♢(1, ¬z, 1). Moreover, we show that this subvariety is, in fact, a discriminator variety.
Lemma 4.1. In every relational PSI-Algebra, (R1), (R2) and (S) hold.
Proof. It is immediate that every relational PSI-Algebra satisfies (S).
(R1) Suppose that ♢(x, y, a) ∧ ¬♢(x, y, b) = 1. Then, ♢(x, y, a) = 1 and ♢(x, y, b) = 0. It follows that a ∧ ¬b > 0. Indeed, if a ∧ ¬b = 0, then a ≤ b, and by (MO4) we obtain 1 = ♢(x, y, a) ≤ ♢(x, y, b) = 0, a contradiction. By (PI3), we have 0 < a∧¬b ≤ ♢(1, 1, a∧¬b), which implies that ♢(1, 1, a ∧ ¬b) = 1, as required.
(R2) Suppose that ♢(x, a, y) ∧ ¬♢(x, b, y) = 1. Then, ♢(x, a, y) = 1 and ♢(x, b, y) = 0. By the same reasoning as before, it follows that a ∧ ¬b > 0. By (PI3) and (MO2), we have 0 < a ∧ ¬b ≤ ♢(a ∧ ¬b, a ∧ ¬b, 1) ≤ ♢(1, a ∧ ¬b, 1), which leads to ♢(1, a ∧ ¬b, 1) = 1.

A variety V is said to have the congruence extension property (abbreviated as CEP) if, for every subalgebra A of an algebra B ∈ V and for each congruence δ on A, there exists a congruence θ on B such that δ = θ ∩ A2. Moreover, V is said to have definable principal congruences (DPC) if there exists a formula ζ(x, y, u, v) in the first-order language of V such that, for every algebra A ∈ V and all a, b, c, d ∈ A,
(c, d) ∈ CgA(a, b) ⇐⇒ A |= ζ[a, b, c, d],
where CgA(a, b) denotes the smallest congruence on A containing (a, b). If the formula ζ(x, y, u, v) can be expressed as a finite conjunction of equations, then V is said to have equationally definable principal congruences (EDPC). Finally, a variety V is called arithmetical if, for every algebra A ∈ V, the congruence lattice of A is both distributive and permutable; equivalently, V is arithmetical if it is both congruence distributive and congruence permutable.
