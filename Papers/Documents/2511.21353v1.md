Analogue of the Galois Theory for arbitrary finite field
extensions
V. V. Bavula
Abstract
This paper is a finishing touch to the (over 200 years) classical ‘Galois Theory’ of arbitrary finite field extensions, i.e. the goal of it is to describe intermediate subfields of an arbitrary finite field extension via invariants of ‘natural/obvious’ objects that are associated with subfields via two Galois-type correspondences. The classical Galois Theory covers the case of finite Galois field extensions. For finite Galois field extensions the objects are their Galois groups and their invariant-
s. In [2], we introduce a new (ring theoretic) approach to the Galois Theory which is based on the principle of maximal symmetry. In [3], the maximal symmetry of normal finite field extensions yields an analogue of the Galois Theory for them. For a normal finite field extension L/K the ‘natural/obvious’ objects are the subalgebra D(L/K) ⋊ G(L/K) of End(L/K) that is generated by the automorphism group G(L/K) and the algebra D(L/K) of differential operators on L/K and its ‘invariants’. The ‘maximal -
symmetry’ means the equality End(L/K) = D(L/K) ⋊ G(L/K) which turns out to be a characteristic property of normal finite field extensions, [3]. The aim of this paper is to obtain an analogue of the Galois Theory for arbitrary finite field extensions based on results and ideas of [2] and [3].
Key Words: finite field extension, B-extension, Galois extension, Galois group, Galois Correspondence, skew group algebra, invariants, algebra of differential operators, differential constants, centralizer, central simple algebra, the Double Centralizer Theorem. Finite purely inseparable field extensions have trivial automorphism group but rich subfield structure. They are normal field extensions but normal field extensions are a larger class. For a long time it was an open problem to produce a ‘Galois Theory’ for normal finite field extensions and the class of finite purely inseparable field extensions was a natural starting point. In [7, 8] (1937, 1944), Jacobson introduced an analogue of the Galois theory for purely inseparable field extensions L/K of -
exponent 1 (meaning that the p’th power of every element of L is in K), where the Galois groups (which are trivial) are replaced by restricted Lie algebras of derivations. Jacobson’s theory establishes a correspondence between intermediate fields of a purely inseparable extension of exponent 1 and restricted Lie subalgebras of derivations. The relationship between derivations and purely inseparable field extensions of exponent 1 was studied by Baer [1] (1927). A field extension is called simple or-
 primitive if it is generated by a single element. A purely inseparable extension is called a modular extension if it is a tensor product of simple field extensions. Modular extensions can be seen as ‘free’ objects in the class of finite purely inseparable field extensions (in the same vein as polynomial algebras are ‘free’ objects in the Algebraic Geometry), i.e. they satisfy no relations (apart from the obvious ones). In particular, every extension of exponent 1 is modular, but there are non-mod-
ular extensions of exponent 2 as shown by Weisfeld [11] (1965). In more detail, let us consider subfields K = Fp(xp, yp, zp2 ) and L = K(z, xz + y) of the field Fp(x, y, z) of rational functions in 3 variables. Weisfeld [11] proved that the field extension L/K is purely inseparable field extension of exponent 2 which is not modular. Sweedler [10] (1968) and Gerstenhaber and Zaromp [5] (1970) produced an analogue of the Galois Theory for modular extensions, where restricted Lie algebras of derivati-
ons are replaced by higher derivations, i.e. the Hasse-Schmidt derivations, [6] (1937). A survey of Galois theory for inseparable field extensions is given in the paper of Deveney and Mordeson [4] (1996). In [3], Bavula introduced B-extensions which are the most symmetrical finite field extensions (a finite field extension L/K is called a B-extension if the endomorphism algebra EndK(L) is generated by the algebra of differential operators D(L/K) on the K-algebra L and the automorphism group G(L/K)-
 := AutK−alg(L)) and obtained an analogue of the Galois Theory for B-extensions. Surprisingly, it turned out the class of B-extensions coincided with the class of normal finite field extensions giving a new characteristic property of them. As a result, an analogue of the Galois Theory is obtained for normal field extensions [3]. In particular, all Galois field extensions and all purely inseparable field extensions are B-extensions. Bavula’s approach is a ring theoretic (characteristic free) approa-
ch which is based on central simple algebras. In this approach, analogues of the Galois Correspondences (for subfields and normal subfields of L) are deduced from the Double Centralizer Theorem which is applied to the central simple algebra EndK(L) and subfields of B-extensions. Since Galois finite field extensions are B-extensions, this approach gives a new conceptual (short) proofs of the key results of the Galois Theory, see [2] for details. It also reveals that the ‘maximal symmetry’ (of norma-
l field extensions) is the essence of the classical Galois Theory and the analogue of the Galois Theory for normal field extensions. In the Galois theory, the two Galois Correspondences are given in terms of the field LG(L/K) of G(L/K)-invariants. But for normal field extensions, analogues of the two Galois Correspondences

As a particular case, we have a ‘differential’ Galois Theory for purely inseparable finite field extensions L/K where the algebra of differential operators D(L/K) plays the role of the Galois

Definition 2.1. ([3]) A finite field extension L/K is called a B-extension if
E(L/K) = D(L/K) ⋊ G(L/K).
‘B’ stands for ‘Bi’=‘2’, i.e. both fundamental objects that are attached to the field extension L/K, the algebra of differential operators D(L/K) and the automorphism group G(L/K), play a key role in the definition. By the definition, a field extension L/K is a B-extension iff the algebra D(L/K) ⋊ G(L/K) is as large as possible. So, B-extensions are the most symmetrical field extensions.
Definition 2.2. ([3]) A finite field extension L/K is called a G-extension if
E(L/K) = L ⋊ G(L/K).
A finite field extension L/K is called a D-extension if
E(L/K) = D(L/K).
Above, ‘G’ and ‘D’ stand for ‘the Galois group’ and ‘the algebra of differential operators’, respectively. By the definition, G- and D-extensions are B-extensions. There are many B-extensions that are neither G- nor D-extensions. They are precisely normal field extensions that are neither Galois nor purely inseparable field extensions (Corollary ??).
Proposition 2.3. ([3]) A finite field extension is a G-extension iff it is a Galois field extension.
Corollary 2.4. ([3]) A finite field extension is a D-extension iff it is a purely inseparable field extension.
Proposition 2.5. ([3]) A finite field extension L/K is separable iff D(L/K) = L.
So, intuitively, purely inseparable field extensions in prime characteristic are direct analogue of Galois field extensions in the separable situation where the algebras E(L/K) = D(L/K) and E(L/K) = L ⋊ G(L/K) are ‘swapped’, i.e. the skew group algebra L ⋊ G(L/K) is replaced by the algebras of differential operators D(L/K) (automorphisms are replaced by differential operators). The general situation for B-extensions (in prime characteristic), is a mixture of two cases: the Galois and the purely in-
separable ones.
