Abstract
The rapid diffusion of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has substantially lowered the costs of launching and developing digital ventures. GenAI can potentially both enable previously unviable entrepreneurial ideas by lowering resource needs and improve the performance of existing ventures. We explore how founders’ technical and managerial expertise shapes GenAI’s impact on digital ventures along these dimensions. Exploiting exogenous variation in GenAI usage across venture categories and-
 the timing of its broad availability for software tasks (e.g., GitHub Copilot’s public release and subsequent GenAI tools), we find that the number of new venture launches increased and the median time to launch decreased significantly more in categories with relatively high GenAI usage. GenAI’s effect on new launches is larger for founders without managerial experience or education, while its effect on venture capital (VC) funding likelihood is stronger for founders with technical experience or -
education. Overall, our results suggest that GenAI expands access to digital entrepreneurship for founders lacking managerial expertise and enhances venture performance among technical founders.

1 Introduction
In late 2022, the public launch of OpenAI’s ChatGPT made large language models (LLMs)
easy to access for common users. Since then, generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has
gained rapid adoption through an increasing variety of features, tools, and platforms built on
top of these LLMs. GenAI has the potential to transform knowledge work across a variety of
domains, due to its ability to generate text and images and perform cognitive and creative
tasks on par with humans. In knowledge work involving software coding, professionals have
especially embraced GenAI features such as code generation and code completion and have
increasingly integrated them into development workflows. It is estimated that by the end of
2024, about 10-30% of open-source Python code in North America, Europe, and Asia had
been generated by AI (Daniotti et al., 2025), and this share has continued to rise to date.
Meanwhile, drastic cost reductions enabled by LLMs in a variety of knowledge tasks have
opened up a window of market opportunities (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000; Ramoglou
and McMullen, 2024), captured by firms introducing new GenAI features and products to
serve the needs of different customer segments, many of which acquired users rapidly. Rapid
GenAI diffusion can potentially have large effects on how entrepreneurs build digital ventures,
for which software tasks are crucial to product development and customer acquisition.
While prior research shows that GenAI can increase individuals’ efficiency in completing
standalone tasks (Peng et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025), less is known about its broader impact
on organizational outcomes in real-world professional settings, where GenAI capabilities and
human expertise are combined to drive strategic decisions and implement work tasks. In
particular, founders’ technical and managerial expertis – both crucial forms of human capi
tal for digital ventures (Schulz et al., 2025) – may shape the impact of GenAI adoption on
venture outcomes. The consequences of GenAI usage in digital venture development may be
particularly salient, given the widespread adoption and rapid progress of GenAI capabilities,
especially for conducting software tasks. While GenAI’s wide adoption may change the role
of different types of human expertise in contributing to organizational activities, empirical
2

evidence on how these shifts occur remains limited. This paper addresses these research
gaps by exploring the following research questions: How does GenAI affect access to en
trepreneurship and subsequent venture performance across venture categories? Can different
types of founder expertise, especially technical and managerial expertise, become sources of
competitive advantage for digital ventures using GenAI?
The immediate consequence of GenAI-driven automation is a reduction in the resource
needs and financial costs of entrepreneurial activities that involve software development tasks.
This is driven not just by a single tool but by a variety of LLMs and GenAI coding features
built on top of these LLMs, with different interface designs that suit the needs of developers
from diverse backgrounds. GenAI may affect digital entrepreneurship through two channels:
(1) reducing the resource needs and increasing the economic viability of implementing poten
tial venture ideas; and (2) enhancing the productivity and accelerating product development
and user acquisition for current ventures.
The effects of GenAI usage are likely uneven across existing expertise in the founding
team, since humans operate GenAI to co-create solutions in many different ways. Ventures’
ability to sustain a competitive advantage from GenAI usage may depend on human-AI
collaboration, especially on founders’ ability to engage with AI systems effectively (Csaszar,
2025; Hasan et al., 2025). On the one hand, GenAI may enable founders with technical
expertise to delegate lower-level tasks to GenAI and focus on higher-level and architectural
tasks that require specialist domain knowledge (Cui et al., 2025). Since GenAI features for
coding are largely trained on publicly available software code, they perform much more reli
ably in retrieving and recombining shallow knowledge than deep domain knowledge. Hence,
GenAI capabilities may complement experienced developers and technical experts in per
forming complex engineering tasks, who may especially gain from GenAI by accelerating
their workflows. On the other hand, GenAI capabilities may mimic and more easily replace
managerial expertise, which is essential to running a digital venture. GenAI has been shown
to bridge the distance between different domains and blend knowledge from across fields
3

when coming up with solutions (Dell’Acqua et al., 2025). This capability mirrors how man
agers synthesize information across functions to make strategic decisions, which requires a
generalist perspective and a holistic understanding of different domains, but not a deep un
derstanding of each specific domain. Therefore, we propose that founders’ lack of managerial
expertise should be associated with larger effects of GenAI on viability, while their technical
expertise should be associated with larger effects of GenAI on venture performance.
To conduct empirical analyses, we use data from Product Hunt, a large online platform
where entrepreneurial teams launch digital products to acquire early users and test market
traction. Identifying each venture by its unique website domain, we collect data on all
first-time public launches of new ventures, including information about the founding team,
the product itself, and initial user traction on the platform. We augment these data by
obtaining information about the underlying venture from PitchBook and the primary founder
from Revelio. Our identification approach leverages the exogenous variation in the extent
to which GenAI usage is prevalent across venture categories. By defining a dichotomous
indicator of high (versus low) GenAI usage at the category level, we use a difference-in
differences regression model to estimate the impact of GenAI on entrepreneurial access and
venture performance in two sets of empirical analyses. In each set of analyses, we explore the
moderating effects of the technical and managerial expertise of the primary founder, using
separate measures constructed from prior work experience and education.
The first set of empirical analyses shows that 42% more initial venture launches take
place in treated (relative to control) categories, driven by reduced product development
time and lower financial costs that improve the viability of venture ideas. Though these
results are broadly present across founders from a variety of demographic groups, younger
and college-educated founders appear to respond disproportionately to GenAI availability,
consistent with recent empirical evidence that these demographic groups also show overall
higher GenAI adoption across populations (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025; Bick et al., 2025;
Daniotti et al., 2025). We find that the treatment effects on launches by founders without
4

managerial expertise (i.e., having worked in a managerial occupation in their last job, or
having a master’s or higher degree in business-related fields) were twice as high as those on
launches by founders with managerial expertise, and the differences between the coefficient
estimates are significantly significant at the 1% level using both measures of managerial
expertise. In contrast, we do not find founders’ technical expertise (i.e., having worked in
a computer occupation in their last job, or having a bachelor’s or higher degree in STEM
fields) to contribute to significantly different treatment effects between ventures.
The second set of empirical analyses shows that GenAI has a close-to-zero and statis
tically insignificant effect on ventures’ likelihood of raising venture capital (VC) funding 
an important milestone that indicates the venture’s scaling potential – after accounting for
venture and founding team characteristics as well as launch year-quarter fixed effects. How
ever, the primary founder’s technical experience has a large positive moderating effect, which
indicates that ventures experience improved chances of raising VC funding due to GenAI
exposure if their founders come from technical and non-managerial backgrounds. Similarly,
we also find a significantly positive moderating effect of founders’ higher education degrees
in a STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) field, but no significant
moderating effect of an MBA or similar advanced degree in a business field.
Our theoretical and empirical results broadly suggest that GenAI usage contributes to
both improved access to entrepreneurship and the potential for enhancing venture perfor
mance. However, founders’ technical and managerial expertise constitute boundary con
ditions for when new digital ventures may gain from GenAI usage. These findings add
to the nascent literature on the impact of generative AI on entrepreneurship (Bao et al.,
2025; Asam and Heller, 2025; Otis et al., 2024), by clarifying access and performance as
two distinct mechanisms through which GenAI channels efficiency gains for potential digital
entrepreneurs. We examine how founders’ technical and managerial expertise – conceptual
ized from the perspective of specialist and generalist knowledge (Kaplan and Vakili, 2015;
Teodoridis et al., 2019) that vary in their susceptibility to complementarity or substitution
5

by GenAI – may determine whether digital ventures can derive a competitive advantage
from GenAI usage (Csaszar, 2025). Finally, we contribute to recent research on how GenAI
changes the productivity of performing knowledge tasks (Peng et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025;
Brynjolfsson et al., 2025b), by analyzing changes in digital ventures’ viability and perfor
mance due to a wide range of GenAI tools as well as practices for incorporating them in
the entrepreneurial process, which go beyond the effects of a single product (e.g., GitHub
Copilot) at the individual level for performing a fixed task.
2 Theory and Background
2.1 Generative AI and Digital Venture Development
Advances in artificial intelligence technologies have disrupted market equilibrium and in
troduced new entrepreneurial opportunities (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000; Ramoglou and
McMullen, 2024). While previous AI technologies excelled at pattern recognition and predic
tion (Agrawal et al., 2022), recent advances in deep learning have unleashed even more pow
erful capabilities that mimic humans’ ability to understand text and images (Grimes et al.,
2023). Powered by the transformer architecture (Holmstro ̈m and Carroll, 2024; Vaswani
et al., 2017) and trained using massive computing power on vast amounts of high-quality
data, large language models (LLMs) have marked a significant breakthrough in the field of
AI (Eloundou et al., 2024). GenAI systems built on top of large language models (LLMs)
can respond to users’ prompts in real time and generate high-quality text, images, and audio.
Generative AI is considered a general-purpose technology (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg,
1995; Goldfarb et al., 2023) due to its broad applicability and impact on many sectors. The
adoption of LLMs and GenAI systems has spread more rapidly than that of the personal
computer and the Internet (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025; Bick et al., 2025). For example,
ChatGPT accumulated over one million users within five days of its first release in November
2022 (Holmstro ̈m and Carroll, 2024), and currently has over 6 billion monthly visits and over
700 million active weekly users as of mid-2025. Not only can GenAI systems automate mun
6

dane tasks, but they also demonstrate superior capabilities in carrying out certain complex
knowledge tasks that demonstrate human-level intelligence and creativity. Due to GenAI’s
superior performance relative to humans in precision, speed, and consistency, it may raise
the productivity of knowledge workers across a variety of professional tasks, including cus
tomer service (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025b), management consulting (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023),
writing (Noy and Zhang, 2023), and software development (Peng et al., 2023; Cui et al.,
2025).
Software development has been among the earliest and swiftest fields to adopt GenAI, as
the industry embraced its capabilities to generate high-quality software code (e.g., Claude
Sonnet 3.5, GPT-4.1) in response to natural language prompts. Since mid-2022, a variety of
GenAI coding agents (e.g., GitHub Copilot, Cursor) and GenAI-powered low-code platforms
(e.g., Replit, Lovable) have emerged to serve the specialized needs of software development
tasks across diverse contexts. By early 2025, GitHub Copilot had reached over 20 million
users worldwide. As the founder of Y Combinator and venture capital investor Paul Graham
put it: “A friend in the AI business estimated that the price/performance of AI had decreased
by about 100x in each of the past 2 years. 10,000x in 2 years. I don’t think any technology
has improved so fast in my lifetime... I ask all the software companies I meet about this
(the percentage of code that’s now written by AI). The number is rarely lower than 40%.
For some young programmers it’s 90%.” According to research that detects AI-generated
Python functions in open-source code, the amount of AI-generated code spiked following the
release of ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot, and has continued to rise over time, reaching close
to 30% of open-source Python code in the United States by the end of 2024 (Daniotti et al.,
2025). As GenAI reaches widespread adoption among software developers performing real
work, it may fundamentally transform how developers handle software tasks, and eventually
the digital products and services built on software.
The digital sector’s quest to automate software development workflows long predated the
rise of GenAI. Since the early 2010s, a variety of low-code tools that let users build web apps
7

through “drag-and-drop” have emerged and gained popularity among digital entrepreneurs.
A notable example is Shopify, which provides a suite of low-code solutions that enable e
commerce entrepreneurs to create digital storefronts and operate their ventures while writing
little to no software code themselves (Dushnitsky and Stroube, 2021). However, these tools
have several limitations, especially for experienced technical users, due to their lack of cus
tomization and flexibility (Bru ̈hl et al., 2023). They only allow users to deploy pre-built
templates and workflows, without modifying the underlying code to implement specific fea
tures or business logic. Moreover, users of these tools can neither inspect nor own the source
code, nor migrate the codebase out of the low-code platform for further development.
In contrast, GenAI overcomes these limitations of traditional low-code tools because of
its capabilities to adapt outputs (i.e., software code) in response to users’ prompts in real
time. Users can exert full control over the codebase and modify the code to suit their specific
needs using natural language prompts, which facilitates debugging, scaling, and integration
much more conveniently than traditional low-code tools. It allows not only non-technical
users to generate a codebase without writing code, but also technical founders to speed up
software development workflows. Hence, GenAI may both reduce the need for new digital
ventures to hire dedicated software developers and allow technical founders to build, test,
and pivot their ideas faster at the same time.
Among the earliest GenAI-powered coding tools is GitHub Copilot, which was publicly
released in late June 2022 (after a one-year technical preview), and became widely adopted
soon after. GitHub Copilot directly embeds GenAI in the software development environment,
serving as a “pair programmer” with code generation and code completion functionalities.
Shortly afterward, in September 2022, Replit – a cloud-based coding platform that enables
developers to create entire web apps in the browser – launched Replit Ghostwriter with AI
coding functionalities highly similar to GitHub Copilot. Both GitHub Copilot and Replit
became widely popular with growing user bases that exceeded 30 million worldwide in early
2025, and they are far from the only GenAI software companies with large customer bases.

Since 2023, a variety of GenAI coding assistants and platforms have been launched, tailored
to different software expertise levels (e.g., non-coder vs. software engineer), development life
cycle stages (e.g., prototyping vs. full launch), and software deployment environments (e.g.,
local vs. cloud). For example, GenAI-native coding editors such as Cursor and Codeium
(later rebranded as Windsurf) target experienced software developers as their core market
segment, directly embedding code generation and suggestion capabilities into the local in
tegrated development environment. On the other hand, GenAI-powered low-code platforms
like Replit and Lovable target less technical developers, allowing users to generate entire
codebases for full-stack web apps from scratch in their browsers by describing the features
they want without the need to write any code. These tools commonly rely on application
programming interface (API) calls to large language models (LLMs) that excel at generating
software code, such as Claude Sonnet 3.5 and GPT-4.1. Many of these AI coding startups
rapidly gained market traction by making software development more accessible and more
efficient across a variety of expertise levels and industry contexts. The market for GenAI
coding tools is currently in rapid evolution, as the rising demand drives the creation of new
tools by innovative startups entering the market to meet underserved needs across different
market niches. Appendix Table A1 lists all the major launches of GenAI coding platforms,
tools, and features powered by LLMs up to the end of 2024.
Instead of being locked into one tool, digital entrepreneurs often combine a variety of
GenAI tools or features for different purposes at various stages of building their ventures.
Low-code GenAI platforms are useful for initial prototyping and quick experimentation,
where entrepreneurs favor the speed of co-creating functional websites and prompting ad
ditional features with these tools, aimed at quickly launching minimum viable products
(MVPs) to gauge potential market demand. However, the rapid code generation through
these platforms can be a double-edged sword: technical debt accumulates at the same time
as rapid generation of product features, which can severely constrain maintainability as ini
tially simple prototypes grow into highly complex codebases. While entrepreneurs may begin
9

product development on low-code platforms, they often need to migrate the codebase else
where after hitting capability limits, when the costs of modifying generated code or correcting
technical errors outweigh the benefits of rapid codebase generation. As the venture grows,
entrepreneurs may increasingly prioritize the ability to customize and own the codebase, and
thus transition to using integrated development environments such as Visual Studio Code
and Cursor (which can natively embed GenAI coding features) to continue developing lo
cally. This often occurs when the venture faces scaling constraints, when the rapid growth of
the userbase makes it necessary to optimize website performance, which requires solid engi
neering practices (e.g., refactoring the codebase) controlled by human developers. Another
trigger for migrating the codebase out of the initial low-code platform or launchpad is often
related to security or compliance requirements, when entrepreneurs need more control over
their infrastructure, e.g., storing data in a private database to ensure security and owning
the development environment instead of relying on a public cloud and third-party platforms.
2.2 Impact of Generative AI on Entrepreneurial Access and Performance
As GenAI speeds up software development workflows, it can both lower costs and improve
productivity in carrying out entrepreneurial tasks. By reducing resource requirements for
product development, GenAI can improve the feasibility of business ideas that were previ
ously too costly to implement and expand entrepreneurial access to founders with lower fi
nancial resources. For example, ventures targeting niche markets may now be viable, whereas
previously the cost-benefit calculus may have discouraged entrepreneurs due to the antic
ipated difficulty of achieving break-even profitability. On the other hand, GenAI can also
improve venture performance by increasing efficiency in conducting various software tasks
throughout the entrepreneurial process. For example, GenAI may enhance venture quality
through faster user feedback loops and more iterative product development and experimen
tation.
However, the wide availability of GenAI may erode its potential to provide a sustainable
source of competitive advantage for entrepreneurial firms, because the technology itself is
10

cheap to access and easy to acquire across the board (Csaszar, 2025; Barney, 2001). Of
ten, a human in the loop is required for co-creating solutions with GenAI across various
tasks in venture development, and human judgment remains crucial for the performance
of the human-AI system overall (Raisch and Fomina, 2025). For example, developers need
to decide what prompt to enter as input for GenAI coding tools and how to edit the soft
ware code output by these tools. Hence, founders’ expertise in various domains may lead
to heterogeneous ways of incorporating GenAI into the entrepreneurial process, some more
effective than others at improving venture outcomes. The resource-based view explains a
firm’s competitive advantage through its internal capabilities and resources, rather than ex
ternal conditions such as widespread GenAI adoption. Firms with complementary resources,
such as skilled workers who understand how to operate the technology to co-create solutions,
are more effective at sustaining their competitive advantage from embracing technological
innovation (Tambe, 2014; Wu et al., 2020; Goldfarb et al., 2023).
GenAI may lead to differential gains for ventures, depending on both the founders’ ex
isting expertise and GenAI’s uneven capabilities in conducting knowledge work. GenAI
may automate cognitively tedious but intellectually mundane tasks (Lichtinger and Hos
seini, 2025), similarly to how technological innovations have historically replaced workers
performing routine, codifiable tasks while augmenting those working on more complex tasks
(Autor et al., 2003). While LLMs demonstrate surface-level understanding of a vast range
of knowledge domains, they lack deep understanding of domain-specific knowledge and the
complex reasoning required for most knowledge tasks beyond the entry level. Recent empir
ical evidence shows that companies have slowed the hiring of junior roles, but senior roles
are unaffected (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025a; Lichtinger and Hosseini, 2025), consistent with
the strengths and limitations of GenAI in relation to different real-world knowledge tasks.
LLMs that power GenAI coding tools are trained on publicly available software code,
which tends to embody generic and shallow expertise that is broadly accessible and lags be
hind frontier practice. Knowledge is much more accessible to the public at a lower level, thus
11

providing LLMs with more training data to replicate the expertise related to such knowl
edge and replace humans with similar expertise. LLMs may make it easier and less costly to
access and combine relatively shallow knowledge across vastly different domains. Similar to
data analytics (Wu et al., 2020), which broadens the information search space across exist
ing knowledge, GenAI can also quickly grasp a vast amount of data across a wide range of
domains to retrieve and combine knowledge from these domains more rapidly than humans.
However, LLMs lack depth in local search and face a disadvantage relative to domain experts
in acquiring domain-specific knowledge required for non-incremental innovation. Hence, the
expertise of specialists (Kaplan and Vakili, 2015; Teodoridis et al., 2019) who possess rarer
domain knowledge may be less substitutable by GenAI compared to the expertise of gener
alists who possess shallow knowledge across a larger number of domains. Specialists’ deep
domain expertise is less likely to be replicated by GenAI, and their ability to innovate and
learn at the frontier of a knowledge domain, which has not yet become codified or entered
training data, makes them more likely to sustain such an advantage over LLMs over time.
Hence, GenAI is more likely to replace human expertise relevant for performing generalist
tasks involving the combination of shallow knowledge from broader domains, and to com
plement human expertise relevant for performing specialist tasks involving domain-specific
and relatively rare knowledge crucial for innovation.
In digital ventures, founders with different types of expertise using GenAI may yield
systematically different gains from GenAI. Founding teams often have co-founders with
complementary skills and combine their distinct expertise as technical and business co
founders. However, GenAI may shift the balance between technical and managerial expertise
in performing key activities throughout the entrepreneurial process of an early-stage ven
ture. Technical expertise is often specialist and focused on a narrow domain with greater
depth (Ferreira and Sah, 2012), whereas managerial expertise is often generalist and requires
spanning the boundaries of domains and effectively combining information from across these
diverse domains to make strategic decisions that require relatively shallow understanding of
12

each domain (Grant, 1996). Since access to shallow knowledge across diverse domains has
become less costly due to GenAI, managerial expertise may become more easily available
and thus less valuable. On the other hand, technical expertise may become more valuable
because it cannot be readily replaced by GenAI and embodies deep knowledge that enables
individuals to keep up with the rapid pace of innovation within the domain (Teodoridis
et al., 2019). Therefore, both entrepreneurial access and performance gains from GenAI
for coding may be larger among those potential founders with more technical expertise and
less managerial expertise. While the former can more easily co-create effective outputs with
GenAI, the latter face lower barriers to acquiring necessary business knowledge to operate
their ventures.
2.3 Measuring Digital Venture Launches: Product Hunt
To examine how GenAI affects the launch and performance of nascent digital ventures, we
collect large-scale data from Product Hunt, a global online platform where startups conduct
initial public launches to acquire early adopters and gain market traction. Product Hunt was
founded as an online community for surfacing new technology products, where individuals
share new discoveries of technology products on the platform. The platform soon became
popular among tech entrepreneurs and early adopters, and is frequently used by early-stage
ventures to conduct initial launches to gauge signals of market traction, acquire early users,
and validate their ideas (Cao et al., 2024). Currently, Product Hunt has about four million
monthly visits and half a million active users, many of whom participate on the platform
using their real names and professional profiles that can be linked to other data sources.
Product Hunt is an appropriate setting for the study because the impact of GenAI coding
on digital entrepreneurship is most salient at the early stage, where entrepreneurs have a
business idea and launch a working product or prototype to test the market. At this stage,
GenAI may alleviate resource barriers for potential entrepreneurs most effectively because it
helps speed up the development of the product itself. Instead of relying on popular databases
(such as CrunchBase or PitchBook) or government records of registered businesses, our
13

approach allows us to observe potential ventures at the beginning of the entrepreneurial
lifecycle, when they are about to launch with a concrete business idea, but possibly do not
yet have a formal organizational entity attached to it yet.
We collect rich data from the platform, including detailed information about ventures’
product launches and their founding teams (tagged as “makers” on the launch posts). Since
we focus on first-time launches close to a venture’s creation, we use the primary founder’s
information (tagged as the lead maker) to construct founder variables for the venture. For
each launch, we obtain the website domain (which uniquely identifies the underlying ven
ture1) and a single venture category associated with the initial launch. For the primary
founder, we observe their number of followers on Product Hunt, name, headline, and social
links, which allow us to match the individual with external data and obtain more information
about demographics, career history, and educational background.

3.1 Data
We collect comprehensive data from the Product Hunt platform on digital ventures’ first
time public launches and the founders of these ventures. We augment the data by matching
it to external databases on both ventures and founders, which are described as follows. We

and fields into standardized categories. Using individual-level job and education history, we

3.2 Sample and Descriptive Statistics
The empirical analyses focus on first launches of ventures during the time period from 2020Q1
to 2025Q2. Since the study focuses on early-stage digital ventures, we apply the following
criteria to ensure that the final data sample consists of new digital ventures that launched
their first working product publicly on Product Hunt. First, we remove launches without a
maker, since these are unlikely to be intentionally planned launches by the founders (and are
instead hunter-initiated discoveries of existing products). Second, we focus on entrepreneurial
product launches by removing podcast episodes, games, and books, as well as products
without standalone website domains that are complementors hosted on third-party platforms
(e.g., design templates, Amazon sellers). Third, we remove observations with more than 30
launch posts, because they are likely to be mature software companies launching many tools.
Fourth, we remove website domains created more than six years prior to their first launch on
15

Product Hunt, since they are unlikely to be early-stage ventures. Fifth, to ensure sufficient
information about the primary founder’s professional background is available, we retain only
ventures whose primary founder’s LinkedIn profile (obtained through Revelio) indicates at
least one company in their work experience. Finally, we restrict the sample to those ventures
where the primary founder’s location is in North America, Europe, or Asia, which together
account for 88% of the remaining ventures, because both LinkedIn and PitchBook coverage
of founders and ventures at the population level falls sharply outside these continents. See
the details of these sample selection criteria in Appendix Section B.
The final dataset contains 59,642 distinct launches, for which we consolidate information
about their underlying ventures and founders to derive key variables for empirical analyses.

3.3 Empirical Strategy
Our empirical analyses aim to uncover the treatment effects of GenAI usage for coding on
digital ventures. The ideal experiment for conducting such empirical analyses would involve
observing two otherwise identical potential entrepreneurs with the same business idea for
a digital venture, and granting one of them access to GenAI but not the other, and then
comparing their GenAI usage as well as outcomes such as launching and fundraising. This
hypothetical experiment would allow the researcher to estimate causal treatment effects
because any differences in outcomes would be entirely attributable to GenAI access, with all
other factors held constant.
However, such an ideal experiment that randomizes access to GenAI is not feasible in
our setting. Founders can choose from different GenAI coding tools, all of which are widely
accessible at relatively low costs. We do not observe which GenAI features potential en
trepreneurs use or how intensively they engage with these features. As low-code platforms

Instead, because the usage and value-add of GenAI are not equal across all types of
ventures, we can leverage exogenous variation in overall GenAI usage across product cate
gories to estimate causal effects using a difference-in-differences regression framework. We
take this approach because technology adoption is driven by perceived cost-benefit tradeoffs
(Hall, 2004), and thus GenAI adoption should be higher when ventures conduct activities
in which the use of GenAI raises efficiency to a larger extent, after accounting for financial
and cognitive costs of using GenAI. Therefore, despite being widely accessible at low costs,
GenAI capabilities are uneven across tasks and problem domains, and thus the extent to
which GenAI lowers resource needs or enhances the productivity of various development
activities may vary across venture characteristics. Hence, our empirical strategy relies on
exogenous differences in GenAI usage across venture categories, driven by varying degrees of
benefits from GenAI adoption.
We describe several reasons that may explain systematic variation in the degrees of GenAI
usage across venture categories as follows. First, different types of ventures involve different
levels of software development needs. For example, some ventures (e.g., consultancy firms)
may only need a landing page showing the address and contact information for customers to
reach them, while their primary activities are conducted offline. Some ventures may build
physical products and require advanced technologies beyond software coding, such as hard
ware, the Internet of Things (IoT), robotics, and augmented reality/virtual reality (AR/VR).
Second, ventures in certain categories entail more complex technical requirements than oth
17

ers and therefore face higher technical barriers to entry. For example, GenAI capabilities
are much more effective at handling front-end software tasks, for which publicly available
code is overrepresented in training data, compared to back-end tasks, which require com
plex reasoning about architecture design, data modeling, and business logic. In addition,
front-end tasks are also easier because the outputs are immediately visualized, and users can
fix the code by describing the changes they want to make to GenAI, whereas the quality of
the back-end code is much more hidden and harder to detect. Hence, GenAI alone is likely
insufficient for building products that require technically complex back-end logic, optimizing
performance when deploying to large-scale user bases, and implementing proper data secu
rity measures, or solving other complex engineering issues. Third, some types of ventures are
relatively “low-tech” (e.g., fashion e-commerce), where more founders may come from less
technical backgrounds and find low-code solutions more useful for building their offerings.
GenAI may lower resource barriers, especially for these entrepreneurs, and is therefore more
widely used to create prototypes and implement ideas by entrepreneurs in these “low-tech”
categories.

To examine the robustness of these analyses and the validity of our data, we estimate the
treatment effects separately for different types of ventures, based on demographic groups of
their primary founders at the time of launch. Recent research shows that GenAI adoption
has been uneven across demographic characteristics at the individual level (Humlum and
Vestergaard, 2025; Bick et al., 2025; Daniotti et al., 2025). It is reasonable to assume that
the effects of GenAI on first-time venture launches should be larger for founder demographic
groups that are shown to adopt GenAI at higher rates in the prior literature. Hence, we
construct different measures of first-time launches based on subsamples of ventures in which
the primary founders belong to different demographic groups (e.g., educational attainment,
age group, gender, and location). Different categorical values of a demographic characteristic
are associated with a separate outcome measure in the difference-in-differences regression
model in Equation 2, Section 3.4. We then compare coefficients across separate regressions
(with correlated error terms) using each of these outcome variables corresponding to a distinct
value of the demographic characteristic. We use the seemingly unrelated regression (SUR)
model, which combines separate regressions to perform joint estimation (Zellner, 1962), and
then conduct a Wald test on the null hypothesis that the resulting coefficient estimates are
equal across the two regressions. The resulting p-value of the Wald test statistic indicates
whether we can reject the null hypothesis of equality between the coefficient estimates from
these separate regressions.

To interpret these results, we draw on the knowledge perspective of GenAI’s capabilities
and its implications for venture development. Previous research on data analytics shows that
it facilitates knowledge recombination across domains (Wu et al., 2020), and recent research
also shows that GenAI may expand the search space for knowledge and lower the costs of
combining information across distant domains (Dell’Acqua et al., 2025). However, GenAI
produces outputs by retrieving and recombining shallow knowledge, because the software
27

code used to train large language models (LLMs) underrepresents highly complex or frontier
technical knowledge, which is less likely to be publicly available for training. Thus, GenAI
is more capable of generalist tasks than of specialist tasks, and therefore the former are
more prone to replacement by GenAI than the latter. Managerial roles are often generalist,
responsible for synthesizing relatively shallow expertise across a larger number of domains
to inform decision-making (Grant, 1996; Autor et al., 2003), whereas technical roles are
often specialist, involving new knowledge creation enabled by deep expertise in a narrower
set of domains without spanning boundaries (Ferreira and Sah, 2012). GenAI makes the
recombination of shallow knowledge less costly, thus substituting managerial expertise that
involves coordination across domains, while complementing technical expertise that involves
reasoning with deeper knowledge within a domain.
5 Discussion and Conclusion
This study explores how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) affects potential digital
ventures’ first-time launches and longer-run performance. We use the exogenous variation
in GenAI usage at the venture category level and the timing of the first cohort of GenAI
for coding to identify causal effects in a difference-in-differences regression framework. Com
bining Product Hunt data with several proprietary databases on ventures and founders, our
empirical analyses show that venture categories with high GenAI usage experienced signifi
cantly more first-time launches and shorter time to launch relative to venture categories with
low GenAI usage after GenAI coding became widely available. We also find that treatment
effects are larger among ventures with college-educated and young founders (and to a smaller
extent, male and US/Europe-based founders), consistent with recent research (e.g., Humlum
and Vestergaard 2025; Daniotti et al. 2025; Bick et al. 2025) showing that these demographic
groups have higher population-level GenAI adoption rates.
GenAI may affect digital ventures through two channels: (1) It may increase access to
entrepreneurship by lowering resource needs and thus increasing the economic feasibility
28

of certain business ideas; (2) It may enhance the productivity of the founding team in
performing venture development tasks and implementing their ideas. However, the extent to
which GenAI can be effectively leveraged through both channels varies across ventures. In
our analyses, we focus on the role of founders’ expertise in contributing to such heterogeneity,
and our empirical results provide evidence that GenAI may complement technical expertise
and substitute managerial expertise, since ventures where the primary founder has a technical
background or lacks a managerial background seem to benefit more from GenAI through both
access and productivity channels.
We interpret the results in light of GenAI’s relationship with humans performing general
ist versus specialist tasks. Since GenAI coding tools are trained on publicly available software
code and related information, they are more likely to capture shallow knowledge and auto
mate the lower-level expertise that is more easily understood by non-technical founders, and
less likely to replicate the deep domain expertise of technical founders. Echoing the findings
from prior research that data analytics supports innovation based on knowledge recombina
tion (Wu et al., 2020), GenAI can also broaden the information search space across existing
knowledge, and allow individuals to more effectively retrieve and combine diverse knowledge
across distant domains. To the extent that managerial professions often require a relatively
shallow generalist understanding of diverse domains, in order to gather information across
these domains and combine them to make strategic decisions, GenAI can perform managerial
tasks that involve coordinating across different knowledge domains with a relatively shallow
understanding of each. Our results are consistent with specialists being positioned to benefit
more from GenAI coding tools relative to generalists, due to their deeper understanding
of a fast-moving domain that enables more effective local search (Kaplan and Vakili, 2015;
Teodoridis et al., 2019), which cannot be replaced by current GenAI capabilities. We find
that GenAI adoption and gains are higher in ventures where founders have complementary
expertise, consistent with predictions from the theory of human capital complementarity in
explaining the diffusion of new information technologies (Aral et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2020).

5.1 Contribution to the literature
This study adds to several bodies of literature. First, we contribute to the increasing volume
of work on generative AI’s impact on entrepreneurship (Asam and Heller, 2025; Bao et al.,
2025; Otis et al., 2024). Our focus is on digital ventures, where software tasks are essential
for venture development activities. On the one hand, our work is related to the literature
on how information technologies lower resource barriers and increase entrepreneurial entry
(Ewens et al., 2018; Dushnitsky and Stroube, 2021). On the other hand, this paper also
relates to the literature that examines how information technologies improve organizational
performance when complementary human capital is present (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Aral
et al., 2012; Tambe, 2014). Relative to prior work, our theoretical framework combines
both channels, and our empirical results show that GenAI usage can both enhance access
to entrepreneurship by reducing resource needs for launching digital ventures and improve
venture performance at the same time, particularly when the founders have complementary
expertise.
Second, our study is relevant to research on founder expertise as a source of differentiation
among firms seeking to benefit from GenAI usage (Barney, 2001; Csaszar, 2025). Specifically,
such differentiation depends on the capabilities of GenAI itself. Frontier LLMs are strong in
retrieving and recombining knowledge from across a large number of domains, but are weak
at attaining or deriving deep domain knowledge required for technically complex problems
that often arise when operating digital ventures. Hence, GenAI may exhibit characteristics
more of a generalist than of a specialist, and thus automate managerial expertise that in
volves combining shallow knowledge from across diverse domains (Ferreira and Sah, 2012),
while complementing technical expertise that embodies deep or frontier knowledge within
a narrow domain (Teodoridis et al., 2019; Kaplan and Vakili, 2015). While GenAI may be
widely available to potential entrepreneurs at a relatively low cost, firms’ internal capabilities
may determine whether they can effectively exploit opportunities and derive a competitive
advantage from using the technology (Csaszar, 2025; Krakowski et al., 2023; Giustiziero
30

et al., 2023), with human capital playing an important role in shaping firms’ value creation
from information technologies (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Aral et al., 2012; Tambe, 2014; Wu
et al., 2020).
Third, our study speaks to how generative AI affects the way knowledge work is con
ducted within organizations and in professions such as software development (Peng et al.,
2023; Cui et al., 2025), management consulting (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023), customer service
(Brynjolfsson et al., 2025b), and writing (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Doshi et al., 2025). Recent
empirical studies have largely focused on the productivity effects of GenAI in performing a
single well-defined task in a controlled experimental setting. This study complements these
prior works by exploring the aggregate impact of GenAI on real-world digital ventures, where
software tasks are essential for product development and operational activities. Our empir
ical findings suggest that GenAI may not only improve efficiency in performing standalone
tasks but also change the role of expertise and the configuration of activities performed by
humans who use GenAI, thereby shaping the overall effects of GenAI at the organizational
level.
5.2 Managerial implications
Our study offers several insights for potential founders and joiners of digital ventures on how
to adapt to rapid changes in technology affordances due to the rise of generative AI. We find
that founders with technical expertise and without managerial expertise derive greater bene
fits from GenAI coding tools, at least within the first few years since Generative Pre-trained
Transformer (GPTs) became widely accessible. While GenAI adoption for software-related
tasks may not necessarily lead to superior performance or competitive advantage at the or
ganizational level, our findings reveal substantial patterns of complementarity between the
existing expertise of founders and GenAI coding tools for digital entrepreneurship. To more
effectively use GenAI, entrepreneurs should consider the technology’s uneven capabilities
across both the breadth and depth of different knowledge domains, and navigate complex
relationships between GenAI and different types of expertise to co-create solutions. Potential
31

founders should consider not only how GenAI fills skill gaps in prototyping a working website
but also evaluate the prospects of incorporating GenAI more effectively into their workflows
at different stages of venture development. Hence, they should pay attention not only to how
GenAI relieves resource constraints but also recognize the need to develop complementary
capabilities to derive larger gains from GenAI usage.
In addition, technology diffusion can substantially reshape the direction of innovation.
Our findings imply that the widespread adoption of GenAI can sharply increase the entry of
new firms into market areas more exposed to the technology compared to other areas. Despite
benefiting from accelerated product development and efficiency gains, digital ventures may
also experience fiercer competition for market share due to industry-wide GenAI adoption.
Widespread GenAI usage may not only result in disproportionate entry of new products and
services in some market areas but also lead to stronger competition for incumbents in these
areas due to lower entry barriers. Hence, both entrepreneurs and incumbent firms in these
market areas should be aware of the changing competitive landscape and the demand-side
implications of GenAI, and make informed decisions that take into account these market-level
changes resulting from the widespread adoption of the technology by competing firms. In
response, they should foster a deeper understanding of the technology’s evolving capabilities
and develop complementary capabilities that allow them to co-create solutions with the
technology more effectively than their competitors.
5.3 Limitations and future research
This study has several limitations that open up avenues for future research. One limitation is
that state-of-the-art GenAI capabilities advance rapidly. While GenAI in its early days may
lack the ability to retrieve deeper domain knowledge or perform more complex technical
tasks, these caveats are also opportunities for AI startups to exploit and address unmet
needs across different customer niches, introducing new products and services that fill market
gaps (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000). For example, while there were no GenAI-powered
mobile app builders in 2023, Vibecode and Rork launched in late 2025, both enabling the
32

creation of fully deployable mobile apps through GenAI prompting. Moreover, future GenAI
systems for knowledge work may involve a large number of specialized LLMs trained on
domain-specific data to tackle more complex tasks, instead of a general-purpose LLM with
continuously improving capabilities. These specialized LLMs may be able to access not only
publicly available knowledge but also proprietary data that incorporate deep domain-specific
knowledge, and thus embody increasingly advanced expertise. Thus, future research should
address the dynamic nature of GenAI capabilities and study how organizations position
themselves to proactively manage or reactively adapt to GenAI as an evolving technology
over time.
Another limitation of this study is that we did not separately measure the impact of
GenAI on different stages of venture development, such as idea generation, prototyping and
testing, marketing and customer acquisition, after-sales services, and internal operations.
In addition, our results focus on founders as individuals to analyze the role of distinct
types of expertise, and do not account for interactions between team members who may use
GenAI individually or jointly. Therefore, future research can explore dynamic interpersonal
and intertemporal dependencies in shaping how GenAI capabilities are incorporated into
venture development. More work remains to be done to understand how teams coordinate
across different GenAI systems and address limitations of these systems, how GenAI affects
entrepreneurial decisions in different operational areas at different stages, and how path
dependency in these decisions shapes GenAI usage in downstream tasks.

B Sampling Criteria and Final Data
Our final dataset is constructed by matching all launch posts on Product Hunt with makers’ LinkedIn career histories using the Revelio database. The Data Appendix4 contains the details of the dataset construction, including how we collected information about launches and makers from Product Hunt, and how we identified the probable matches of the makers to their LinkedIn profiles. In particular, we clarify how our data construction recovers the information hierarchy of the Product Hunt structure by-
 tracing launch posts (PostId ) to products (ProductId ) and then to the parent organization (websiteDomain5). This process leaves about 106,757 firms in the sample period (January 2021 to mid-2025) in the final dataset.
1. Fixing the unit of analysis at the venture level. Our primary unit of analysis is a venture rather than a product or launch post, as our analyses focus on entrepreneurial firms and their founders rather than product lines within established companies. We to focus on ventures also because more information is available at the organizational level by matching each venture’s website domain to external databases containing outcomes such as funding (e.g., PitchBook).
2. Removing non-launch posts (with no makers tagged). To focus on firms’ intended public launches rather than hunter-initiated product discoveries, we keep only launch posts that tag at least one maker. Posts without tagged makers are not firminitiated but externally hunted, typically by unaffiliated users discovering and posting a product after it has already been widely adopted.
3. Pruning the data to focus on entrepreneurial firms. Because the raw data come from an open platform that accepts a wide range of posts with few restrictions, we prune the dataset to include only entrepreneurial firms in our empirical sample. Starting from all Product Hunt launch posts linked to their associated products and parent organizations, we filter the data to retain only launches that meet the criteria for entrepreneurial ventures. The process for constructing the final sample is outlin-
ed as follows:
• Remove podcast episodes, games, and books. This step removes all launch posts categorized as podcast episodes, games, or books.
• Remove content hosted on platforms without standalone domains.6 This step removes launch posts by platform complementors, including but not limited to design templates, OpenAI marketplace chatbots, Amazon or Etsy shops, and low-code apps using an AI coding platform’s domain.

C Description of Variables in Empirical Analyses
We describe the key variables summarized in Table 1 and define them as follows. Some variables are based on the primary founder, identified as the lead maker tagged in the venture’s first public launch post on Product Hunt. Venture Category. Product Hunt assigns each product launch to the category that best matches its type. The venture category variable reflects the category assigned to the venture’s first launch on Product Hunt. As described in the main paper, we restrict the sample to large cat-
egories with at least 100 launches between January 2021 and June 2025. Each venture in the final sample belongs to one of 85 distinct categories. Time to Launch (Months). This variable measures the number of months between the website’s creation (see the Data Appendix10 for measurement details) and the venture’s first public launch on Product Hunt. It serves as a proxy for the time required to develop the initial product from the start of business idea implementation. Raised VC Funding. This binar-
y variable indicates whether a venture raised venture capital (VC) funding – including seed, early-stage, and late-stage rounds, but excluding accelerator participation, loans, crowdfunding, and grants – by the end of the sample period (2025Q3). It is constructed by merging Product Hunt and PitchBook data using the venture’s website domain. Founding Team Size. This variable counts the number of “makers” listed in the Product Hunt post for a venture’s first public launch. It captures the founding t-
eam size, as the “makers” represent the core members behind the venture’s initial public product launch. Primary Founder Number of Followers on Product Hunt. This variable records the number of users following the primary founder’s Product Hunt account as of mid-2025. It serves as a proxy for the founder’s clout or influence in attracting customers to the venture. Primary Founder Educational Attainment. Three educational attainment indicators – High School, Bachelor’s, and Master’s or Above – are -
constructed based on the highest degree earned by the lead “maker” in the venture’s first Product Hunt launch. Education data come from Revelio: High School includes individuals whose highest degree is “Associate” or “High School”; Bachelor’s includes those with a “Bachelor” degree; and Master’s or Above includes those with “Master,” “MBA,” or “Doctor” degrees.
