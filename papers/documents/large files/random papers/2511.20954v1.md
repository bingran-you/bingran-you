δ-CORE SUBSAMPLING, STRONG COLLAPSES AND TDA
ELI ́AS GABRIEL MINIAN
Abstract. We introduce a subsampling method for topological data analysis based on strong collapses of simplicial complexes. Given a point cloud and a scale parameter δ, we construct a subsampling that preserves both global and local topological features while significantly reducing computational complexity of persistent homology calculations. We illustrate the effectiveness of our approach through experiments on synthetic and real datasets, showing improved persistence approximations compared to -
other subsampling techniques.
1. Introduction
Topological Data Analysis (TDA) applies methods and tools from algebraic topology to analyze datasets. One of the most widely used techniques in this area is persistent homology, which aims to infer the homology groups of an underlying (unknown) space from a sample X by examining the data at all possible scales [6, 8, 9, 10, 14, 15, 24, 25]. Currently, TDA and persistent homology have found diverse applications in many scientific fields including medicine, biology, engineering, physics, chemistry,-
 and industry [6, 15, 16]. One important consideration is the computational complexity of persistent homology: the standard matrix reduction algorithm has complexity O(s3), where s is the number of simplices in the Vietoris-Rips filtration [8, 2, 13]. When analyzing a dataset of several thousand points, the number of simplices in the filtration used for computing persistent homology can reach several million if we wish, for example, to compute homology up to degree 2. It is therefore necessary to -
have effective subsampling methods that preserve the topology of the data. Some known methods exist in this direction, such as farthest point sampling (FPS) [7], landmark selection with witness complexes [5], graph sparsification methods [17] and, more recently, adaptive approximation techniques [12] and outlier-robust subsampling techniques [18]. In this article we propose a new subsampling method based on the concept of strong collapse, a reduction technique in the context of simplicial complexe-
s that we introduced some years ago in collaboration with J. Barmak [1]. Strong collapses allow the study of the topology of polyhedra through minimal subpolyhedra (the cores) that preserve the homotopy type of the original complex. A strong collapse removes a dominated vertex from the simplicial complex that is, in some sense, redundant, along with all simplices containing it (see Section 2 below). The core of a simplicial complex is obtained by iteratively removing dominated vertices until reach-
ing a space with no dominated vertices. This space preserves the (strong) homotopy type of the original space but generally contains far fewer simplices. In the particular case of Vietoris-Rips complexes (used for persistent

A problem that arises when attempting to replace VR(X, ε) with its core is that an inclusion of subcomplexes does not induce an inclusion of their cores. As a simple example, consider the complex K consisting of a filled rectangle obtained by gluing two triangles along one of their edges, and let L be the boundary of the rectangle. Clearly, the core of L is L itself, whereas the core of K is a point. Therefore, if we apply the core construction to every complex in a filtration, we do not obtain a -
filtration. To circumvent this problem, Boissonnat and Pritam [4] defined a construction that turns the tower of cores into a filtration. But this also has a computational cost, so they approximate the PH by taking cores at some scale values (see [4]). Other applications in data analysis based on strong collapses can be found in [20, 21, 22]. Here we propose a different approach: we use the notion of strong collapse to define a subsampling technique. The advantage of this subsampling method over o-
ther alternatives is that it preserves both the global topology of the sample and its local structure: the

underlying idea is to remove redundant points that are dominated by other points. This technique adapts equally well to samples with high concentration as to more sparse or heterogeneous samples (regions with varying density). The method has the following key properties: if a point x is δ-dominated by a point y, then every δ-neighbor of x is also a neighbor of y, ensuring preservation of local information. The reduction is adaptive to density since, by construction, it removes many points in dense-
 regions while preserving structure in sparse regions. We will see that, given δ > 0, the δ-core is unique up to equivalence (see Section 4 below). In particular, any two δ-cores (for the same parameter δ) have the same number of points and share similar structures. The complexity of the construction algorithm is O(n log n + nk ̄) for computing neighborhoods using spatial data structures in low-dimensional Euclidean spaces, plus O(nk2max) per iteration for removing dominated
vertices, where n is the number of points, k ̄ is the average δ-degree and kmax is the maximum δ-degree. In practice, the empirical running time is close to O(n log n) for geometric point clouds in low dimensions. This preprocessing step substantially reduces the number of simplices in the Vietoris-Rips complex—often by millions when computing homology in degree greater than 1 for datasets with more than 1000–2000 points—thereby enabling significantly more efficient persistent homology computation-
. The construction algorithm and its pseudocode can be found in Section 4. In Section 5, we present experimental results on both synthetic and real datasets, comparing the δ-core method with the original sample and with other established subsampling techniques. 2. Strong collapses and cores of simplicial complexes
We recall first the basic constructions and results on strong collapses of simplicial complexes. For more details on strong homotopy types, including the relationship between cores and nerves of complexes, and applications to topology, combinatorics and the Evasiveness conjecture, the reader may consult [1]. All the simplicial complexes that we deal with are assumed to be finite. Let K be a simplicial complex and let σ be a simplex in K. Recall that the closed star of σ, denoted by st(σ), is the s-
ubcomplex of simplices τ such that τ ∪ σ is a simplex of K. The link lk(σ) is the subcomplex of st(σ) of simplices disjoint from σ. We denote by K ∖ v the deletion of the vertex v, which is the full subcomplex of K spanned by the vertices different from v. Note that K ∖v is obtained from K by removing all the simplices containing v.
Definition 2.1. Let K be a simplicial complex and let v ∈ K be a vertex. We say that there is an elementary strong collapse from K to K ∖ v if lk(v) is a simplicial cone v′L with apex v′. In this case we say that v is dominated (by v′) and we denote K ↘↘e K ∖ v.
Remark 2.2. Note that a vertex v is dominated by a vertex v′ ̸= v if and only if every maximal simplex that contains v also contains v′.
Figure 1 shows an elementary strong collapse. The vertex v is dominated by v′. We say that there is a strong collapse from a simplicial complex K to a subcomplex L if there exists a sequence of elementary strong collapses that starts in K and ends in L. We write K ↘↘ L.

Remark 2.3. If v is dominated by v′, the map r : K → K ∖ v which is the identity in K ∖ v and such that r(v) = v′ is a simplicial map. Moreover, it induces a strong deformation retract.
Proposition 2.4. If K ↘↘ L then K simplicially collapses to L and, in particular, the inclusion i : L ,→ K is a strong deformation retract. A simplicial retraction r : K → L which is a homotopy inverse to i is defined by iterating the retraction maps of the previous remark.
We say that a simplicial complex K is minimal if it has no dominated vertices. A core of a simplicial complex K is a minimal subcomplex L ⊆ K. Note that one can obtain a core of K by removing dominated vertices one by one. The following result, proved in [1], asserts that the ordering in which we remove the dominated vertices is irrelevant since the core is unique up to isomorphism.
Theorem 2.5. Every simplicial complex has a core and it is unique up to isomorphism.
Cores of flag complexes. The most commonly used simplicial complexes in persistent homology are Vietoris-Rips complexes. Given a point cloud (finite metric space) (X, d) the Vietoris-Rips complex for a parameter ε > 0 is the simplicial complex VR(X, ε) with vertex set X and whose simplices are the finite sets σ = {x0, . . . , xr} such that d(xi, xj) ≤ ε for all 0 ≤ i, j ≤ r. By construction, Vietoris-Rips complexes are flag complexes. Recall that a simplicial complex K is flag if it satisfies the -
following condition: every finite subset of vertices σ = {vo, . . . , vr} is a simplex of K if and only if every pair of vertices in σ is a simplex (edge) of K. Equivalently, K is flag if it is the clique complex of its 1-skeleton (viewed as a graph).
Remark 2.6. It is easy to see that, if K is a flag complex, a vertex v is dominated by a vertex v′ if and only if for every vertex w such that {v, w} ∈ K, we have {v′, w} ∈ K. Equivalently, v is dominated by v′ if and only if the closed neighborhood N [v] of v in the 1-skeleton of K (viewed as a graph) is contained in the closed neighborhood N [v′] of v′. Note also that, in this case, K ∖ v is also flag, which implies that the cores of flag complexes are also flag.
3. Persistent homology
We recall very briefly the basic notions on persitent homology and refer the reader to the excellent books [8, 10, 15, 3, 24] for more details. We use simplicial homology over a field F. A filtration is a nested sequence of simplicial complexes K0 ⊆ K1 ⊆ K2 ⊆ . . . ⊆ Km. For every degree q ≥ 0, if we apply the homology functor Hq, we obtain a sequence of

Any (finite) persistence module can be decomposed as a multiset of intervals of the form [i, j). The intervals can be viewed as points (i, j) ∈ R × (R ∪ {∞}). This multiset is called the persistence diagram. Each interval (point) [i, j) corresponds to a cycle which appears at i (birth) and dissapears at j (death). The points (i, j) far from the diagonal are those that-
 persist for a long time. Given a point cloud (X, d), one can take, for each ε > 0, Kε = VR(X, ε). Recall that VR(X, ε) is the Vietoris-Rips complex of X with parameter ε, whose simplices are the finite subsets {x0, . . . , xr} ⊆ X with d(xk, xl) ≤ ε. Note that, if ε ≤ ε′ then Kε ⊆ Kε′, so the Vietoris-Rips complexes form a filtration, which is finite (since X is finite). The persistent homology of the sample X is the PH of its associated Vietoris-Rips filtration.

4. The δ-core subsampling
Given a point cloud X endowed with a metric d, a parameter δ > 0 and a point x ∈ X, we define the δ-neighborhood of x as the subset Nδ(x) = {y ∈ X | d(x, y) ≤ δ}.
Definition 4.1. Let (X, d) be a finite metric space. Fix a parameter δ > 0. We say that a point x ∈ X is dominated by a point x′ ̸= x if Nδ(x) ⊆ Nδ(x′).
Remark 4.2. Note that if x is dominated by x′, then d(x, x′) ≤ δ and every point y that is δ-close to x (i.e d(y, x) ≤ δ) is also δ-close to x′.
We define a δ-core of (X, d) similarly as we did for simplicial complexes.
Definition 4.3. A δ-core of a point cloud (X, d) is a subsample with no dominated points.

Construction of the δ-core. Starting with a point cloud X and a fixed parameter δ > 0, we look for dominated points. If x1 ∈ X is a dominated point of X, we remove it from the sample and obtain a subsample X − {x1}. We repeat this procedure until we reach a subsample without dominated points. In each step i we remove a point xi that is dominated in X − {x1, . . . , xi−1} (with the metric d inherited from X). The δ-core that we construct with this procedure depends, of course, on the points that we-
 choose to remove. But, similarly as in the simplicial complex case, two different δ-cores are equivalent in the following sense.
Definition 4.4. Given a point cloud (X, d) and a parameter δ, we say that two subsamples Y, Z ⊆ X are δ-equivalent if there is a biyection φ : Y → Z such that, for every y, y′ ∈ Y , d(y, y′) ≤ δ if and only if d(φ(y), φ(y′) ≤ δ.
Theorem 4.5. Given (X, d) and a parameter δ, the δ-core of X is unique up to δequivalence.
Proof. Consider the Vietoris-Rips complex K = VR(X, δ). Recall that the vertex set of K is X and a subset σ = {x0, . . . , xr} is a simplex of K if and only if d(xi, xj) ≤ δ for every i, j. By Remark 2.6, x is a dominated point of X if and only if it is a dominated vertex of the simplicial complex K. Note also that K ∖ x = VR(X − {x}, δ). This implies that a δ-core of the point cloud X corresponds to the set of vertices of a core of K. Now the results follows from Theorem 2.5. □
In particular all possible δ-cores of X have the same cardinality and similar shape. See [Algorithm 1] below for the pseudo-code of the construction. The computational cost of the δ-core algorithm has two components. First, computing all δ-neighborhoods (lines 5–7 in [Algorithm 1]) requires determining, for each point, which other points lie within distance δ. A naive implementation evaluates all O(n2) pairwise distances. However, for low-dimensional Euclidean data (dimension d ≲ 20) using spatial
data structures such as KD-trees or ball trees, this step can be performed in O(n log n+nk ̄) time, where k ̄ is the average δ-degree. In the second phase (lines 10–22), the algorithm repeatedly removes dominated vertices. Checking whether N (x) ⊆ N (y) for a pair of adjacent vertices requires O(kmax) time, and each vertex compares itself to at most kmax neighbors. Therefore one full sweep costs O(nk2max). In the worst case the algorithm may perform O(n) sweeps, leading to a theo
retical upper bound O(n2k2max). However, for geometric point clouds in low dimensions, kmax is typically small and many vertices are removed simultaneously, so the number of sweeps is small in practice. With efficient neighborhood computation, the empirical running time is close to O(n log n). This preprocessing step can significantly reduce the size of the Vietoris-Rips complex. Persistent homology via matrix reduction has complexity O(s3), where s is the number of simplices. For VR(X, ε) up to d-
imension d, the worst-case number of simplices is s = O(nd+1). If the core preserves only a fraction α < 1 of the vertices, the number of simplices scales as αd+1, and the complexity improves from O(s3) to O (αd+1s)3 .

The parameter δ. The choice of the parameter δ depends on the characteristics of the data. If δ is too large, the subsample size will be small and topological information may be lost. For instance, when δ approaches the diameter of the dataset, the core tends to a single point (the core of a simplex is a vertex). Conversely, if δ is too small, the reduction will be minimal and the computational gain negligible. For relatively uniform samples, a value around the 15th to 20th percentile of pairwise -
distances is typically appropriate, yielding substantial computational improvement, especially when the sample contains more than 1000 points and persistent homology is computed up to degree 2 or higher. For sparser or more heterogeneous samples, a lower percentile such as the 7th to 10th may be preferable to preserve topological features in regions of varying density. In practice, the optimal choice of δ can be determined by

5. Experimental results
We present experimental results on both synthetic and real datasets. First, we show two examples with synthetic data: the heterogeneous point cloud in the unit cube from the previous section, and a more homogeneous sample of 2000 points on a 2-dimensional torus in R3. We evaluate computation times and distances between persistence diagrams. We also contrast the δ-core subsampling with FPS (maxmin) subsampling, using the same number of points, and benchmark our construction against the witness comp-
lex (with randomly chosen landmarks) Finally, we present two additional examples with real heterogeneous datasets and compare our method with FPS subsampling and a recently introduced outlier-robust subsampling method [18]. In all cases, our method achieves better approximations (both in bottleneck and Wasserstein distances). We implement our algorithm in Python and use Ripser.py [19] for persistent homology and GUDHI [11] for witness complexes. All experiments are performed on a standard desktop -

6. Conclusions
We presented an effective subsampling method based on the topological reduction technique of strong collapse. We contrasted our construction with other known methods and observed that, in general, it produces more accurate subsamples and better approximates persistent homology, while maintaining a relatively low computational cost compared with other alternative methods.
