Introduction
Theoretical ecologists have long leveraged empirical data in various forms to advance ecology (1). Recently increased volumes and access to ecological data present an expanding set of opportunities for theoreticians to inform model development, framing, and interpretation. Whereas statisticians have collective guidance on best practices for data use (e.g., (2)), theoreticians might lack formal education on how to integrate diverse types of data into a single ecological model. As a group of predomi-
nantly early-career theoretical ecologists, we have developed guiding principles and practical tips to support theoretical ecologists in synthesizing multiple types of data at different phases of the modeling process (Figure 1). Our rules fall into three overarching themes: iteration in the data-model integration process (Rule 1, 10; (3)), leveraging multiple sources of data (Rules 2-6; (4)), and understanding uncertainty (Rules 7-9; (1)). Across these rules, we emphasize that the data-model integ-
ration requires transparent, justifiable, and defensible communication of modeling choices to support readers in appropriately contextualizing the model and its implications.
Rule 1: The data-model integration process is iterative
Though workflows for data-model integration can appear linear in academic writing, the process commonly requires revisiting prior steps when new insights are gained from later steps (3). Across the data-model integration workflow, there are multiple places to incorporate different data sources and types, which can help support modeling choices and refine models (Figure 1). For example, a theoretician studying a stage-structured model might revisit the scientific literature, model formulation, or p-
arameterization if they later realize their choice for stage categorization or transition rates mismatches empirical knowledge of the natural system. In model development, drawing on established knowledge from qualitative and quantitative data (Table 1) can help revise the question framing (Rule 3), model structure (Rule 4), and parameterization (Rule 5) to better answer the central question being explored. Moving ‘backwards’ in the workflow is not regression on the research project, but rather a -
natural and necessary procedure.

Rule 2: Expand your idea of data
There are diverse data types that can shape and inform ecological models (Table 1). We advocate for using multiple forms of data throughout the modeling process (Figure 1, Rule 4). Mental models and intuition can shape model conceptualization and interpretation (19), while qualitative data can guide model framing and reveal dynamics not captured by quantitative datasets. Quantitative data can inform parameterization and sensitivity analysis (see further discussion in Rules 3, 7-9). Expert opinion -
can help ground model formulation, parameterization, and outputs (Rule 6).
We also encourage modelers to consider whether and how to bring together multiple ways of knowing (sensu (20,21)) in their research. Co-production and collaboration with empiricists, social scientists, and knowledge holders outside academia can identify new research questions, reveal new hypotheses, and broaden model relevance (Rules 6, 10; (4)). For example, MacCall et al. (22) co-produced heuristic models of fish population dynamics, built on Traditional Knowledge of Pacific herring migration dy-
namics, to explore alternative models of fish behavior

and implications for sustainable fisheries management. Incorporating diverse types of knowledge throughout the modeling process requires more than extracting data; it calls for ethical engagement and critical reflection on the systems from which data emerge (4). This includes reflecting on the epistemologies (23), historical and present contexts, uncertainties, and biases embedded in how data is defined, collected, transmitted, and interpreted. Rather than integrating knowledge into Western norms-
 of scientific inquiry, ethical engagement involves respecting distinct knowledge systems and sovereignty, avoiding extractive practices, and supporting Indigenous leadership in co-producing questions and knowledge (24–26). Engagement with Indigenous title- rights- and knowledge holders will ethically, and sometimes legally, require co-production (see Glossary; (25,27)). For a more comprehensive discussion of ethical knowledge co-production, we direct the reader to, among others, (25,27–30). Embra-
cing a broader view of data and learning how to ethically engage with diverse data and multiple ways of knowing (20,29) enables more relevant, inclusive, and impactful ecological modeling (31,32).
Rule 3: Align your research question or framing with available data
The types of data available and their intended use can help theoreticians identify thoughtful and compelling research questions. One framework for categorizing research questions and associated models is Levins’s triangle (33), which posits inherent trade-offs between generality, precision, and realism (33). Strategic questions (left edge, Figure 2) aim for generalizable takeaways rooted in a realistic case, sacrificing precision (1). For example, Karatayev and Baskett (34) used quantitative and q-
ualitative data to build a kelp forest-inspired model to explore a more general question about the effect of dispersal on alternate stable states. Mechanism-motivated questions (35) (right edge, Figure 2) sacrifice realism to test the logic of hypotheses and investigate mechanisms within a system. They use data to articulate how systems work without necessarily matching model parameters precisely to quantitative data. For example, Chesson and Warner (36) mathematically tested alternative mental mo-
dels for how species coexist in variable environments. Tactical questions (bottom edge, Figure 2) require detailed, system-specific information for realistic, precise takeaways (1), sacrificing generality. For example, Kaare-Rasmussen et al. (7) used a dynamic energy budget model to understand how anemone symbionts affect host growth, matching their model outputs with system-specific laboratory data. Using available data to situate the research question within Levins’s triangle can help connect th-
e work within the existing literature while highlighting novel insights.
Rule 4: Link mathematical form to biological meaning
Theoretical ecologists translate biological systems into mathematical equations and mathematical results into ecological insights. Generally, the first step in building and explaining a mathematical expression is to identify key biological mechanisms within the model. This step can be informed by diverse types of data (Rule 2). Then, the next step is to formalize the biological mechanism into mathematics, often drawing on typical mathematical forms (e.g., Holling functional responses for predator--
prey interactions) or past mathematical theory directly. In some cases, it might also be possible to derive the functional form from first principles (e.g., (37)) or empirical insights (e.g., (38)). Importantly, there are many sources of possible biological data to justify a near infinite number of mathematical assumptions. No modeling choice nor assumption is necessarily wrong; in fact, the synthesis of multiple mathematical models with

different underlying biological data and assumptions can create a more robust scientific understanding.
Rule 5: Use as much data as you need, but no more
While our ability to access “big data” makes adding more data to models tempting, consider the amount and precision necessary for the project goals (1). Models emphasizing precision and prediction (Rule 3) might require carefully identifying parameter values from empirical literature or reducing error between quantitative empirical data and model output in order to identify parameter values. In contrast, models emphasizing ecological patterns (Rule 3) might use qualitative data to ground model ass-
umptions, coupled with sensitivity analysis (Rules 7-8) to understand how parameters affect outcomes; determining the precise value of a parameter might not be necessary to achieve the model’s goal (Rule 9). Letting model goals guide the need for specific pieces of data can help ensure that the data-model integration process sees the forest through the trees. Einstein’s philosophy of “Make it as simple as possible, but no simpler” guides theoreticians when designing models, and we would suggest, i-
n the context of model building, “Use as much data as you need, but no more.”
Rule 6: Gut-check data with others and question your interpretations
Data interpretations can vary as they reflect an individual's perspective, modeling approach, and understanding of the system (4). Clarity in data interpretation helps readers understand the model’s parameterization and possible applications, and, more broadly, contributes to reproducibility in modeling efforts. Erroneous assumptions about data early in model building can lead to incorrect model mechanisms, misinterpretation or bias in results, unrealistic patterns, and poor predictive power. A ca-
reful, critical gut-check of both the data and the interpretation of it can help avoid these issues in the data-model integration process. While reviewing literature and creating mental models can help support data interpretation, talking frequently to people with system expertise can ensure thoughtful integration. Just as talking with academics requires thoughtful accreditation of ideas and understanding (e.g., CReDIT, (39)), talking with system experts outside of academia similarly requires ethi-
cal processes to avoid harmful extraction of data, ensure consent, properly recognize contributions, and build long-term respectful relationships (20,27,28,40,41). Questions to ask such experts (including oneself) might include:
● Which processes do you think are most important in driving process X within this population/system? ● Based on the data, I’ve done Y with my model. Do you think Y represents the data and your understanding of the system? ● Are the challenges I’m facing in building and parameterizing my model reflective of possible gaps in the empirical literature? Is there expert knowledge that I’m not finding that would support my modeling process? ● Do you predict your results would be similar across populatio-
ns, or is there a unique aspect of the population you studied that leads to these results? ● How did you measure variable Z? What are the units of variable Z? How confident are you that you captured this value?

Rule 7: Formally evaluate model uncertainty and prediction sensitivity
Models capture a limited picture of the natural variability in complex systems (33) and ecological knowledge is inherently imperfect (42). This can propagate model uncertainty (1). Conducting a formal sensitivity or uncertainty analysis (SA/UA; see glossary) helps to systematically describe uncertainty, clarify interpretation of the model results, and increase model credibility (43–46). It can also inform future research priorities (43,47). SA/UA can be implemented in many ways depending on the mo-
del goals (Rule 3), the amount of data (Rule 5), the type of uncertainty of interest (Rule 7), the computational complexity of the model (e.g., number of input factors, interactions, etc.) and the intended application of the model or SA/UA (discussed in (43–45,48)). For example, a local, one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis can help describe how changing a set of key input factors, usually established a priori based on the central modeling question, influences model outputs (although see limitations-
 noted in (49)). In contrast, a global sensitivity analysis can identify the effects of interactions between input factors (50). Another approach might be to test multiple parameterizations (e.g., representative of different possible species or systems) to identify which model results and associated qualitative trends are robust, and which are not, to parameter choices.
Rule 8: Acknowledge and discuss the uncertainty in your choices for model structure and parametrization
Formal tools (Rule 7) can be helpful to address how uncertainty might affect quantitative results, but in some cases, there might be uncertainty about the uncertainty itself that cannot be fully quantified (i.e., ecological unknown) or resolved (i.e., irreducible uncertainty). Clearly acknowledging missing, stochastic, or imperfect data, in both model structure and parameterization, can help readers better understand the model assumptions and limitations. Structural uncertainty refers to uncertain-
ty in the design of the model itself, e.g., choice of compartments in a disease model. This uncertainty can occur when there is inconsistent or missing ecological knowledge for a particular system. Discussing the data sources informing model structures, and the limitations of those choices, can help the reader understand the empirical contexts for which the model applies and inspire future research needs (Rules 9-10). Parameter uncertainty refers to error or imprecision in parameter estimates. Thi-
s uncertainty can arise from measurement error, limited data informing the value of a parameter, parameters representing non-measurable quantities, or parameters with inherent variability. For instance, a model parameter such as average clutch size could vary due to observation error (reducible uncertainty) or demographic stochasticity (irreducible uncertainty; (42)). Theoreticians can address uncertainty in parameter estimates by providing a metric of uncertainty or formally exploring model sensi-
tivity to changes in the parameter value (Rule 7).
Rule 9: Contextualize and discuss results given data and model uncertainty
Humans often struggle to understand and interpret uncertainty (51,52). To communicate uncertainty, theoreticians can use formal tools like SA/UA (Rule 7), create figures that visualize uncertainty (see discussion in (4)), and critically discuss how uncertainty in model choices propagates to uncertainty in model results (noting discussion in (53)). Building on mental models and intuition can help readers understand uncertainty (54). How unknowns in data and

uncertainty in results are interpreted depends on critical analysis of the underlying data and the model’s objective (Rules 3, 6-7; (1,3)). When model interpretation focuses on qualitative results and relative trends, a central question for results interpretation might be, “Do key uncertainties in underlying data propagate to uncertainty in model outcomes, and what data are needed to determine the qualitative patterns in results?” (Rule 10). Alternatively, when focusing on quantitatively precise -
results, consider asking, “What data sources drive uncertainty in model estimates and would more precise data improve prediction?” (Rules 7-8, 10).
Rule 10: The iterative data-model conversation also applies across studies
Data-model integration often extends beyond a single research project. Expanding from Rule 1, we finish our set of rules by recognizing the importance of cross-talk between modeling efforts and data collection across projects. Ideas for new models or clarity about previously developed models can be informed by insights from new data, drawing on all the dimensions of data we have highlighted here (Rules 2-3). Inversely, theoretical ecology can help inform future data collection through modeling exp-
loration (Rule 7) and UA/SA (Rule 8), which can identify the mechanisms and parameters that have outsized influence on results, indicating the need for more precise estimation. Cross-talk between theory and data collection (Figure 1) is necessary to inform our understanding and interpretation of results derived from both theoretical and empirical work: “A model without data is empty; data without a model is blind” ((55), pg. 72).
Conclusion
Some uncertainty or imprecision is inevitable in both the structure and parameterization of theoretical models. Working with data that are biased, uncertain, imprecise, or incomplete does not render a model useless. Iterating across the data-modeling integration process (Rule 1, 10; (1)), leveraging diverse data sources (Rules 2-6; (2)), and understanding uncertainty (Rules 7-9; (3)) can help theoreticians situate their models within the best available scientific context, support appropriate model-
 interpretation, and identify future directions for empirical and theoretical research.

Co-production of knowledge The process of jointly designing and implementing research projects with project partners, recognizing that project partners have the option to engage fully or in part at any step of the research process and equal authority over if or how data and findings are shared ((56), forthcoming). Epistemology The philosophy of how and what we know and where that knowledge comes from (23). Generality How broadly applicable a model is across systems. Indigenous ecological knowledge-
 “A cumulative body of knowledge, practice and belief evolving by adaptive processes and handed down through generations by cultural transmission, about the relationship of living beings (including humans) with one another and with their environment” ((57), p. 7). Indigenous knowledge is unique to a people and place, and is “situated knowledge” in that it “is not separable from the knowledge holders/keepers or the environment in which it is embedded” ((21) p. 246; (58,59))

Irreducible Uncertainty Uncertainty endogenous to the system, e.g., natural variability or inherent randomness (42). Reducible Uncertainty arises from uncertainty not endogenous to the system, e.g., measurement or systematic error (42). Local ecological knowledge A “cumulative body of intergenerational knowledge, practices, values, and worldviews, and embedded in the relationships between local people and nature” ((60), p. 483). Precision The repeatability of a measurement or, for Levins’ triangl-
e, how testable a model’s outputs are. Imprecision can arise when there are conflicting or variable measurements. Realism How accurately a model integrates the mechanisms of the observed biological system. Qualitative data Data that is not numeric, including narrative or descriptive data. Box 1. Putting the rules to action. Here, we provide the behind-the-scenes process of building, parameterizing, and interpreting theoretical models from our own experiences to highlight how our rules can be used by theoreticians to create more informed–and more informative–models.
Rules 1, 2, 4, 6 and 9: In Serpico et al ((63)), the authors (including CMH) built a model for aquatic population dynamics to describe the effects of lake eutrophication and warming scenarios at different trophic levels. The model was parameterized using time series data of dissolved oxygen concentration, population dynamics, and various other lake characteristics. While the data suggested that organisms could survive extremely low dissolved oxygen concentrations, the authors’ own literature scan -
seemed to contradict this information (Rule 2). The authors thought maybe the time series were inaccurate. However, through further literature search and discussion with others (Rules 1-2, 6), the authors realized they were modeling a “well-mixed” environment and the data was collected from the bottom of the lake, where water tends to be stagnant and have less dissolved oxygen. To account for this (Rule 4), and to support readers in understanding the uncertainty and limitations of the model (Rule -
9), the authors pointed out the key ecological contexts their model would (lakes without spatial dynamics) and would not (lake systems with complex spatiotemporal dynamics) apply.
Rules 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 10: In Baskett et al (64), the authors (including MLB) investigated

how including evolutionary dynamics for coral symbionts affected anticipated coral reef responses to future climate change. Most of the parameters came from existing data-driven models of coral dynamics, but empirical values were not available for the width of the thermal tolerance curve. Therefore, the authors tuned this parameter to match qualitative trends of coral declines during previously-observed bleaching events (Rules 2, 5). Because of limited data for some model components, the authors -
framed their research around a qualitative question, rather than emphasizing the model output as a precise prediction (Rule 3). When looking into the output model dynamics, they observed seasonal fluctuations in symbiont dynamics, an outcome that was unplanned. Following this observation, the authors re-searched the literature to see if this was realistic and found empirical evidence for such dynamics, which provided an unexpected opportunity for model validation (Rule 6). A local sensitivity (ela-
sticity) analysis of model parameters ranked the thermal tolerance curve breath, the most poorly known parameter, highest in its influence on coral cover; this helps guide priorities for empirical research that might enable more quantitatively precise predictions (Rules 8, 10). Finally, an exploration of multiple climate models and climate scenarios, which capture scientific and societal uncertainty in climate projections (Rules 7-8), elucidated the central takeaway of the paper: evolution can pro-
mote coral persistence under more moderate, but not more severe, climate scenarios.
