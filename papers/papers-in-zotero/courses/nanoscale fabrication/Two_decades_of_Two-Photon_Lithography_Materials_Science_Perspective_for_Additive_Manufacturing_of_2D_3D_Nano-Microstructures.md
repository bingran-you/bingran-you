Abstract
Two-photon lithography (TPL) is a versatile technology for additive manufacturing of 2D and 3D micro/nanostructures with subwavelength-resolved features. Recent advancement in laser technology has enabled the application of TPL fabricated structures in several fields such as microelectronics, photonics, optoelectronics, microfluidics, and plasmonic devices. However, the lack of two-photon polymerisable resins (TPPR) induces bottleneck to the growth of TPL to its true potential, and hence continuou-
s research efforts are focussed on developing efficient TPPRs. In this article, we review the recent advancements in PI and TPPR formulation and the impact of process parameters on fabrication of 2D and 3D structures for specific applications.

Fundamentals of two-photon polymerisation
Two-photon absorption (TPA) is a nonlinear optical process in which a fluorophore is excited to a higher state via a virtual intermediate state due to a sequential absorption of two photons of lower energy. To facilitate excitation by absorption of two photons, the sum of the energies of each isolated photon needs to match the electronic transition of the PI. Sometimes, a photosensitiser (PS) is introduced along with the PI to enhance its absorption characteristics.As the lifetime of the intermedi-
ate states of PS or PI is very small (~few femtoseconds), ultra-short

119 laser pulses are required to realize TPA. This became possible only after the advent of ultrafast

120 lasers. The TPL technique utilizes spatial confinement of TPA via a non-linear interaction of

121 polymeric resin within the polymer matrix with tightly focused femtosecond laser pulses. Since

122 TPA is confined to the area where light intensity is very high, it can induce photo-chemical and

123 photo-physical changes, not only on the surface but also in the bulk of the material. TPL generally

124 utilizes commercially available UV-curable photopolymers mixed with free radical or cationic

125 PIs which absorb two photons of near-infrared (NIR) light simultaneously and generate suitable

126 speices to initiate photo-polymerisation. This leads to polymerised voxels (PV) with a volume of

127 the order of femtoliters and hence features formed by TPL are of resolution beyond the diffraction

128 limit. PV serves as the smallest building block for the microstructures, and smaller PVs allow

129 the tighter arrangement resulting in better resolution and quality of the structures. For TPPR with

130 higher TPP sensitivity, smaller PVs are formed as compared to the TPPR with low TPP

131 sensitivity, furthermore, the PV size can be controlled by varying process parameters 47,48 as well 132 as TPPR composition 49. The lateral resolution of the fabricated lines (detailed derivation can be 133 seen in theoretical section) can be expressed by the relation below26;

327 Advantages and limitations of TPP technique
328
329 TPP uses a pulsed laser system that operates in the near infra-red region, where most of the
330 curable monomers and polymers are transparent. This enables the laser light to penetrate deeper.
331 Besides, TPA occurs only in defined spatial regions where the light intensity is high enough.

332 Owing to this feature, this process can polymerise a small and specific region in the bulk of the 333 liquid resin, without making any changes to its surface or the surrounding region. TPP-based 334 microfabrication has a set of unique advantages such as (i) fabrication of high-resolution 335 micro/nanostructures with greater precision, (ii) 3D printing in a single exposure step and (iii) 336 fabrication of complex features with high accuracy and repeatability over conventional 337 microfabric-
ation techniques

338 Unfortunately, the raster scanning of tiny voxels results in longer printing time which makes this

339 method unsuitable for mass production. A few developments have been made for parallel

restricted to the microvolumes offered by the large numerical aperture of the focusing optics. 701 Two-photon photoinitiating system (TPPS) serves three main purposes: facilitation of two702 photon up-conversion, initiation, and termination of crosslinking monomers/oligomers. 703 Typically, a TPPS is composed of either PI, photoinhibitor, and PS or their combination. TPPS 704 plays a crucial role in spatially localized TPA and transfer of the up-converted energy to the 705 cross-linker/radical generator. The charge transfer between the PI and the monomer initiates the 706 crosslinking proce-
ss leading to the formation of polymerised voxels within the irradiated volume.

975 Conclusion and outlook

976 In this review, we have presented a comprehensive overview of recent advancements in TPL977 based 3D printing of micro/nanoscale devices. Special attention has been given to discuss design 978 strategies for the formulations of resins and PI to achieve improved TPP sensitivity. Further, the 979 strategies to control TPL process parameters to obtain optimal 3D patterning with the best 980 achievable resolution have been discussed regarding their applications in micro/nanophotonics, 981 MEMS, me-
tamaterials and biomedical applications among others. We have discussed the role of 982 incorporation of dopants (e.g., metal /carbon nanoparticles, dyes, semiconductor quantum dots, 983 and biomolecules) into photo-resist PI, to impart additional functional characteristics to the 984 fabricated micro/nanostructures. With present technology and available TPPRs, the true potential

985 of TPL fabrication is constrained due to low writing speed, small processing volumes and the

986 complexity of the materials system arising due to multiple component formulations of the PI and

987 PR. Though the synthesis protocols for designing PI and PR materials are well established, their

988 complex synthesis procedure forms a bottleneck.

989 The synergistic advancements in material science, optics and laser technology, are pivotal for

990 further progress of TPL-based additive manufacturing and their potential practical applications.

991 Development of novel functional materials with good TPP sensitivity and minimal material

992 components are expected to drive TPL towards fabrication of monolithically integrated devices

993 such as lab-on-a-chip sensors and actuators. The incorporation of stimuli-responsive functional

994 995 996 997 998 999 1000 1001 1002 1003 1004 1005 1006

Journal Pre-proof groups to the resin can be exploited for fabrication of externally controlled/driven
micro/nanomachines applicable in biomedical and therapeutical applications, microfluidic devices, microreactors. Further research and development into materials can be directed towards the development of smart materials programmed to deform-reconfigure in response to the changes in the local environment, leading to the application of TPL in next-generation, selfdriven micromachines. On the other hand, advancements in optics and laser technology, are envisioned to offer new classes of lasers with h-
igher average laser powers and, engineered optical components can be exploited to attain scalable fabrication with improved throughput. For, instance the parallel printing TPL process is still limited to a few spots processed at once due to the small focal volume of the objective lens. However, an array of miniature, planar, focusing elements in combination with collimated laser beams can be incorporated to counter these issues. This will assist in increasing the throughput of the TPL printer. Wit-
h the encouraging results, contributions from enthusiastic researchers and technological advancements we can envisage a

1007 paradigm shift and accelerated development in additive manufacturing of 3D micro/nanoscale

1008 structures, leading to advancement in fundamental sciences, engineering and medical science in

1009 near future.
