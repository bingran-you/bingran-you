The Self-Projecting Grassmannian
Alheydis Geiger∗ and Francesca Zaffalon∗†
Abstract
We introduce the self-projecting Grassmannian, an irreducible subvariety of the Grassmannian parametrizing linear subspaces that satisfy a generalized self-duality condition. We study its relation to classical moduli spaces, such as the moduli spaces of pointed curves of genus g, as well as to other natural subvarieties of the Grassmannian. We further translate the self-projectivity condition in the combinatorial language of matroids, introducing self-projecting matroids, and we computationally in-
vestigate their realization spaces inside the self-projecting Grassmannian.
1 Introduction
The Grassmannian is the point of entry to moduli spaces. It parameterizes k-dimensional vector subspaces of Kn, or configurations of n points in Pk−1, modulo PGL-action. Subvarieties of the Grassmannian encode additional constraints on such point configurations. One important example is self-duality, where the set of points satisfies Gale duality. These were studied, among others, by Dolgachev–Ortland, Coble, Petrakiev and Eisenbud–Popescu [DO88, Cob22, Pet09, EP00]. The subvariety of such point c-
onfigurations inside the Grassmannian is the self-dual Grassmannian [GHSV24]. Formally, a point V ∈ Gr(k, 2k) is said to be self-dual if V is an element of the (K∗)2k torus orbit of V ⊥. In this paper, we generalize self-dual point configurations to self-projecting point configurations. These describe points V ∈ Gr(k, n) such that V is contained in an element of the (K∗)n torus orbit of V ⊥. We introduce the subvariety of the Grassmannian parameterizing such point configurations: the self-projecti-
ng Grassmannian. It is defined as the Zariski closure of the space of V ∈ Gr(k, n), represented by a k × n matrix X, such that

2.4 Associated point configurations
The study of (self-)dual point configurations has a rich history. Introduced in 1922 by Coble [Cob22], they were studied among others by Dolgachev–Ortland, Petrakiev, Kapranov [DO88, Pet09, Kap93]. Recall that an associated point configuration is a pair (x, y) ∈ (Pk−1)n × (Pn−k−1)n, such that (φ(xi, yi))i∈[n] ∈ (Pk(n−k)−1)n is a minimal projectively dependent set, where φ : Pk−1 × Pn−k−1 → Pk(n−k)−1 is the Segre embedding.
Proposition 2.16. A self-projecting point configuration X ∈ X(k, n)sd defines associated points (x, y) ∈ (Pk−1)n×(Pn−k−1)n, with π(yi) = xi, for all i ∈ [n], such that the equivalence class of x in X(k, n)sd is equal to X. Here π : Pn−k−1 → Pk−1 is the projection to the first k coordinates.
Proof. The claim follows from the following observation. Let X, Y be matrices with representatives of the projective points xi, yi in the columns, respectively. Then x, y are associated if and only if there exists a diagonal matrix Λ with non-zero entries such that XΛY t = 0. Equivalently, Y = Λ · ker(X). In this section, we study in detail some self-projecting Grassmannians and give a geometric interpretation for them. We set K to be algebraically closed and omit it from the notation.
3.1 9 self-projecting points in P3
The first non-trivial example of the self-projecting Grassmannian, which differs from the self-dual case, is SGr(4, 9). An element of X(4, 9) is a generic configuration of 9 points in P3 up to projective equivalence. Given 9 points in general position in P3 there is a unique quadric surface passing through the points. If the point configuration is self-projecting, there exists a 1-dimensional family of quadric surfaces through the points. The intersection of two linearly independent quadric surfac-
es in P3 defines a quartic curve. For general quadric surfaces, this curve is irreducible and of genus 1, i.e., it is an elliptic curve. Hence, a generic V ∈ X(4, 9)sd defines an elliptic curve with 9 marked points. That is, we obtain a map φ : X(4, 9)sd → M1,9.
However, the moduli space M1,9 of elliptic curves with 9 marked points is a 9-dimensional variety, while X(4, 9)sd is 10-dimensional. Informally, the extra dimension of the selfprojective configuration space corresponds to the choice of an embedding for the elliptic curve. Formally, we have the following.
Theorem 3.1. The self-projecting configuration space X(4, 9)sd and the moduli space M1,10 are birationally equivalent.
Proof. Consider the open subset U of X(4, 9)sd of points V ∈ X(4, 9)sd on an irreducible curve CV of genus 1. Different representations of V ∈ U define isomorphic elliptic curves in P3. Consider the hyperplane H through p1, p2, p3 in P3 and denote by p10 the fourth intersection point of H with CV . Since the 9 points are in general position, no four of them lie on a hyperplane. Thus, p10 ̸= pi for all i ∈ [9]. Therefore, we can define a map ψ : U → M1,10.
The map ψ is injective on U . For any two configurations V, W ∈ X(4, 9)sd, where V = (p1 . . . p9) and W = (q1 . . . q9), and ψ(V ) = ψ(W ), then the curves CV and CW and the linear systems |p1 + p2 + p3 + p10| and |q1 + q2 + q3 + q10| are isomorphic. Hence, there exists a map A ∈ PGL(4) realizing this isomorphism. Since the curves are isomorphic as marked curves in M1,10, this implies that A maps each pi to qi. It follows that V and W are equivalent up to PGL(4)-action. In particular, they descri-
be the same point in X(4, 9)sd. Since M1,10 is irreducible and has the same dimension as X(4, 9)sd, it follows that ψ is a birational map between the two varieties.
Corollary 3.2. There is a birational map between X(3, 9) and X(4, 9)sd obtained by lifting the elliptic curve from P2 to P3.

3.3 13 self-projecting points in P4
If a point V ∈ Gr(5, 13) lies in the self-projecting Grassmannian, then the 15 × 13 matrix ν(V ) has rank strictly smaller than 13. Hence, the columns of V define points lying in the

Theorem 3.5. The configuration space X(5, 13)sd is birationally equivalent to the moduli space M5,13 of genus 5 curves with 13 distinct marked points.
Proof. Consider the open subset U of X(5, 13)sd of points V such that ν(V ) has rank equal to 12. Then, the columns of V lie on a unique genus 5 curve, given by the complete intersection of 3 linearly independent quadrics in the left kernel of ν(V ). Hence, there is a well-defined map ψ : U → M5,13. The map is injective because the columns of V correspond to the canonical embedding of the marked points on the genus 5 curve, whenever this is non-trigonal and non-hyperelliptic. Since M5,13 is irredu-
cible and the varieties have the same dimension, the claim follows.
4 Self-projecting matroids and their realization spaces
In this section, we study the combinatorial properties of self-projecting point configurations. We introduce self-projecting matroids and computationally study their realization spaces inside the self-projecting Grassmannian. Recall from [GHSV24] that a self-dual point V ∈ Gr(k, 2k) defines an (identically) selfdual matroid, i.e. a matroid equal to its dual. In this sense, self-dual matroids encode how Plu ̈cker coordinates can vanish without breaking Equation (2). Realization spaces of selfdual m-
atroids inside the self-dual Grassmannian SGr(k, 2k) where studied in [GHSV24, Section 3]. In particular, there exist realizable self-dual matroids that cannot be realized by a self-dual point configuration. Here we show how this extends beyond the case n = 2k.
4.1 Self-projecting matroids
We start by noticing that self-duality has many equivalent definitions. Recall that a matroid M has the disjoint basis property if for every basis B of M there exists a basis C of M such that B ∩ C = ∅. Moreover, we introduce the following.
Definition 4.1. Given a matroid M of rank k on [n], an element e ∈ [n] is said to be a half-coloop if there exist two rank k − 1 flats F1, F2 such that F1 ∪ F2 = [n] \ {e}.

4.2 Self-projecting matroid realization spaces
As seen above, self-projecting point configurations give rise to self-projecting matroids. In the following we study the reverse question, that is, when and how are self-projecting matroids realized by self-projecting point configurations. We show computationally that most small realizable self-projecting matroids can be realized (and are generically realized) by a self-projecting point. This provides strong evidence that our definition of self-projecting matroid is the right generalization of sel-
f-duality. Note that we cannot expect all realizable self-projecting matroids to be realized by a self-projecting point configuration, as this fails even for self-dual matroids [GHSV24]. Formally, we study the following objects.
Definition 4.8. A k × n matrix X with entries in K is a realization of a matroid M if the columns of X indexed by I form a basis of Kk if and only if I is a basis of M. The realization space RK(M) of M is the quotient of the matroid cell Gr(M)◦/(K∗)n generated by all realizations of M over K. The matroid M is realizable over K if RK(M) ̸= ∅. A realization X of M is a self-projecting realization of M if there exists λ ∈ (K∗)n such that X diag(λ)Xt = 0. The self-projecting realization space SK(M) of-
 M is the quotient of the matroid cell SGr(M)◦/(K∗)n generated by all self-projecting realizations of M over K.
Note the self-projecting realization space of a matroid might be strictly smaller than the intersection of its realization space with the self-projecting Grassmannian. In particular, whenever M is a non self-projecting matroid of rank k on n elements, and k and n are such that SGr(k, n) = Gr(k, n), then SK(M) = ∅, while the latter will be equal to RK(M).
