MOHAMED CHAOUCH AND THANASIS STENGOS
Abstract. This paper investigates how market competition influences poverty dynamics using a
functional econometric framework that captures both contemporaneous and lagged effects. Using
annual data for 48 countries from 1991–2017, we estimate function-on-function regressions linking
poverty headcount ratios to market concentration and other macroeconomic indicators. The results
show that, based on the entire sample, stronger competition initially increased poverty during
structural adjustment phases, but its adverse impact weakened after 2010 as economies adapted
and efficiency gains emerged. The estimated bivariate surfaces reveal that the effect of competition
on poverty often persists over multiple years (around 5 years), highlighting the importance of
intertemporal transmission. Then, functional clustering based on market capitalization (MCAP)
uncovers strong heterogeneity: pro-poor 5-years lagged effect of competition in low- and medium
MCAP economies, while it remains insignificant to weakly negative in high-MCAP countries.
Overall, the findings underscore the value of functional data methods in uncovering evolving and
lag-dependent poverty–competition linkages that static panel models fail to capture.
JEL classification: C33, E60, I32
Keywords: Competition, Functional data analysis, Poverty.
1. Introduction
Reducing poverty and promoting shared prosperity remain central objectives of development
policy, yet the channels through which structural reforms influence poverty dynamics are still
imperfectly understood. Over the past three decades, many low- and middle-income countries
have implemented product-market liberalization, privatization, and deregulation, often combined
with trade opening, in ways that have substantially strengthened competition in domestic markets.
These reforms are typically justified on efficiency grounds: by reducing market power and barriers
to entry, stronger competition is expected to compress markups, reallocate resources toward more
productive firms, stimulate innovation, and ultimately support higher growth. Whether such
efficiency gains translate into lower poverty, however, depends on how the gains and losses from
competition are distributed across households and along the income distribution.
Competition affects poor households through several intertwined channels. On the consumption
side, lower markups and reduced market power can generate substantial price reductions in markets
for food, energy, transport, medicines, and other basic goods. Because poor households devote
a large share of their budgets to these necessities, even modest price cuts can have large welfare
effects (e.g. Begazo and Nyman, 2016; OECD, 2012, 2013). On the production side, increased

in the short and medium run (Begazo et al., 2017).
Recent theoretical work has sharpened this ambiguity by embedding competition into general
equilibrium frameworks with heterogeneous agents, imperfect labor markets, and explicit own
ership structure. In a vertically differentiated setting with consumers who can be workers and
shareholders, Ghazzai et al. (2023) show that moving from monopoly to duopoly can either re
duce or increase the headcount poverty rate, depending on the position of the poverty line, the
dispersion of preferences and productivities, and the share of profits accruing to poor versus rich
households. When ownership of firms is concentrated among richer households and the poor
obtain most of their income from wages and informal earnings, intensified competition tends to
reduce profits for the rich while lowering prices and sometimes raising wages, so that poverty can
fall. When poor households also rely on small-scale business profits that are threatened by more
efficient entrants, or when labor-market adjustment is slow, poverty may instead increase. This
literature emphasizes that the impact of competition on poverty is inherently state-dependent and
mediated by the distribution of assets, the structure of labor and product markets, and the depth
of financial systems, rather than being unconditionally pro-poor.
A parallel policy-oriented literature reaches similar conclusions using case studies and distri
butional simulations. World Bank and OECD reports document that a lack of competition in
markets for essential goods disproportionately harms poor consumers, whereas tackling cartels or
anti-competitive regulations in sectors such as food, transport, and pharmaceuticals can generate
meaningful poverty reductions through the price channel (Begazo et al., 2017). For South Africa,
for example, Begazo and Nyman (2016) simulate that dismantling just four cartels in wheat,
maize, poultry, and pharmaceuticals could have reduced the national poverty headcount by 0.4
percentage points, with income gains for the bottom decile more than three times larger than for
the top decile. Similar evidence from Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa shows that cartels
and regulatory restrictions in food and transport markets have raised prices by 30–50%, with
disproportionate impacts on poor households (Begazo et al., 2017).
Despite this growing interest, empirical evidence that directly links competition to poverty
outcomes remains relatively limited and fragmented. Much of the existing work either focuses on
sector-specific price effects or treats competition as part of broader trade and regulatory reforms.
Moreover, most empirical analyses rely on static or near-static frameworks—typically reduced-form
panel regressions—that link annual poverty indicators to contemporaneous or one-period-lagged
measures of competition, imposing constant effects over long periods of time. Such specifications

are ill-suited to capture the idea that both competition and poverty evolve gradually as part of
longer-term development trajectories, and that the impact of competition on poverty may change
sign along the reform process. For example, reforms that intensify competition may initially raise
poverty through job losses and business closures but later reduce it as efficiency gains are passed
on to consumers and as labor markets and institutions adjust. The timing and magnitude of
these effects are likely to differ systematically across countries depending on their level of financial
development and their institutional capacity to manage structural change.
In this paper we contribute to this debate by studying how competition affects poverty over time
and across levels of financial development in a large cross-country setting. Using annual data for
48 countries over the period 1991–2017, we combine internationally comparable poverty headcount
ratios at different international poverty lines with a Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) of market
concentration as a proxy for product-market competition and a set of macroeconomic controls,
including GDP per capita, government expenditure, trade openness, inflation, and growth, as
well as a measure of financial development (market capitalization over GDP). We model poverty
and competition as smoothly evolving trajectories and estimate how their relationship varies both
over calendar time and across lags. We further ask whether the competition–poverty relationship
differs across clusters of countries with low, medium, and high financial development, recognizing
that the ability of poor households to benefit from competition depends critically on access to
credit, the breadth of asset ownership, and the existence of mechanisms to insure against shocks.
In doing so, we speak directly to recent theoretical work on competition and poverty and to policy
debates on competition policy as an instrument for inclusive growth.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a literature review about
the relationship between competition and poverty. Section 3 describes the data and main variables
used in the analysis. Section 4 outlines the functional data analysis (FDA) framework and the
estimation strategy. Section 5 presents the empirical results, including the functional coefficient
estimates and their interpretation across poverty thresholds and financial-development clusters.
Finally, Section 6 concludes with the main findings and policy implications.
2. Literature review
The empirical literature on competition and poverty spans several strands that differ in their
focus and methodological approach. A first strand consists of sectoral studies that analyze how
changes in competition in markets for basic goods affect the real incomes of poor households.
These papers typically combine household expenditure surveys with detailed price data and sim
ulate how prices would change under more competitive market structures, for instance, under the
elimination of cartels or marketing boards. Cadot et al. (2009) examine the abolition of Madagas
car’s vanilla marketing board and find that increased competition in the vanilla market lowered
poverty among smallholders. Warr (2005) analyzes food policy in Indonesia and shows that price
distortions in staple food markets have sizable poverty impacts. More broadly, World Bank tools

such as the Welfare and Competition approach developed by Rodrı ́guez-Castela ́n et al. (2021) sim
ulate the distributional effects of reductions in market concentration in food and other essential
sectors, showing that poor households gain disproportionately from lower prices. These studies are
typically partial-equilibrium and price-channel focused: they take quantities and factor incomes as
given, or treat them in a highly stylized manner, and concentrate on the direct consumption-side
impact of price changes.
A second, closely related strand examines local market concentration and poverty using micro
or meso-level data. Rodrı ́guez-Castela ́n et al. (2018) study retail market concentration in Mex
ico, using a municipality-level panel with detailed measures of concentration in the retail sector.
They find that higher local concentration is associated with higher poverty, and that simulated
reductions in concentration through more competition would have non-trivial poverty-reducing
effects. Their work resonates with other studies documenting that market power in downstream
sectors such as retail and logistics can erode the real incomes of the poor, even when upstream
production is competitive. In a similar spirit, case studies on Kenya and other African countries
show that trade and regulatory policies that shield domestic incumbents in sugar and maize from
competition keep consumer prices well above world levels, with adverse effects on income and
poverty among net consumers (Argent and Begazo, 2015; Begazo et al., 2017). Methodologically,
these contributions typically estimate reduced-form regressions of poverty or welfare indicators on
measures of local concentration, controlling for fixed effects and standard observables, sometimes
complemented by policy simulations.
A third strand focuses on the broader relationship between competition, market power, and
poverty using cross-country or national panel data. Rodrı ́guez-Castel ́an (2015) uses a panel of
countries to analyze how market concentration at the national level is related to poverty, employing
fixed-effects regressions where headcount poverty ratios at different lines are regressed on measures
of product-market concentration and macroeconomic controls. His findings indicate that higher
concentration is associated with higher poverty, particularly in countries with weaker institutions
and lower financial development. In parallel, World Bank and OECD flagship reports synthesize
a range of country experiences and econometric evidence, arguing that competition policy can
support shared prosperity by lowering prices, improving access to jobs and inputs, and unlocking
productivity growth, while highlighting that the poverty impact of competition is mediated by
complementary policies and institutions (Begazo et al., 2017; OECD, 2023). Empirically, these
studies predominantly rely on linear panel models with country and time fixed effects, sometimes
allowing for interaction terms (for example between competition and financial development or
inequality), but generally treating the coefficients as constant over time.
A more structural strand of the literature employs computable general equilibrium (CGE) mod
els or analytically tractable general-equilibrium frameworks to study how changes in market struc
ture and competition affect poverty through both price and income channels. Early CGE studies

embed competitive or oligopolistic product markets into multi-sector models calibrated to so

changes in markups, or the dismantling of marketing boards on poverty and inequality. More
recently, Ghazzai et al. (2023) develop a vertically differentiated general-equilibrium model with
heterogeneous households who act as consumers, workers, and shareholders, and compare poverty
outcomes under monopoly and duopoly. They show that the effect of increased competition on
poverty depends on the poverty line, the distribution of preferences and productivity, and, cru
cially, the ownership structure. Their calibration and simulation exercises illustrate cases where
moving from monopoly to duopoly is poverty-reducing and others where it is poverty-increasing,
highlighting the importance of context. In their empirical companion analysis, they estimate static
panel regressions of poverty headcounts at multiple lines on measures of concentration and macro
controls, finding suggestive evidence that lower concentration tends to reduce poverty but with
substantial heterogeneity across income groups and regions.
Across these strands, two methodological features stand out. First, poverty and competition
are almost always modeled as discrete-time variables observed annually or at similar frequencies,
and the effect of competition on poverty is assumed to be linear and time-invariant (conditional
on observables). Even when dynamics are introduced through a small number of lags or through
error-correction terms, the structure is highly constrained and does not allow for rich, time-varying
effects. Second, the link between competition and poverty is predominantly estimated in a scalar
on-scalar framework, in which a single measure of competition at date t (or t − 1) is related to a
single poverty statistic at date t, without modeling the joint evolution of their trajectories. As a
result, existing empirical approaches are ill-equipped to capture the possibility that competitive
reforms are initially poverty-increasing but eventually poverty-reducing, that their effects propa
gate over several years, and that the shape of this propagation mechanism differs systematically
across development and financial regimes.
By contrast, the approach we pursue in this paper is explicitly dynamic and trajectory-based.
We treat competition and poverty as smoothly evolving processes and use a functional economet
ric framework to estimate a time-varying and lagged relationship between them, while allowing
this relationship to differ across clusters of countries defined by their financial-development paths.
In doing so, we build on the economic competition–poverty literature reviewed above, while in
troducing tools that are better suited to capture the long-run, state-dependent dynamics that
theoretical and policy discussions emphasize but standard empirical models largely abstract from.
Specifically, our framework allows us to trace how the impact of competition on poverty changes
over the course of reforms, to quantify the horizon and intensity of lagged effects, and to compare
these patterns systematically across low-, middle-, and high–financial-development regimes. The
resulting evidence provides a richer characterization of the competition–poverty nexus and clarifies
the conditions under which pro-competitive reforms are most likely to be poverty-reducing.

3. Data
In this paper we employ an annual panel dataset spanning the years 1991 to 2017 and covering
a broad set of developing and developed countries. The empirical analysis is based on macroeco
nomic indicators and competition metrics constructed to examine the relationship between market
structure and poverty incidence at the national level.
The dependent variable in this study is the poverty headcount ratio, measured at three inter
national poverty lines: $1.90, $3.20, and $5.50 per day (in 2011 PPP-adjusted dollars). These
indicators, drawn from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI), represent the
proportion of the population living below the specified income thresholds and serve to quantify
extreme, moderate, and upper-bound poverty across countries and years.
The primary explanatory variable is the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI), a standard measure
of market concentration derived from firm-level sales or value-added data. Higher values of the
HHI indicate more concentrated markets, i.e., lower levels of competition. In the context of
this study, the HHI is aggregated at the national level using firm data sourced from Orbis and
Worldscope databases. The index captures the overall structure of competition in each economy
and is assumed to reflect the potential for firms to exercise market power.
To control for macroeconomic confounding factors that might influence poverty levels, we include
a set of standard covariates. These are: log GDP per capita (as a proxy for average income),
trade openness (exports plus imports as a share of GDP), inflation rate (measured as the annual
percentage change in the Consumer Price Index), government consumption as a percentage of
GDP, market capitalization (stock market capitalization over GDP), and annual GDP growth.
All variables are sourced from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, except for the
HHI, which is computed from firm-level databases. Table 1 summarizes the key variables used,
along with their definitions and data sources.

s affects poverty at time t. Such an approach would permit detection of immediate, delayed or
persistent effects, as well as variation in magnitude and sign of the effect over the full time horizon.

Models described above have gained increasing traction in economics as many economic pro
cesses naturally evolve over continuous time and exhibit smooth dynamics that are poorly captured
by discrete or static models. Applications span a wide spectrum of economic domains. For ex
ample, FDA has been used to analyze lifetime income trajectories, enabling researchers to study
heterogeneous earnings paths and long-run mobility patterns rather than relying on cross-sectional
income snapshots (Ecker et al., 2022). In financial econometrics, functional methods have been

smooth temporal variation and structural patterns that traditional GARCH-type models overlook
(Mu ̈ller et al., 2011). FDA has also proven valuable for term-structure modeling: functional dy

interpretation of interest-rate movements (Hays et al., 2012). Moreover, functional approaches
have been used to investigate income inequality dynamics, where hybrid models and functional
representations of income evolution allow the separation of persistent inequality from transient
shocks across the life cycle (Scott and Handcock, 2005).
Importantly, FDA is particularly well suited to our application, where both poverty and compe

model the effect of competition on poverty not as a fixed scalar coefficient, but as a function that

4.3. Market capitalization profiles-based clustering and clusters characteristics. An
important feature of the empirical strategy in Ghazzai et al. (2023) is the stratification of countries
based on their financial market structure. In particular, the authors classify countries according to
their average market capitalization over the 1991–2017 period, thereby distinguishing economies
with deep capital markets from those with bank-based or less developed financial systems. This
categorization is interpreted as a proxy for differences in ownership structure, which is theorized
to condition the impact of competition on poverty.
To account for potential heterogeneity, the authors split the sample into two subsamples: coun
tries with high market capitalization and countries with low market capitalization, using a data
driven threshold for the average MCAP. Separate regressions are then estimated for each group
to examine how the competition–poverty relationship varies across different financial regimes.
To explore potential heterogeneity in the competition–poverty relationship across countries with
different financial market structures, we applied an unsupervised classification method to the
smoothed market capitalization trajectories over the period 1991–2017. Specifically, we imple
mented a functional k-means clustering algorithm on the market capitalization curves obtained
via penalized B-spline smoothing. This approach allows us to group countries based on the entire
temporal profile of their financial development, rather than relying on static or arbitrarily defined
thresholds (as is the case in Ghazzai et al. (2023)). The optimal number of clusters was determined
to be three, based on visual inspection, interpretability, and stability of the resulting groups.

groups of countries that differ systematically in the depth and maturity of their financial markets

5. Empirical results
5.1. Benchmark Model: Static Regression Framework (Ghazzai et al. (2023)). Before
introducing the our results based on functional regression models, it is useful to recall the bench
mark empirical framework adopted by Ghazzai et al. (2023).
Their analysis investigates how market competition, financial development, and their interaction
affect poverty levels across countries and over time, using a panel data approach with country and
time fixed effects.
The estimated baseline model takes the form:
(5.1) Povit = αi + λt + β1 HHIit + β2 MCAPit + β3 (HHIit × AVMCAPi) + γ′Zit + εit,
where AVMCAPi is the average MCAP for country i calculated over the period of study, Zit is
a vector of control variables including GDP per capita, government expenditure, trade openness,
inflation, and output growth.
The estimation is conducted using panel fixed effects with robust standard errors clustered
at the country level. The coefficients β1, β2, and β3 capture, respectively, the direct effects of
competition and financial development, and their joint interaction on poverty.

Across the three poverty thresholds ($1.9, $3.2, and $5.5), Ghazzai et al. (2023) report the
following main empirical results:
(1) The coefficient on market concentration (β1) is positive and statistically significant at the
lower poverty thresholds, implying that greater competition (lower HHI) is associated with
lower poverty. This suggests a pro-poor role of competition at the extensive margin.
(2) The coefficient on financial development (β2) is negative and significant, confirming that
deeper financial markets are associated with lower poverty rates.
(3) The interaction term (β3) is negative and significant for the lowest poverty line ($1.9), but
weaker at higher thresholds. This indicates that the poverty-reducing effect of competition
is amplified in countries with more developed financial systems.
(4) Control variables behave as expected: higher GDP per capita and trade openness are
negatively associated with poverty, while inflation and government expenditure show mixed
effects depending on the poverty threshold.
Overall, these results suggest that both competition and financial development contribute to
poverty reduction, and that their interaction has a reinforcing effect, particularly for extreme
poverty. However, the static specification in equation (5.1) assumes constant coefficients over
time, thus providing only average effects across the entire sample period. In contrast, our func
tional regression framework introduced below relaxes this assumption by allowing the effects of
competition, financial development, and their interaction to vary smoothly over time.
5.2. Analysis of the dynamic contemporaneous effect of competition on poverty. Fig

coefficients close to zero reflect an economically insignificant contemporaneous association.

strongest magnitude in the early 2000s and gradual convergence toward zero thereafter. This

veloping and emerging economies, where liberalization and intensified competition initially led to
firm exits, labor market disruptions, and short-run welfare losses before efficiency gains were real
ized. The temporal attenuation of this negative effect indicates a gradual adjustment as economies
adapted to more open and competitive environments.
The heterogeneity across clusters is economically meaningful. In the medium-MCAP cluster
(green dashed line), the slope fluctuates around zero and becomes slightly positive after 2000,

In the high-MCAP cluster (blue dashed line), encompassing financially mature economies such as

6. Conclusion
This paper introduces a functional econometric framework to analyze how market competition
affects poverty dynamics over time and across heterogeneous financial systems. By treating both
poverty and competition as continuous trajectories, our approach departs from conventional panel
models that assume constant parameters and contemporaneous effects. The function-on-function
regression models reveal rich temporal structures and lagged dependencies that would be obscured
under static specifications.
Three main insights emerge. First, the impact of competition on poverty is inherently time
varying and nonlinear. Stronger competition initially coincides with higher poverty—reflecting
short-run adjustment costs from liberalization—but becomes poverty-reducing once economies
adapt and efficiency gains diffuse. Second, financial development conditions the competition–poverty
relationship. In low-MCAP economies, where markets are shallow and access to credit limited,
competition exerts strong pro-poor effects once structural bottlenecks ease. In contrast, in high
MCAP economies, where financial inclusion and safety nets are already mature, the marginal role
of competition is negligible. Third, the interaction between competition and financial development
is positive and dynamic, confirming that deeper financial markets magnify the poverty-reducing
impact of competition, especially during phases of rapid financial expansion.
Our approach allows the uncovering of smooth, time-localized effects, to identify when policy
reforms exert their greatest influence, and to disentangle short-run disruptions from long-run struc
tural benefits. In the context of the competition–poverty nexus, this framework demonstrates that
policy evaluation should not rely on static averages but on evolving temporal responses. Future
research could extend this work by integrating functional quantile regression to assess distribu
tional effects across income groups, or by embedding spatial functional dependence to capture
cross-country diffusion in competition and welfare dynamics. Overall, the evidence suggests that
competition, when supported by financial deepening and institutional strengthening, can be a
powerful instrument for inclusive and sustained poverty alleviation.
