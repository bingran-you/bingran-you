Varieties of Lines in 3-Space
Benjamin Hollering, Elia Mazzucchelli,
Matteo Parisi, and Bernd Sturmfels
Abstract
We consider configurations of lines in 3-space with incidences prescribed by a graph. This defines a subvariety in a product of Grassmannians. Leveraging a connection with rigidity theory in the plane, for any graph, we determine the dimension of the incidence variety and characterize when it is irreducible or a complete intersection. We study its multidegree and the family of Schubert problems it encodes. Our spanning-tree coordinates enable efficient symbolic computations. We also provide numeri-
cal irreducible decompositions for incidence varieties with up to eight lines. These constructions with lines play a key role in the Landau analysis of scattering amplitudes in particle physics.
1 Introduction
Lines in complex projective 3-space P3 are given by points A = (a12 : a13 : a14 : a23 : a24 : a34) in the Grassmannian Gr(2, 4), which is the hypersurface in P5 defined by the Plu ̈cker quadric
a12a34 − a13a24 + a14a23 = 0 . (1)
The Pl ̈ucker coordinates aij are the 2 × 2 minors of any 2 × 4 matrix A whose rows span the line. If B is a second line, also given by a 2 × 4 matrix B, then we consider the bilinear form
AB := det A
B = a12b34 − a13b24 + a14b23 + a23b14 − a24b13 + a34b12 . (2)
This expression is zero if and only if lines A and B intersect in P3. Note that (1) is AA = 0. A configuration of l lines in P3 is a point in the product Gr(2, 4)l. In this paper we study subvarieties of Gr(2, 4)l that are defined by the equations (2) for prescribed pairs of lines.
Example 1.1 (l = 3). The 12-dimensional variety Gr(2, 4)3 ⊂ (P5)3 encodes triples of lines. We work in the polynomial ring C[A, B, C] in the 18 variables aij, bij, cij modulo the Plu ̈cker ideal ⟨AA, BB, CC⟩. In this quotient ring, the ideal ⟨AB, BC⟩ is prime. Its variety parametrizes 3-chains of lines in P3. On the other hand, the ideal ⟨AB, AC, BC⟩ is radical but not prime. It is the intersection of two prime ideals I[3] and I∗
[3]. Here, I[3] represents
triples of concurrent lines, while I∗
[3] represents triples of coplanar lines. Each of these two associated primes has ten additional generators of degree three. These ten cubics are written in [16, Theorem 3.1] as 3 × 3 determinants, where the entries are linear forms in A, B, C.

Proposition 2.6. The primary decomposition of IG is obtained by homogenizing that of IeG.
Proof and Discussion. In our setting, homogenization is the following process. For each line A, we write the equations that relate Plu ̈cker coordinates and affine coordinates as follows:
a23 + α1a12, a24 + α2a12, a13 − α3a12, a14 − α4a12, a34 − α1α4a12 + α2α3a12 . (11)
To homogenize an ideal in C[α, β, γ, . . .], we add these 5l polynomials to it, we next saturate by the degree l Plu ̈cker monomial a12b12c12 · · · , and we finally eliminate the 4l unknowns α, β, γ, . . .. The result is a homogeneous ideal in the Plu ̈cker coordinate ring C[A, B, C, . . .]. Conversely, let J be any Zl-homogeneous ideal in C[A, B, C, . . .] that contains the ideal of Gr(2, 4)l. Suppose that none of the variables a12, b12, c12, . . . is a zero divisor modulo J. The
dehomogenization Je is an ideal in C[α, β, γ, . . .]. If we now homogenize Je via (11) then we recover the original ideal J. This holds because a12b12c12 · · · is not a zero divisor modulo J. Morever, J is prime (resp. primary) if and only if Je is prime (resp. primary).

3 Multidegrees
The homogeneous coordinate ring of (P5)l is the polynomial ring C[A1, A2, . . . , Al], where Ai is the vector of Plu ̈cker coordinates for the line i. This ring has a natural Zl-grading. Each of the six entries of Ai has degree ei, which is the ith standard basis vector of Zl. All our ideals describe subschemes of Gr(2, 4)l and they are homogeneous in this Zl-grading. Let G be a graph with l vertices as before, and let c = codim(VG). The incidence variety VG is a subvariety of dimension d := 4l − -
c in Gr(2, 4)l. Fix u ∈ Nl with u1 + · · · + ul = d. We denote by Gu the graph that is obtained from G by attaching ui pendant edges at the vertex i, for all i ∈ [l]. The augmented graph Gu has l + d vertices and |G| + d edges. We consider the map ψu : VGu → Gr(2, 4)d that deletes the l original lines. The fiber of ψu over a general point in Gr(2, 4)d is given by d constraints on a d-dimensional variety. Each constraint imposes a Schubert condition on one of the l lines represented by G. We expect-
 the fiber to consist of finitely many points. We refer to these points as the leading singularities of the graph Gu. This name is a reference to Landau analysis for scattering amplitudes in particle physics (see Section 8). We define the LS degree of Gu to be the cardinality of the generic fiber of ψu if this cardinality is finite. We set it to 0 otherwise. In this definition, the cardinality of the fiber is understood with multiplicity; see Remark 3.6.

7 Six, Seven and Eight Lines
We now return to the classification of the incidence varieties VG. In Section 2 we computed all of them for l ≤ 5. We quickly saw that the structure of the ideals IG can be quite rich and interesting. The combinatorics of the multidegrees in Section 3 further underscores this. This section is dedicated to much harder computations. We compute the incidence varieties for graphs with up to l = 8 vertices. This involves polynomials in 24, 28, and 32 variables in the affine coordinates from Section 2. -
We seek to compute the dimension, degree, and primary decomposition of IG. For most graphs G with l ≥ 7, this is intractable with symbolic computation, even if we leverage the spanning tree coordinates in Section 6. We instead use tools from numerical algebraic geometry (cf. [3]). This allows us to compute a numerical irreducible decomposition for all connected graphs up to l = 7 and all connected triangle-free graphs up to l = 8. Our findings are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

8 Towards Physics
This project started from a discussion about scattering amplitudes in particle physics. In this section we explain the set-up to algebraically-minded readers who are not familiar with theoretical physics. It serves as a warm welcome to our forthcoming physics paper [12]. The determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix is a quadratic form on the vector space R4. This quadratic form has signature (2, 2). To emphasize this, we use the notation R2,2 for the space R4. The (2, 2) signature was made manifest in (15), -
which was our point of entry to rigidity theory. Viewing elements of R2,2 as point pairs allowed us to harness the Geiringer-Laman Theorem. The Grassmannian Gr(2, 4) serves as the natural compactification (Lemma 4.1). The setting in physics is similar, but the role of the space R2,2 is now played by Minkowski spacetime R1,3. We need to replace the signature (2, 2) with the Lorentzian signature (1, 3).
This is accomplished by a coordinate change which uses i = √−1. The keyword for this transformation is Wick rotation. Namely, we replace the 2 × 4 matrices A and B in (9) with

We now take a step back and take a quick look at the larger scientific landscape. Particle physics studies fundamental interactions among elementary particles in nature. A scattering experiment consists in making n particles with momenta P, Q, . . . ∈ R1,3 collide in a particle accelerator, such as the Large Hadron Collider at CERN. By repeating the experiment many times, experimental physicists measure the joint probability for different outcomes as a function of the momenta. This probability can-
 be obtained as the square modulus of its probability amplitude, called scattering amplitude. Theoretical physicists use the framework of Quantum Field Theory (QFT) to compute such scattering amplitudes, both to compare with scattering experiments and to advance the mathematical understanding of QFT itself. Scattering amplitudes are computed by summing Feynman integrals [19]. These are functions of the momenta P, Q, . . . ∈ R1,3 and are expressed as integrals of rational functions in 4l variables.-
 Their arguments are l loop momenta K, L, . . . ∈ R1,3. That rational function in 4l variables is singular when some linear combinations of momenta and loop momenta is light-like separated. This is where Lemma 8.1 comes in. In the geometric approach we pursue, the n momenta P, Q, . . . and the l loop momenta K, L, . . . are lines in 3-space, given by points in the Grassmannian Gr(2, 4). Light-like separation means that two lines intersect. The rational functions to be integrated have denominators -
that are products of quadrics like PgQ in (29). The relation between the singular locus of these integrands and the singularities of Feynman integrals and scattering amplitudes is the subject of the Landau analysis. Recent work in [6] connects Landau analysis to computational algebraic geometry. However, their geometry looks disturbingly different from ours. This is a feature, not a bug. By [19, Section 2.5], there many different ways to write a Feynman integral. Ours closely relates to the moment-
um representation, while Fevola et al. [6] use the Lee-Pomeransky representation. The article [12] will develop Landau analysis in the Grassmannian. This is about functions on varieties of lines in 3-space. For a physical theory known as planar N = 4 super Yang-Mills (sYM), one can write the sum over all Feynman integrals for fixed l as a single integral over a rational function in l loop momenta. When expressed in terms of lines in P3, the rational function is conjectured to be the canonical form-
 of a positive geometry called amplituhedron [1]. Boundaries of amplituhedra are closely related to line incidence varieties. Therefore our work is also connected with the fields of total positivity and positive geometry. Every graph G and every u ∈ Nd specifies a Schubert problem. This was described in Section 3. Given n = 4l − c lines, we seek l lines which intersect each other and the given lines according to Gu. The number of solutions is the LS degree. The solution is an algebraic function of-
 the data. Usually, these cannot be written in radicals when the LS degree is ≥ 5. The graphs Gu are closely related to Feynman diagrams [19]. According to Corollary 8.2, edges in Gu indicate which pairs of momenta are light-like separated. Variants of the incidence variety VGu can be found in the physics literature. The recent article [10] by Hannesdottir et al. uses the term on-shell space and attributes the concept to work of Pham in the 1960s. The projection in [10, equation (5.4)] corresponds-
 to our map ψu in Section 3. In physics, one is interested in special loci in the image space Gr(2, 4)d of the map ψu. This has two aspects. First, there is the branch locus. This consists of all points in Gr(2, 4)d over which the fiber of ψu does not consist of LS degree many distinct points. The equation
