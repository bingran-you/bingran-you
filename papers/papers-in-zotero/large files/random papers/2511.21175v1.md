The Pseudocentre of a Group1
M. Brescia – B. Di Siena – E. Ingrosso – M. Trombetti
Abstract
In 1973, Jim Wiegold introduced the concept of pseudocentre P(G) of a group G as the intersection of the normal closures of the centralizers of its elements. He proved that the pseudocentre of a non-trivial finite group is always non-trivial, giving a new variable on which one can use induction in finite group theory. In the same paper, Wiegold states that no obvious relations seem to hold between the pseudocentre and the “canonical” characteristic subgroups of a group. The aim of this work is to -
show that the pseudocentre is indeed much more involved in the structure of an arbitrary group then anyone could have expected. For example, we prove that a soluble group coincides with its pseudocentre if and only if it is abelian (see Corollary 2.27), and that the structure of the commutator subgroup strongly influences the structure of the pseudocentre (see Theorem 2.14 and its corollaries). And this is not the end of the story. In fact, the behaviour of the pseudocentre in arbitrary (possibly -
infinite) groups can be extremely wild: sometimes it is very difficult even to understand whether the pseudocentre is trivial or not. This wilderness is exampled by some of our main results (see the introduction for a complete list):
• There exists a polycyclic group of Hirsch length 3 in which the pseudocentre is trivial (see Example 3.2).
• The pseudocentre of the group of unitriangular matrices over any field is the largest term of the upper central series that is abelian (see Theorem 4.2).
• Free products have a trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 6.8), but there exist amalgamated free products of non-trivial groups coinciding with their pseudocentre.
• Weakly regular branch groups have a trivial pseudocentre, so, in particular, Grigorchuk groups have trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 8.8). Note also that most free Burnside groups have a trivial pseudocentre as well (see Theorem 6.16).
• The pseudocentre of the Thompson group is the derived subgroup (see Theorem 9.3).
• Wreath products can have a totally arbitrary pseudocentre (see Chapter 5). In fact, using wreath products we can construct a non-trivial group G which is isomorphic to both its pseudocentre P(G) and the factor G/P(G) (see Theorem 5.26).
Finally, we remark that the most beautiful thing about this is that in order to prove many of the previous results, one needs techniques ranging from many different realms of mathematics. For example, the pseudocentre of PSL(2, Z) is computed by using Pell’s equations and Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kind, while Example 3.2 allows us to state a conjecture about the existence of certain Fibonacci numbers in terms of the pseudocentre of the group. Note that we have explicitly stated-
 all relevant open problems in our work.

Relevant definitions
Let X and Y be group classes, G a group, π a set of primes, and p a prime. We define the X-radical of G as the product of all normal X-subgroups of G. For example, if p is any prime, then Op(G) is the radical with respect to the property of being a p-group. Now, G is:
• X-by-Y if it contains a normal subgroup N ∈ X such that G/N ∈ Y. For example, if X is the class of all abelian groups, and Y is the class of all finite groups, then G is X-by-Y if and only if it is abelian-by-finite, that is, if it contains a normal abelian subgroup of finite index.
• divisible if it is abelian and the equation xn = g is solvable for every g ∈ G and n ∈ Z.
• Cˇ ernikov if it is abelian-by-finite and satisfies the minimal condition on subgroups. This is equivalent to requiring that G has an abelian subgroup of finite index which is the product of finitely many Prüfer groups (this is the divisible radical of G).
• polycyclic if it is soluble and satisfies the maximal condition on subgroups.
• Dedekind if all its subgroups are normal, and Hamiltonian if it is Dedekind and not abelian.
• a π-group (resp. a p-group) if all its elements x are π-elements (resp. p-elements), that is, o(x) ∈ π (resp. o(x) = pn for some positive integer n).
• an FC-group if all its elements have finitely many conjugates.
• is a BFC-group if its elements have boundedly many finite conjugates; note that a wellknown theorem of B.H. Neumann characterizes the BFC-groups as those groups with a finite commutator subgroup.
• hypercyclic if it has an ascending normal series with cyclic factors; of course, every supersoluble group is hypercyclic.
• hypercentral if it has an ascending central series; of course, every hypercentral group is hypercyclic.
• Z-group if it has a central series, that is, a chain S of subgroup connecting {1} to G in which [K, G] ⩽ H for every pair (H, K) of consecutive subgroups of S.
• locally X if every finitely generated subgroup of G is contained in an X-subgroup. If the X is closed with respect to forming subgroups, then this is equivalent to requiring that all finitely generated subgroups are X-group. Thus, for example, G is locally nilpotent if and only if its finitely generated subgroups are nilpotent.

• a Baer group if its cyclic subgroups are subnormal; the Baer radical is defined as the largest normal subgroup that is a Baer group (note that a well-known theorem assures that the product of normal Baer subgroups is still a Baer subgroup).
• a Fitting group if it coincides with its Fitting radical, that is, the product of all the nilpotent normal subgroups of G.
• residually X if the intersection of the normal subgroups N with G/N ∈ X is trivial. The intersection of all normal subgroups with X-quotient is usually called the X-residual of G. Clearly, it is not always the case that the quotient by the X-residual is an X-group. • minimal non-X if G ̸∈ X but all its proper subgroups are X-groups.
• locally graded if every non-trivial finitely generated subgroup has a proper subgroup of finite index.
• just-infinite if every non-trivial normal subgroup of G has finite index.
• of Hirsch length n if there is a finite series

in which the factors are either periodic or infinite cyclic, and there are precisely n factors of the latter type.
Moreover, if H is a subgroup of G, then we say that:
• H is a Sylow π-subgroup (resp. Sylow p-subgroup) of G if it is a maximal element of the set of all π-subgroups (resp. p-subgroups) of G.
• H is subnormal in G if there is a finite series

• H is ascendant in G if there is an ascending series
H = H0 ⊴ H1 ⊴ . . . Hα ⊴ Hα+1 ⊴ . . . Hn = G
connecting H and G. Clearly, if H is subnormal, then it is also ascendant.
• H is G-invariant if it is normal in G.
• H is the X-radical of G if H is the product of all normal X-subgroups of G; in particular, if X is the class of locally nilpotent groups (resp. Baer groups, nilpotent groups), then we obtain the Hirsch–Plotkin radical (resp. Baer radical, Fitting radical).

1. Introduction and main results
Let G be a group. The pseudocentre P(G) of G is the intersection of the normal closures of the centralizers of the elements of G, that is,

This is a characteristic subgroup of G introduced by Jim Wiegold in [60]. In that paper, Wiegold proved that the pseudocentre of a non-trivial finite group is always non-trivial, a remarkable result that allows for a new variable on which to make induction arguments in proving results for finite groups. Clearly, P(G) always contains the centre Z(G) of G, but is also somewhat connected to the derived subgroup by its very definition. Despite this fact, in [60], Wiegold states that no obvious relatio-
ns seem to hold between the pseudocentre and the “canonical” characteristic subgroups. The aim of this work is to show that the pseudocentre is actually much more involved in the structure of an arbitrary group (and in some other algebraic problems) than anyone could have guessed. This is achieved in three parts. In the first part, we prove some general results connecting the pseudocentre with other “canonical” characteristic subgroups. Although some of the main results in this part do not have a -
very long proof, they are quite surprising in many respects, and have many relevant and non-obvious consequences. In fact, the real difficulty about the pseudocentre is discerning what is true from what is false. Here, we summarize the main structural results we have obtained:
• The pseudocentre contains every minimal normal subgroup (see Theorem 2.1). In particular, groups satisfying the minimal condition on normal subgroups have a non-trivial pseudocentre (see Corollary 2.3). Moreover, the pseudocentre contains the square of any infinite cyclic normal subgroup (see Lemma 3.7).
• Let A be a normal abelian subgroup of a group G. If g has order n modulo CG(A), then An ⩽ CG(g)G (see Lemma 3.9). As a consequence, if G is periodic, then P(G) contains every normal divisible abelian subgroup (see Corollary 3.12), and a further consequence shows that every nilpotent-by-finite group has a non-trivial pseudocentre (see Corollary 3.11). In particular, every ˇCernikov group has a non-trivial pseudocentre (it actually is “pseudonilpotent”), as well as CC-groups (see Theorem 3.15).
• Any group inducing a Prüfer group of automorphisms on an abelian normal subgroup has a non-trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 3.19). As a consequence, every soluble minimal non-nilpotent group and every group of Heieneken–Mohamed type have a non-trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 3.20 and Corollary 3.21).
• The pseudocentre of the direct product is the direct product of the pseudocentres (see Lemma 2.6). Also, it turns out that the class of groups coinciding with their pseudocentre is local (see Theorem 2.25).
• Let G be a group. Then P(G)′ ⩽ G′′ and any G′-central section of G is also centralized by P(G) (see Theorem 2.14). This is probably one of the most non-obvious relations between the pseudocentre and the commutator subgroup. It implies for example that: (1) if G is soluble and G = P(G), then G is abelian; (2) if G′ is hypercentral, then so is also P(G)

(see Corollary 2.18); and that (3) finite groups with cyclic Sylow subgroups (for example finite groups of square-free order) have a cyclic pseudocentre (see Corollary 2.20).
• Let H be a subgroup of a group G. If H has a central supplement in G, then P(H) is contained in P(G) (this is something that rarely occurs) and actually has a central supplement in P(G) (see Theorem 2.23).
• Let G be a group. If G = P(G) and G′ is finite, then G/Z(G) is finite (see Theorem 2.31). This shows that the converse of the celebrated Schur’s theorem holds in the context of groups coinciding with their pseudocentre.
In the second part of the work, we focus on computing the pseudocentre of certain relevant examples of groups and constructions. In this part, we show that sometimes the pseudocentre coincides with certain very well-known subgroups (thus offering an alternative description of these subgroups) and at other times it allows to describe new features of the groups in question. We should also note that this is the (most technically difficult) part of the work in which many techniques from different real-
ms of mathematics are employed, and which shows how the pseudocentre is related to other types of problems, for example in number theory.
• The pseudocentre of a finite symmetric group is the alternating group, while infinite symmetric groups coincide with their pseudocentre (see Theorem 2.9).
• There exists a polycyclic group of Hirsch length 3 with trivial pseudocentre (see Example 3.2). This example allows us to re-state a conjecture about the existence of certain Fibonacci numbers in terms of the pseudocentres of its finite quotients (see Remark 3.3).
• The Baumslag–Solitar group BS(1, n), with |n| ⩾ 2, has a trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 3.4).
• The pseudocentre of the group of all unitriangular matrices over any field is the largest term of the upper central series that is abelian (see Theorem 4.2). More generally, the pseudocentre of the McLain’s groups over any field and any linearly ordered poset is computed in Theorems 4.2 and 4.4. In particular, such groups coincide with their pseudocentre if and only if their associated poset is dense (see Corollary 4.5). Applications of this construction are used to produce strange examples of g-
roups with various types of properties in connection with the pseudocentre (see Examples 2.12, 4.6, 4.7 and 4.8).
• The pseudocentre of wreath products is investigated in Chapter 5. It is proved for example that the wreath product of infinitely many groups often coincides with its pseudocentre (see Theorem 5.1), obtaining (among other results) that every group can be subnormally embedded in a group that coincides with its pseudocentre (see Corollary 5.2). On the other hand, it turns out that the pseudocentre of a wreath product of two groups can be rather arbitrary. This is exemplified by the pseudocentre of -
wreath products of the form H wr Alt(n) (see Theorems 5.15 and 5.17), H wr Sym(n) (see Theorem 5.22), and H ≀ K, where K is infinite cyclic (see Theorems 5.30 and 5.26). In particular, Theorem 5.26 provides an example of a group in which the pseudocentre is isomorphic to the whole group, while Theorem 5.30 shows that the lamplighter group has a trivial pseudocentre.
• The pseudocentre of the Rubik’s cube group and of the symmetry group of the Rubik’s cube obtained by disassembling and reassembling is computed in Remark 5.24, partially as a consequence of our results on the pseudocentre of wreath products.
• The pseudocentre of a non-dihedral free product is trivial (see Theorem 6.8). In particular, free groups have a trivial pseudocentre. Note that some of the proofs here use

• In Chapter 7, we compute the pseudocentre of the (affine) general linear group, the (affine) special linear group, and of some of their relevant quotients.
• The free Burnside group B(m, n), where m is any cardinal number ⩾ 2 and n is an odd integer ≫ 1, has a trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 6.16).
• The full automorphism group of an infinite rooted tree has a trivial pseudocentre (see Theorem 8.4), and the same is true for every weakly regular branch group (see Theorem 8.8). • The pseudocentre of the Thompson group and of the groups of all orientation-preserving piecewise linear homeomorphisms over any open set is the commutator subgroup (see Theorem 9.10).
The third and final part of the work is devoted to the study of a couple of well-known problems that always come up in the literature when a new relevant characteristic subgroup or property is defined:
• the inverse problem, that is, understanding which groups (up-to-isomorphism) are allowed to be the pseudocentre of a group (see Chapter 11).
• the minimality problem, that is, understanding the structure of groups in which all proper subgroups coincide with their pseudocentre (see Chapter 10).
We hope that this work will serve as a basic reference in the subject area, as a text for postgraduate studies, and also as a source of new research ideas. This is in fact the reason why we have highlighted the most relevant problems in this new subject, the solution of which will indubitably offer new, precious insights on the role played by the pseudocentre in the structure of a group, and on many other related problems in group theory and in algebra as a whole.

2. The pseudocentre
The aim of this section is to study the pseudocentre of an arbitrary group. Our first basic result is a huge generalization of the main result of [60], which actually provides a very short and easy proof of the same result.
Theorem 2.1 Let G be a group. If N is a minimal normal subgroup of G, then N ⩽ P(G).
Proof — Let g ∈ G. If CG(g)G ∩ N = {1}, then [CG(g)G, N] = {1}, so N ⩽ CG(g) ⩽ CG(g)G, a
contradiction. Therefore, N is always contained in CG(g)G. The arbitrariness of g yields that N ⩽ P(G) and completes the proof. ⊓⊔
Corollary 2.2 Let G be a group. If N is a non-trivial normal subgroup of G satisfying the minimal condition on G-invariant subgroups, then N ∩ P(G) ̸= {1}.
Corollary 2.3 Let G be a non-trivial group satisfying the minimal condition on normal subgroups. Then P(G) is not trivial. In particular, every non-trivial finite group has a non-trivial pseudocentre.
Remark 2.4 Actually, Wiegold’s proof of Corollary 2.3 relies on the fact that the intersection of finitely many normal closures of centralizers of elements of an arbitrary non-trivial group G is not trivial. This is easily proved by induction using the fact that the normal closure of the centralizer of an element g of G cannot have trivial intersection with any non-trivial normal subgroup N of G (otherwise N would centralize g and N ⩽ CG(g)G).
Remark 2.5 It is not possible to replace normality with subnormality in Theorem 2.1: in fact, the pseudocentre of the dihedral group of order 8 coincides with the centre.
Corollary 2.3 has a couple of relevant special cases: groups with finitely many normal subgroups, and groups with finitely many conjugacy classes of elements. The relevance of these classes of groups stems from the results and constructions in [32],[47] — it is, in fact, possible to prove that any torsion-free group can be embedded in a group with only two conjugacy classes of elements.
Now, we describe the basic facts and closure properties of the pseudocentre.

Lemma 2.7 Let N be a group and K a group of automorphisms of N. If K and N are simple, the following conditions are equivalent:
(i) For every h ∈ N there is a non-trivial k ∈ K such that hk = h.
(ii) G = K ⋉ N is a pseudocentral group.
Proof — Clearly, (ii) implies (i). Assume (i). Let g be an element of G, write g = kx with k ∈ K and x ∈ N, and put C = CG(g)G. By Theorem 2.1, N ⩽ C so if k is not trivial, we obtain G = C. On the other hand, if k = 1, by (i) there is a non-trivial element h ∈ K centralizing g. It follows that K ⩽ ⟨h⟩G ⩽ C and again G = C. ⊔⊓

Proof — This follows at once from the fact that CG(g)N/N ⩽ CG/N(gN) for every g ∈ G. ⊔⊓
It turns out that the pseudocentre of a group is not necessarily pseudocentral. In fact, the pseudocentre of the symmetric group of degree 4 is the alternating group of degree 4, whose pseudocentre has order 4.
Theorem 2.9 (1) For every n ⩾ 3, we have that P Sym(n) = Alt(n).
(2) For any infinite set I, Sym(I) is pseudocentral.
(3) For any infinite set I, FSym(I) is pseudocentral.
Proof — (1) Let P be the pseudocentre of G = Sym(n). If n = 4, then P = Alt(4) because every element of the Klein subgroup of Alt(4) is centralized by some odd permutation. Assume n > 4. If n is odd, then X = ⟨(1 . . . n)⟩ is self-centralizing and so P ⩽ CG(X)G = XG ⩽ Alt(n). If n is even, then X = ⟨(1 . . . n − 1)⟩ is self-centralizing, and again P ⩽ Alt(n). Since Alt(n) is simple, the result follows from Corollary 2.3.
(2) If x is any element of Sym(I), then either the support of x has the same cardinality of I, or its complement has that cardinality. In any case, the centralizer of x contains elements whose support has cardinality I, and so whose normal closure is Sym(I). The statement follows.
(3) The proof is similar to that of (2). ⊔⊓
Remark 2.10 The consideration of Alt(4) and Sym(4) shows that in a finite soluble group G we do not have N ∩ P(G) ⩽ P(N) for any normal subgroup N of G.

Before studying wreath products in relation to the pseudocentre, we briefly wish to recall one of the most general definitions of wreath product for the reader’s convenience. This is mainly taken from Section 6.2 of [53]. Let Λ be a linearly ordered set. For each λ ∈ Λ, let Hλ be a transitive permutation group acting on the non-empty set Xλ, and fix an element 1λ ∈ Xλ. Let X be the restricted set product of all the Xλ’s, that is, X is the set of all (xλ)λ∈Λ, where xλ ∈ Xλ and xλ ̸= 1λ for finitely-
 many λ’s. Put (1λ) = 1. Two elements x and y of X are said to be λ-equivalent if xμ = yμ for all μ > λ; in this case we write x ≡λ y. Then Hλ acts faithfully as a group of permutations of X as follows. If ξ ∈ Hλ and x ∈ X, then (x)ξ = x if x ̸≡λ 1, otherwise ξ only affects the λ-component of x as ξ would do. The wreath product W = Wrλ∈Λ Hλ of the groups Hλ, λ ∈ Λ, is by definition the group of permutations of X generated by the Hλ’s. Of course, W depends on the groups Hλ and on the way in which H-
λ acts on Xλ but does not depend on the choice of the elements 1λ. Moreover, W acts transitively on X. If Γ and ∆ are subsets of Λ such that γ < δ for all γ ∈ Γ and δ ∈ ∆, we write Γ ≺ ∆. A nonempty subset Γ of Λ is a segment of Λ if for each λ ∈ Λ exactly one of the following conditions holds: λ ∈ Γ , {λ} ≺ Γ , and Γ ≺ {λ}. If Λ ≺ Λ \ Γ (Λ \ Γ ≺ Λ, resp.), then Γ is said to be a lower segment (an upper segment, resp.). A collection {Γi}i∈I of mutually disjoint segments of Λ is said to be a segmen-
tation of Λ if Λ = S
i∈I Γi. If Wi = Wrλ∈Γi Hλ for every i ∈ I, then one can show that W = Wri∈I Wi. If {Γ1, Γ2} is a segmentation of Λ, then we put W = Wri=1,2 Wi = W1 wr W2. It is well-known that the free product of two groups of order 2 is the infinite dihedral group, and it is easy to see that the pseudocentre of the infinite dihedral group is the derived subgroup. We start by recalling some basic definitions and results about regular rooted trees; this is mostly taken from [21] and [22]. Let T be a rooted tree, that is, a tree with a distinguished root vertex r. The set of vertices of the tree is V(T ). The norm of a vertex u is the number of edges in a geodesic path from r to u. Clearly, |u| = 0 if and only if u = r. The cardinality of the set of vertices that are both adjacent to u and of norm |u| + 1 is the degree of u. The tree is spherically homogeneou-
s if the vertices with the same norm have the same degree. The n-th level Vn of T is the set of vertices of T with norm n. A spherically homogeneous tree is determined by its branch index, that is, a sequence m = {mn}N
n=1, where N can be finite or infinite, and mn is the degree of a vertex at level n. The Thompson group F was first introduced by Richard J. Thompson in an unpublished manuscript written in the 60s in order to find a counterexample to the von Neumann conjecture (a group is non-amenable if and only if it contains a countable free subgroup). Since then, the Thompson group has been rediscovered in many different contexts and has attracted a lot of attention for its remarkable properties. For example, F is a torsion-free, finitely presented simple group containing no free subgroups of-
 rank greater than 1. The wider group-theoretical context in which F is studied is that of PLo(R) (or PLF(R)), namely the group of all orientationpreserving piecewise linear homeomorphisms of the real line with only finitely many points of non-differentiability. In this chapter, we study the pseudocentre of a family of relevant subgroups of PLo(R), including F itself. 10. Groups whose proper subgroups are
pseudocentral
Since the class of pseudocentral groups is not closed with respect to forming subgroups, an interest may arise in understanding what a group whose all (proper) subgroups are pseudocentral would look like. This is a very natural question for properties that are not inherited by subgroups, as for example happens for the class of groups in which normality is a transitive relation (see [51] and [52]). As one may expect, it turns out that these kinds of groups mostly resemble minimal non-abelian groups-
. In fact, since every locally graded minimal non-abelian group is metabelian, then every locally graded minimal non-abelian group is a minimal nonpseudocentral group. The following result shows that the converse holds in the universe of periodic groups.
Theorem 10.1 Let G be a locally graded periodic group whose proper subgroups are pseudocentral. Then G is metabelian. In particular, G is either abelian or minimal non-abelian.
Proof — Suppose first that G is finite. We use induction on the order of G to prove that G is metabelian. If H is a proper subgroup of G, then H is metabelian because all proper subgroups of H are pseudocentral. But then H is abelian by Corollary 2.27. Then every proper subgroup of G is abelian, and hence G is either abelian or minimal non-abelian. In any case, G is metabelian. Assume that G is arbitrary periodic. Since the class of metabelian groups is local, we only need to show that G is locall-
y finite. Let H be an infinite finitely generated subgroup of G. Then H/H′′ is finite, so there is a proper H-invariant subgroup K of finite index of H′′. However, the finite case yields that H/K is metabelian and hence that H′′ ⩽ K, a contradiction. ⊔⊓
Question 10.2 Can we get rid of the periodicity assumption in the statement of Theorem 10.1?
We are not able to answer this question even if the whole group is pseudocentral. In fact, as shown by the following consequences of Theorem 10.1, answering the previous question could be a difficult task.
Corollary 10.3 Let G be a locally graded group with an ascending series whose factors are locally (soluble-by-periodic). If all proper subgroups of G are pseudocentral, then G is metabelian. In particular, G is either abelian or minimal non-abelian.
Proof — By the main result of [37], we may assume that G is locally (soluble-by-periodic). As in the proof of Theorem 10.1, we only need to show that G is soluble. If G is finitely generated, then G is soluble-by-periodic, and hence soluble by Theorem 10.1 (and again [37]). If G is not finitely generated, then every finitely generated subgroup of G is soluble pseudocentral, and so even abelian by Corollary 2.27. Consequently, G is abelian. ⊔⊓
Corollary 10.4 Let G be a locally graded group whose commutator subgroup is finitely generated. If all proper subgroups of G are pseudocentral, then G is metabelian. In particular, G is either abelian or minimal non-abelian.
Proof — Suppose G is not abelian. Since G′ is finitely generated and G is locally graded, so there is a proper normal subgroup N of G′ such that G′/N is finite. By Theorem 10.1, G′/N is soluble, and hence G′′ < G′. Since all proper subgroups of G/G′′ are pseudocentral, they are abelian, and hence G/G′′ is minimal non-abelian. Thus, G/G′′ is finite, and G′′ is finitley

11. Pseudo-integrability
Every time a new characteristic subgroup of a group is introduced, the question arises as to which groups can or cannot appear as this subgroup. A simple example in this context is the centre: every abelian group is its own centre, so every abelian group can be the centre of a group. The situation dramatically changes when we consider the derived subgroup: which groups can be derived subgroups of some groups? This problem first appeared in [44], where it was also noted that not all groups can be d-
erived subgroups, and has been investigated (mostly for finite groups) in [2], [3], and [27]. It has been shown for example that every finite abelian group is integrable (that is, can be a derived subgroup of a group), and that every integrable group has a finite integral (that is, a group realizing the integrability). The goal of this chapter is to provide a brief insight into the analogous problem concerning the pseudocentre of a group, which seems to be even more difficult to tackle due to the -
further complication that the pseudocentre contains the centre. Thus, we give the following definition. A group G is said to be pseudo-integrable if there exists a group H such that P(H) ≃ G. Clearly, every pseudocentral group is pseudo-integrable, so in particular every abelian group is pseudo-integrable. As the reader will see, it appears from our results that if a group is pseudo-integrable, then it is also integrable, although the converse does not hold (see Example 11.8), and that most result-
s for pseudo-integrability are analogous to the results for integrability. This justifies a bit the name pseudo-integrability, and naturally leads to the following question.
Question 11.1 Is there any relation between integrable and pseudo-integrable groups?
It has been proved in [2], Theorem 5.1, that a complete group is integrable if and only if it is perfect. Recall that a group G is complete if Aut(G) = Inn(G) and Z(G) = {1}. Our first result gives a general criterion for a complete group to be pseudo-integrable. This criterion will be employed in Example 11.8 to show that there exist integrable groups that are not pseudo-integrable.
Theorem 11.2 Let G be a complete group. Then G is pseudocentral if and only if G is pseudointegrable.
Proof — Clearly, if G is pseudocentral, then it is also pseudo-integrable. Assume P(H) = G for some group H. By Theorem 13.5.8 of [55], H = L × P(H) for some subgroup L of H. Then Lemma 2.6 yields that P(H) is the direct product of the pseudocentres of the direct factors, so P(L) = {1} and P(P(H)) = P(H), as required. ⊔⊓
Corollary 11.3 Sym(n) is not pseudo-integrable for every finite 3 ⩽ n ̸= 6.
Proof — This follows at once from Theorems 2.9 and 11.2. ⊔⊓
