PARTITION-THEORETIC MODEL OF PRIME DISTRIBUTION, II
ROBERT SCHNEIDER
Abstract. In recent work by Botkin, Dawsey, Hemmer, Just and the present author, a deterministic model of prime number distribution is developed based on properties of integer partitions that gives almost exact estimates for π(n), the number of primes less than or equal to positive integer n, up to n = 10,000. In this follow-up paper, the author summarizes the ideas behind this partition-theoretic model of primes and formulates a computational model that is practically exact in its estimates of π(-
n) up to n = 100, 000.
1. Introduction
1.1. Partition model of prime distribution. The sieve of Eratosthenes provides a method to identify prime numbers out of the larger set of positive integers Z+. In carrying out the algorithm, one gets the feeling that the primes P are positioned in the sequence of integers precisely where they need to be in order to generate the rest of Z+ by multiplication. Despite the sense of inevitability associated to the primes, many well-known theorems and conjectures related to prime numbers are very diffi-
cult (or seemingly impossible) to prove. As a way of sidestepping a direct attack on prime number phenomena, mathematicians often use a probabilistic approach based on the prime number theorem. We recall that if π(x) denotes the number of prime numbers less than or equal to x ∈ R+, the classical prime number theorem states that as x → ∞,
(1) π(x) ∼ x
log x ,
where log x denotes the natural logarithm function. Thus the probability π(n)/n that n ∈ Z+ is prime is asymptotically equal to 1/ log n as n increases. A random model of prime distribution might assume the primes are modeled by a random sequence with probability 1/ log n of the nth term being prime, n ≥ 2, or impose similar probabilistic measures on the prime gaps as in Cram ́er’s model (see e.g. [12, 13]). In recent work [2], the author of the present paper and their collaborators develop a dete-
rministic model of prime number distribution based on facts and observations about integer partitions. Inequalities that relate the partition norm [11] and supernorm [5] statistics impose constraints on the distribution of primes. Based on this, the authors of [2] deduce the nth prime gap may be modeled by the relation
(2) pn+1 − pn = 2 d(n)
2,
where d(n) is the divisor function. While this partition model of prime gaps is formulated based on rather heavy-handed simplifying assumptions, it yields a surprisingly accurate estimate of prime distribution. Below, we summarize the model of primes proposed in [2].

In [2], Model 2 is derived from Model 1 by making a simplistic estimate of the missing contribution to prime gaps of partitions having three parts and norms equal to n. The authors of [2] make a comparison of Models 2 and 2*: “Since it involves simpler computations and is evidently more accurate in its estimates of π(n) as n increases, we consider Model 2* to be a better model of primes.” In the following section, we focus on refining the error term (15).
2.2. Refining the estimate of π2(pn−1) in the error term (Model 3). In [2], the authors note that as n increases, “the missing contribution of partitions (and factorizations) of lengths greater than three creeps up, causing the [Model 2 and 2*] estimates to become farther from exact as n increases.” It is reasonable to expect that estimating the contributions of partitions with more than three parts may improve the models’ accuracy; Model 2 was formulated for the same reason, but related to partit-
ions of lengths greater than two. That the Model 2 correction term (4) and subsequent Model 2* correction (5) were suggested by the premises of Model 1, and do produce improved estimates of prime distribution, strengthens the plausibility of the model’s premises to some extent. However, as the number of parts increases, the premise is compromised that partitions of norm n with few parts should map immediately above pn. To eliminate this concern, let us focus instead on modifying the asymptotic est-
imate (15) to produce a better fit.
2.2.1. Interpolating between Models 2 and 2*. As discussed in [2], inspection of Table 1 suggests that from some point on, the Model 2 estimate for π(n) is strictly less than π(n) itself while the Model 2* estimate is strictly greater than π(n); this observation is recorded in [2, Conjecture 4.1]. If these inequalities were true, it would imply that Model 2 accurately captures the behavior of π(n) with smaller error than the error in the Landau estimate (15) for π2(pn−1). (In [2], the authors note-
 this conjecture relies on limited data.) It is proved in [2] based on a formula from [4] that, for M the Meissel-Mertens constant,
(16) π2(pn) − n log log n ∼ M n
as n → ∞. Then a better approximation to (4) than (5) provides is given by
(17) ε(n) := ⌊(n − 1) · (log log(n − 1) − 2γ + M )⌋ .
One can effectively interpolate between Model 2 and Model 2* by introducing a parameter t ∈ [0, 1] into (17), so that t = 0 gives Model 2* and t = 1 approximates Model 2:
(18) ε(n) = ε(n; t) := ⌊(n − 1) · (log log(n − 1) − 2γ + M t)⌋ .
By varying t, perhaps one can locate a “sweet spot” between the estimates of Model 2 and Model 2* to produce a better model of π(n). See Table 2 for examples.
2.2.2. Introducing a second parameter to produce a better fit. Another variable parameter can be introduced into the asymptotic estimate π2(pn) ∼ n log log n in a somewhat ad hoc manner, to further fine tune estimates for π(n). In [6] it is noted that for fixed r ≥ 0, it is also the case that (n + r) log log(n + r) ∼ n log log n as n → ∞.1 Computational experiments reported in [6] show that replacing (5) in Model 2* with
(19) ε(n) = ε(n; r) := ⌊(n + r − 1) log log(n + r − 1) − 2γ(n − 1)⌋
and varying r may improve Model 2* estimates of π(n) at small n, with a decreasing effect as n → ∞ since (n + r) log log(n + r) − n log log n ∼ 0. See Table 3 for examples.

3. Closing remarks
The models formulated in [2] and here, as well as recent works like [3, 5], demonstrate that partition theory – the theory of addition – encodes information about prime numbers. To what extent this insight is useful to the theory of prime distribution, compared to classical approaches like random models, is yet to be seen. In [3], prime numbers are proved to be intrinsically connected to the coefficients of certain quasimodular forms via special partition-theoretic functions; this application is s-
eemingly unconnected to the topic of prime distribution. In [2], while a partition model of primes and of the prime
