Comment on “Repair of DNA Double-Strand Breaks Leaves Heritable
Impairment to Genome Function”
Yi Wang1and Shu-Feng Zhou1*
1College of Chemical Engineering, Huaqiao University, Xiamen, China
*Correspondence: szhou@hqu.edu.cn
Abstract
Bantele and colleagues recently reported that repair of a single CRISPR/Cas9-induced DNA double-strand break (DSB) in the c-MYC topologically associated domain leads to a persistent depletion of chromatin interactions and long-term transcriptional attenuation across multiple generations of human cells. They interpret this observation as evidence
for a previously unrecognized principle—“chromatin fatigue”—in which DSB repair generates a stable architectural defect that acts as a heritable impairment to genome function. Such an idea, if correct, would carry profound implications for genome biology, epigenetic inheritance, cancer evolution, aging, and the safety of therapeutic genome editing. However, our detailed reassessment of the experimental design, underlying
assumptions, and data interpretation reveals that the evidence provided is inadequate to support these sweeping conclusions. Instead, the observed outcomes are more plausibly explained by a combination of Cas9 persistence, off-target DNA damage, repair-factor retention, MYC enhancer plasticity, and the well-documented genomic instability of HeLa
cells. The study does not demonstrate mechanistic causality, does not exclude simpler explanations, and does not provide data consistent with true chromatin memory or heritable architectural change. Moreover, its statistical inferences are based on noisy measurements that fall within expected variability of unstable oncogenic loci. Here, we
present a comprehensive critical analysis showing that the proposed model of chromatin fatigue is unsupported by the available evidence. We offer a corrected interpretation in which the chromatin landscape experiences a temporary, repair-associated perturbation that resolves without leaving enduring or heritable impairment.

1. Introduction
Repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) is generally understood as a tightly regulated, self-limiting process in which chromatin undergoes rapid relaxation, recruitment of repair factors, and subsequent restoration of its pre-damage configuration13. This canonical “access–repair–restore” model is supported by extensive work
showing that nucleosome architecture, enhancer–promoter interactions, and 3D genome topology recover efficiently following resolution of the DNA lesion1,2,4-6. Against this backdrop, Bantele and colleagues7 recently proposed an alternative and far more consequential hypothesis: that repair of a single CRISPR/Cas9-induced DSB within the
c-MYC topologically associated domain (TAD) generates a persistent and heritable disruption of chromatin architecture. They interpret modest, short-lived changes in TAD contacts and transcriptional output as evidence of a previously unrecognized principle“chromatin fatigue”—in which DSB repair leaves a stable structural scar that compromises genome function across generations.
Such a conclusion would represent a substantial departure from the established resilience and dynamic plasticity of chromatin architecture. Consequently, it requires exceptional
evidentiary support. However, the experimental system chosen—HeLa Kyoto—poses immediate limitations. HeLa cells are hyper-aneuploid, p53-deficient, and epigenetically unstable, with documented enhancer rewiring, transcriptional noise, and clonal variability8. These properties render them particularly prone to pseudo-heritable patterns unrelated to durable chromatin states.
Equally concerning is the decision to perform all analyses exclusively at the MYC locus. The MYC regulatory domain is one of the most enhancer-dense and structurally volatile
regions of the human genome, particularly in cancer cells9,10. Its highly dynamic looping behavior and sensitivity to perturbation make it an unsuitable proxy for inferring general principles of DSB-associated chromatin remodeling. Minor and transient fluctuations in enhancer–promoter proximity at this locus are well within expected biological variability and cannot justify extrapolation to genome-wide architectural impairment.
Moreover, the study does not convincingly distinguish the reported effects from wellestablished artifacts of CRISPR/Cas9 activity. Cas9 is known to persist at DNA ends
after cleavage, obstruct transcriptional elongation, and perturb local chromatin organization11,12. Off-target cleavage events—left uncharacterized here—can trigger widespread repair-factor recruitment and transcriptional suppression13-15. Without appropriate controls such as catalytically inactive dCas9, off-target mapping, multiple genomic loci, or diploid non-cancer cell lines, the observed changes in chromatin
topology cannot be attributed specifically to a heritable post-repair architectural defect.

Taken together, these methodological choices create an experimental context in which transient perturbations, Cas9 binding effects, and cancer-cell genomic instability can easily mimic the appearance of heritability. In the sections that follow, we provide an
evidence-based reassessment demonstrating why the concept of “chromatin fatigue” is unsupported, unnecessary, and inconsistent with established mechanisms of chromatin repair and genome organization (see Figure 1).
We argue that the reported observations can be fully accounted for by a combination of Cas9 persistence, off-target and on-target damage responses, MYC enhancer plasticity, and HeLa-specific genomic instability, without invoking any new principle of
“chromatin fatigue.” We further propose a mechanistically grounded alternative model
in which chromatin undergoes a transient, repair-associated perturbation that ultimately resolves, leaving no durable or heritable impairment to genome function.

2. Major Technical Flaws in Experimental Design
The conclusions drawn by Bantele and colleagues7 rest on an experimental framework that is not suited to support claims of universal, heritable chromatin impairment. Three design choices are particularly problematic: exclusive use of HeLa Kyoto cells, focus on
a single hyperdynamic regulatory locus (MYC), and reliance on Cas9-induced DSBs without comprehensive controls.

4
A fundamental weakness of the original study is its heavy reliance on a single, severely compromised biological system: HeLa Kyoto cells. Every conclusion in the manuscript is derived from experiments performed exclusively in this hyper-aneuploid, p53-deficient,
chromatin-unstable, and transcriptionally noisy cervical cancer cell line8. HeLa cells are well known to accumulate spontaneous genomic rearrangements, aberrant epigenetic marks, and stochastic enhancer rewiring over short passages—precisely the conditions under which false appearance of “heritability” is most likely8.
2.1 Exclusive use of HeLa Kyoto cells invalidates generalization
HeLa Kyoto is a hyper-aneuploid, p53-deficient cervical cancer cell line with a highly abnormal karyotype and extensive genomic rearrangements8. Its epigenome is characterized by widespread copy-number variation, non-physiological enhancer wiring, elevated transcriptional noise, and rapid clonal drift over relatively few passages.
These properties create an inherently unstable background in which differences in chromatin architecture and transcriptional output readily arise between subclones, even in the absence of experimental perturbation. Yet the study extrapolates from this single, pathological context to broad conclusions about how DSB repair affects genome function in general. A minimal standard for such claims would require validation in multiple,
genetically stable, diploid, p53-competent cell types, including primary or near-primary human cells, which was not attempted.
Chromatin architecture and transcriptional dynamics differ substantially among embryonic stem cells, primary epithelial cells, fibroblasts, neuronal progenitors, immune cells, and diploid non-transformed cells16. Yet the authors provide no validation in any of these physiologically relevant systems. A claim of ‘universal chromatin fatigue’ cannot be derived from a single cancer cell line with pathological chromatin behavior. A
scientifically appropriate approach would require cross-validation in at least two additional cell types, ideally including a diploid epithelial cell line (e.g., RPE1), a p53competent fibroblast line, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). The absence of such controls is disqualifying for any genome-wide or heritable claim.
2.2 The MYC TAD is a pathological choice for inferring general principles
The c-MYC domain is a super-enhancer–rich, disease-distorted regulatory hub whose 3D configuration is exceptionally dynamic and sensitive to cellular state9,17. In cancer cells, MYC is subject to frequent amplification, enhancer hijacking, and oncogenic rewiring of long-range contacts; it is super-enhancer enriched, highly mobile, subject to rapid loop
turnover, sensitive to replication stress, and frequently rewired in cancer cells9.
As a result, contact-frequency and transcriptional fluctuations at this locus are common and do not necessarily indicate persistent damage or structural collapse. By targeting only

5
the MYC TAD and failing to interrogate additional loci—such as housekeeping genes, lamina-associated domains, enhancer-poor regions, strong TAD boundaries, or heterochromatic regions—the study cannot establish any general principle of DSB
induced architectural impairment. Instead, the findings are confounded by the wellknown structural volatility of MYC in cancer cells. This renders the claim of a universal post-repair phenomenon scientifically indefensible.
2.3 Artificial Cas9-induced DSBs do not model physiological damage and have no
adequate controls
CRISPR/Cas9 nuclease activity differs in important respects from physiologic sources of DSBs. Cas9 generates blunt, enzyme-bound ends, can remain tethered at the cut site for hours or days, and is associated with off-target cleavage events that may not be immediately apparent11,13,18. Cas9 also induces R-loops and unwinding, recruits repair
factors abnormally, and produces off-target nicks and cuts11,13,18. Persistent Cas9 occupancy can impede RNA polymerase II progression and delay restoration of nucleosomes and local architecture11,13. Furthermore, Cas9-mediated genome editing without comprehensive off-target mapping risks introducing additional breaks that activate global DDR signaling and chromatin remodeling.
In the Bantele et al. study7, no dCas9 control is used to distinguish binding from cleavage effects, no systematic off-target profiling is presented, and no comparison is made to
other sources of DSBs such as irradiation or topoisomerase inhibitors. These omissions make it impossible to ascribe the observed changes specifically to canonical DSB repair, rather than to Cas9-specific artifacts or uncharacterized off-target damage.
Thus, the chromatin architecture perturbations observed are far more likely to reflect Cas9 persistence and artifactual remodeling, not a biologically encoded “memory”. A scientifically credible study would compare CRISPR-induced DSBs with irradiationinduced breaks, topoisomerase II poisons, or replication stress-induced breaks.
Taken together, these technical limitations mean that the experiments, as designed, cannot support the far-reaching claim that DSB repair intrinsically leaves a heritable chromatin scar. Instead, the system is optimized to reveal exactly the kind of transient,
noisy, context-dependent behavior that one would expect from a highly abnormal cancer cell line, a hyperdynamic oncogenic TAD, and prolonged Cas9 activity.
3. Conceptual and Mechanistic Misinterpretations
A central conceptual flaw in the study by Bantele et al.7 is the assumption that transient chromatin alterations associated with DSB repair constitute a “persistent epigenetic memory” (see Figure 2). This claim contradicts decades of chromatin repair literature
demonstrating that post-repair chromatin states are highly plastic, rapidly restored, and

6
stabilized across cell generations1-5. Instead, the reported changes are modest, short-lived, and fully explainable by canonical chromatin responses to repair. The authors mistake a short-lived remodeling event—common in DNA repair—for a durable and heritable state.
Such an interpretation reveals misunderstandings of both chromatin biology and epigenetic inheritance principles.
Figure 2. Real DSB repair vs. claimed chromatin fatigue model.
3.1 Correlation is misinterpreted as causation
The study presents three main observations: (1) Cas9-induced cleavage in the MYC TAD, (2) shifts in contact frequencies in Hi-C–like assays, and (3) transient and moderate transcriptional attenuation. These correlations are interpreted as causal evidence for
chromatin degeneration. However, MYC expression and enhancer activity are known to fluctuate widely even in unperturbed conditions17. The data are purely correlative. Correlations in highly unstable HeLa cells cannot serve as evidence of causation. Without mechanistic dissection, direct manipulation of architectural proteins, or causal rescue
experiments, the inference of a persistent structural deficit is unwarranted.
MYC transcription is known to oscillate by >30% even in the absence of perturbation17. Likewise, enhancer interactions in the MYC TAD fluctuate in response to cell cycle,

7
metabolic state, and stochastic loop dynamics9. Thus, the conclusion that a DSB ‘caused’ long-lasting impairment is unwarranted.
3.2 Lack of mechanistic evidence invalidates the “chromatin fatigue” model
If the proposed “chromatin fatigue” model were accurate, one would expect persistent
changes in histone modifications, replication timing, CTCF or cohesin occupancy, architectural loop extrusion dynamics, and nucleosome positioning. None of these were profiled. Without biochemical or structural mechanisms, the claim reduces to a metaphor lacking empirical grounding.
Generally, a valid mechanistic model requires identification of molecular effectors, inheritance machinery, chromatin marks, architectural proteins, and causal dependencies. None are provided. Instead, the authors propose an unverifiable metaphor (“fatigue”),
implying mechanical degradation rather than biochemical regulation. In contrast, established epigenetic memory requires propagation of histone marks (H3K9me3, H3K27me3), maintenance of DNA methylation patterns, stable recruitment of architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin, and mitotically persistent enhancer
promoter interactions19-21. Not a single such element is demonstrated.
3.3 Missing rescue experiments undermine causality
The authors do not test whether the alleged impairment can be reversed or prevented. Essential controls—such as dCas9 to isolate binding effects, off-target mapping, or ectopic restoration of architectural proteins—are missing. Without such tests, causality cannot be established. Key missing controls should include:
⚫ dCas9 expression (no cutting) to assess Cas9-binding artifacts, ⚫ RNA Pol II profiling to determine if transcriptional repression is due to stalled polymerases,
⚫ cohesin/CTCF perturbation to test structural dependencies, ⚫ enhancer reactivation to restore MYC transcription.
Without rescue or reversal experiments, the causal link between DSB repair and persistent impairment remains unproven.
4. Absence of Evidence for True Heritability
The term “heritable” is used repeatedly, yet no experiment demonstrates mitotic
inheritance of a chromatin state. The persistence observed is confined to a narrow 3–5 cell-division window—far too short to constitute epigenetic memory19,21. Moreover, the
authors do not test whether the phenotype depends on cell-cycle stage, environmental cues, or stochastic variability. Heritability requires stringent demonstration; yet the authors show none.

8
4.1 The claimed inheritance window is far too short
The study demonstrates effects lasting 3–5 generations only. In chromatin biology, this duration is insufficient to establish heritability or epigenetic memory19,21. Many transient chromatin disruptions—stress responses, enhancer silencing, DDR-induced transcriptional pausing—can persist for similar durations19,21. Thus, the observed
persistence is neither unique nor indicative of heritable impairment. Heritable chromatin features must persist through multiple mitotic condensations, re-establishment of chromatin marks, differentiation cues, and environmental perturbations. The observed mild alterations in MYC TAD contacts do not meet these criteria.
4.2 No lineage tracing or clonal stability analysis
Clonal variation in HeLa cells is well documented8. Lineage tracing is essential to
distinguish genuine inheritance vs. stochastic drift or Cas9-induced off-target lesions18. The authors perform no barcoding, no lineage reconstruction, and no clonal stability
mapping, leaving the possibility of false heritable patterns due to clone selection.
4.3 Persistence of DDR foci is not heritability
The study relies partly on persistent chromatin marks such as γH2AX and 53BP1 foci. But γH2AX can remain for days even without active DNA damage22. Its persistence reflects
slow protein turnover, not heritable chromatin states. Thus, the authors mistake a temporary, repair-factor–retention artifact for a stable biological signal.
4.4 No demonstration of mitotically inherited chromatin marks
True inheritance would require stable detection of mitotically transmitted histone marks, DNA methylation changes, architectural mark reinforcement, and re-established CTCF
boundary perturbation23-25. None are assayed. The authors rely solely on contactfrequency shifts, which are probabilistic, not deterministic structural changes.
5. Misuse of the MYC TAD as a Representative Model of Genome
Architecture
A major conceptual flaw in the study by Bantele et al.7 is their extrapolation from a single
genomic locus—the c-MYC TAD—to global claims about the human genome. Small changes in enhancer–promoter proximity are normal and do not indicate damage. This is methodologically unjustifiable. The MYC regulatory environment is one of the most
“unusual, unstable, and cancer-distorted” chromatin domains in the entire human genome9. Its architecture cannot reasonably be taken as representative.
5.1 The MYC locus is inherently hyperdynamic and unstable

9
MYC sits within a super-enhancer–rich regulatory hub, characterized by continuous enhancer–promoter switching, rapid loop extrusion, sensitivity to metabolic and growth signals, frequent structural variation in cancer cells, and transcriptional bursting
behaviors9. Thus, any perturbation—Cas9 binding, transient DNA damage signaling, cell-cycle progression—will naturally produce temporary destabilization. Claiming that this reflects a heritable or universal shift in chromatin organization is a categorical error.
5.2 MYC is one of the worst possible loci for generalizing structural principles
If the authors wished to test heritable architectural disruption, they should have targeted
stable housekeeping promoters, lamina-associated domains (LADs), heterochromatic regions, TAD boundaries with strong CTCF occupancy, or enhancer–poor regions. By
selecting MYC—"the most structurally volatile TAD in HeLa”—the authors have
biased the experiment toward detecting spurious fluctuations. HeLa cells harbor copy
number abnormalities and enhancer duplications near MYC, which also magnify contactfrequency noise.
5.3 Single-locus analysis precludes generalization
The authors test one locus and show minor, short-lived changes, yet generalize to all TADs, all chromosomes, the entire human genome, the fundamental biology of DNA repair. This leap is unjustified and unprecedented. Genome-wide structural principles
require, at minimum, dozens of loci tested, multiple chromatin environments, andparallel assays in different cell types. None of these criteria are met.
6. Cas9 Persistence, Off-Target Damage, and HeLa Instability: More
Plausible Explanations
Rather than invoking a novel biological mechanism (“chromatin fatigue”), the findings
can be fully explained by “well-established artifacts” associated with Cas9 function, cancer cell heterogeneity, and enhancer plasticity.
6.1 Cas9 persistence on DNA explains reduced MYC expression
Cas9 is known to remain tethered to DNA for hours to days following cleavage12. Persistent Cas9 occupancy can block transcriptional elongation by RNA polymerase II, prevent nucleosome reassembly, distort loop extrusion, interfere with enhancer–promoter
proximity, and delay reactivation of topological configurations. These effects can readily account for reduced MYC transcription, altered contact frequencies, and slow recovery over several divisions. This explanation requires no new biological principle.
6.2 Off-target DNA damage produces widespread chromatin alterations

10
Cas9 off-target activity is well documented13,15. In the absence of GUIDE-seq, CIRCLEseq, or Digenome-seq, the authors cannot exclude the possibility that multiple additional DSBs occurred elsewhere in the genome. Such off-target events can activate global DNA
damage responses, induce chromatin compaction, alter transcription factor binding, trigger persistent DDR foci. These are all far more plausible explanations for the observed phenomena.
6.3 HeLa instability produces pseudo-heritable patterns
HeLa cells exhibit intrinsic enhancer rewiring8, recurrent copy-number variation,
epigenomic drift over short passages, strong clonal selection pressures, and hypervariable MYC expression dynamics. HeLa subpopulations with lower MYC expression or altered chromatin could be selectively enriched following DNA damage8. This easily mimics heritability. Any clonal analysis performed in HeLa will inevitably show ‘pseudo-stable
differences’ across generations, especially following a DNA damage event that selects for particular cellular subpopulations. This instability alone can generate the illusion of heritability.
6.4 Repair factor retention can mimic persistent chromatin states
Several DNA repair proteins (53BP1, RIF1, BRCA1, RAD51) remain bound at break sites long after repair has concluded, especially in cancer cells3,4,6. Their presence alters
chromatin accessibility, reduces transcription locally, delays nucleosome reassembly, and shifts looping probabilities. Such effects can last 2–6 cell cycles—exactly the window reported by the authors. This is not heritable memory. It is “slow clearance” of repair factors.
7. Incorrect Extrapolation to Aging, Cancer, and Genome Editing
Perhaps the most concerning aspect of Bantele et al.7 is the speculative overreach.
Without adequate data, the authors claim that their findings affect human aging, cancer progression, genome instability, genome editing safety, and long-term chromatin homeostasis. These claims lack empirical grounding.
7.1 No evidence connects chromatin fatigue to aging
Aging involves mitochondrial decline, chronic inflammation, replication stress, telomere
erosion, and loss of proteostasis26. None of these processes are modeled in HeLa cells or addressed experimentally. Moreover, DSB-induced chromatin alterations in primary cells typically recover fully, even in aged cells1. There is no evidence that transient MYC TAD distortion in HeLa contributes to organismal aging.
7.2 The link to oncogenesis is speculative

11
The authors propose that “chromatin fatigue” could promote cancer evolution. But HeLa is already cancerous, MYC dysregulation is a hallmark of HeLa behavior. The study does not examine mutation rates, nor clonal expansion, nor transformation assays.
The claim is rhetorical rather than empirical.
7.3 Claims about CRISPR safety are misleading
The authors warn that therapeutic gene editing could cause chromatin fatigue. This is scientifically irresponsible. Clinical CRISPR workflows differ markedly:
⚫ editing occurs in primary cells, not HeLa; ⚫ target loci are chosen for functional relevance, not MYC; ⚫ rigorous off-target analysis is performed; ⚫ Cas9 is delivered transiently, not stably;
⚫ DSBs are often avoided entirely with base editors or prime editors.
Thus, extrapolating HeLa/MYC artifacts to clinical CRISPR genomics is unjustifiable.
7.4 No evidence supports intergenerational or mitotic inheritance
True heritable states persist across differentiation, remain fixed after reprogramming, and
propagate through chromatin cycles. None of these are demonstrated. The authors use the word “heritable” rhetorically. There is no evidence of true epigenetic inheritance.
8. Logical, Statistical, and Methodological Fallacies
Beyond experimental limitations, the paper’s core conclusions rest on several logical and statistical fallacies. These issues fundamentally compromise the integrity of the
“chromatin fatigue” model.
8.1 Overinterpretation of noisy Hi-C–derived contact data
The authors rely heavily on Hi-C–like assays to claim persistent architectural dysfunction. But Hi-C contact frequencies are “probabilistic”, not absolute27. Differences of ±5–15% fall well within normal variability for enhancer-rich loci like MYC28. HeLa’s hyperaneuploid karyotype amplifies noise in 3D structure measurements8. Small sample
sizes and lack of biological replicates amplify stochastic variability. Thus, what the authors interpret as “durable chromatin impairment” is more plausibly measurement noise, compounded by MYC’s natural architectural instability. No statistical method is provided to distinguish random variation from biologically meaningful shifts. This is a
cardinal flaw.
8.2 False equivalence between temporary remodeling and persistent damage

12
A well-established hallmark of DSB repair is “transient chromatin relaxation”, followed by restoration of architecture via histone chaperones, nucleosome remodelers, and loop extrusion resumption29,30. The authors observe early-phase relaxation, then
misinterpret incomplete short-term recovery as evidence of “permanent architectural collapse”. This is a false equivalence.
8.3 Failure to test alternative hypotheses
Scientific inference demands testing alternative models. The authors fail to test obvious rival explanations including Cas9 persistence, repair factor retention, clonal selection,
MYC enhancer detachment noise, and off-target DSB accumulation. The failure to test these alternatives leaves their model unsupported.
8.4 Absence of baseline variability measurements
A robust conclusion requires understanding baseline chromatin variability in HeLa. Yet the authors do not quantify MYC transcriptional noise, normal TAD interaction fluctuations, and cell-to-cell structural variance. Without these baselines, no comparison
can legitimately claim “impairment.”
9. A Revised Interpretation Consistent with Chromatin Biology
Given all evidence, the most parsimonious interpretation is “not” that DSB repair leaves a heritable scar, but that: (1) Cas9 cutting perturbs MYC enhancer–promoter interactions, because Cas9 remains bound and delays nucleosome recovery. (2) MYC’s intrinsically
unstable chromatin architecture exhibits transient fluctuations, which are common and not indicative of damage9. (3) DNA repair factors linger post-repair in cancer cells, creating the appearance of local chromatin attenuation29. (4) HeLa clonal drift produces pseudo-stable differences, which mimic inheritance. (5) The observed effects fall within the normal fluctuation range, and cannot be attributed to a new biological rule.
9.1 Temporary chromatin remodeling is well known and well characterized
Transient remodeling is a universal feature of DSB repair1,2,29,30: H2AX phosphorylation spreads rapidly then resolves, loop extrusion pauses during repair, then restores, enhancer–promoter hubs transiently detach and later reconnect, and nucleosome gaps close progressively over hours. This is “normal”, not pathological.
9.2 No evidence supports permanent architectural change
The authors provide no evidence of lasting CTCF boundary displacement, persistent loss
of loop extrusion, enduring depletion of architectural proteins, and stable epigenetic marks. Notably, the mild shifts they observe “recover substantially” by later time points—contradicting their own conclusions.

13
9.2 The simplest explanation fits the data best
The simplest explanation—supported by multiple independent studies—is that chromatin temporarily relaxes after repair, enhancer loops re-establish across several divisions, transcription returns to baseline, and no heritable impairment exists. This “relaxation and restoration” model aligns with all known mechanisms.
10. Recommendations for Rigorous Testing
If the authors wish to validate or salvage any component of the “chromatin fatigue”
hypothesis, the following experiments are essential.
10.1Testing multiple loci across diverse chromatin environments
These tests should target a stable housekeeping promoter, a lamina-associated domain, a heterochromatin region, a strong TAD boundary, and enhancer-poor loci. This will reveal whether any claimed phenomenon is MYC-specific.
10.2 Repeating experiments in diploid, p53-competent primary cells
HeLa cannot serve as a generalizable model. Repeat experiments in RPE1, MRC-5
fibroblasts, primary epithelial cells, and iPSCs. If results remain MYC-specific or HeLaspecific, the hypothesis collapses.
10.3 Performing essential Cas9 controls
The authors should include dCas9, to test binding vs. cutting; Cas9 nickases; GUIDE-seq or CIRCLE-seq, to measure off-targets. Without these controls, no DSB-specific
conclusion is valid.
10.4Adding mechanistic profiling
The authors should use ATAC-seq for accessibility, ChIP-seq for H3K27me3, H3K9me3, γH2AX, and CTCF, HiChIP or PLAC-seq for loop dynamics, and CUT&RUN for architectural protein binding. Only mechanistic data can support claims of long-term impairment.
11. Implications for the Field and Corrective Framework
The original paper risks misleading the field by suggesting that DSB repair is inherently mutagenic to chromatin architecture, TADs can collapse permanently, transcriptional defects reflect epigenetic inheritance, and chromatin is fragile rather than resilient. These interpretations are contradicted by chromatin robustness studies, loop extrusion models,
enhancer reconstitution kinetics, and repair dynamics1,2,29,30. A corrected model recognizes that: (1) chromatin is highly resilient, (2) DSB-induced disruption is reversible,

14
(3) MYC is uniquely unstable, (4) HeLa is unsuitable for generalization, (5) Cas9 and DDR artifacts explain the data, and (6) there is no heritable impairment.
12. Summary and Conclusion
The claim by Bantele et al.7 that a single, Cas9-induced DNA double-strand break
permanently disrupts local chromatin architecture and generates a “heritable impairment to genome function” is not supported by the data and is inconsistent with
established principles of chromatin biology, genome topology, and DNA repair dynamics. Across this critique, we have demonstrated that:
1. The experimental system is fundamentally inappropriate. All experiments were conducted exclusively in HeLa Kyoto cells, a hyper-aneuploid, p53-deficient, epigenetically unstable cancer cell line known to exhibit clonal drift, enhancer rewiring,
and structural genome instability8. No results were validated in diploid, p53-competent, or primary cells.
2. The MYC TAD is a uniquely unstable regulatory domain. Its super-enhancer
saturation and rapid 3D dynamics make it the worst possible locus for deriving universal chromatin principles.
3. Cas9 persistence, off-target activity, and slow repair-factor clearance fully explain the observations. Cas9 remains tethered to DNA after cleavage and can inhibit transcription, distort chromatin structure, and produce spurious signals. Repair factors such as 53BP1 and RIF1 can remain for multiple cycles. Off-target cuts are common and were never assessed in this study.
4. Chromatin remodeling during DSB repair is known to be transient and reversible. Nucleosome reassembly, loop reformation, enhancer re-engagement, and DDR resolution
are all short-lived events29,30, not permanent impediments.
5. No evidence of epigenetic memory or heritability is presented. No lineage tracing, no persistent marks, no methylation changes, no stable CTCF boundary shifts, and no
long-term multi-passage studies are included. The observed 3–5 division persistence falls well within normal noise in HeLa cells.
6. The logical and statistical interpretation of 3D chromatin data is flawed. Random
fluctuations in contact frequencies are misrepresented as structural collapse. The authors’ Hi-C–style inferences lack proper statistical baselines.
7. The biological extrapolations (aging, cancer progression, CRISPR safety) are
speculative and unsupported. None are justified from the data obtained in a single cancer cell line.

A Corrected Model
A more rigorous, evidence-based interpretation of the data is (see Figure 3):
⚫ A Cas9-induced DSB produces temporary chromatin relaxation. ⚫ Repair factors and Cas9 itself produce delayed re-normalization.
⚫ MYC’s enhancer network produces natural fluctuations. ⚫ Clonal variation produces pseudo-heritable patterns in cancer cells. ⚫ Chromatin architecture is robust, resilient, and fully restored after repair.
This interpretation fully accounts for the observations without requiring a novel biological principle like “chromatin fatigue.”

Final Conclusion

16
The “heritable impairment to genome function” claimed by Bantele et al.7 is best
understood as the combined result of HeLa cell instability, MYC locus volatility, Cas9induced artifacts, off-target DNA damage, and delayed restoration of chromatin dynamics.
There is no compelling evidence of heritable chromatin impairment, no demonstration of persistent 3D architectural collapse, and no mechanistic basis for a new epigenetic
memory pathway. The study’s claims should be interpreted cautiously and do not change the current understanding that chromatin architecture—while dynamic and responsive
is ultimately resilient and restored after DSB repair.
