On the Degrees of Freedom of some Lasso procedures
Mauro Bernardi1, Antonio Canale1, and Marco Stefanucci2
1Department of Statistical Sciences, University of Padova, Italy
2Department of Economics and Finance, University of Rome Tor Vergata,
Italy
Abstract
The effective degrees of freedom of penalized regression models quantify the actual amount of information used to generate predictions, playing a pivotal role in model evaluation and selection. Although a closed-form estimator is available for the Lasso penalty, adaptive extensions of widely used penalized approaches, including the Adaptive Lasso and Adaptive Group Lasso, have remained without analogous theoretical characterization. This paper presents the first unbiased estimator of the effective-
 degrees of freedom for these methods, along with their main theoretical properties, for both orthogonal and non-orthogonal designs, derived within Stein’s unbiased risk estimation framework. The resulting expressions feature inflation terms influenced by the regularization parameter, coefficient signs, and least-squares estimates. These advances enable more accurate model selection criteria and unbiased prediction error estimates, illustrated through synthetic and real data. These contributions o-
ffer a rigorous theoretical foundation for understanding model complexity in adaptive regression, bridging a critical gap between theory and practice.
Keywords: Degrees of Freedom; Adaptive Lasso; Stein’s Lemma.
1 Introduction
Regularization has become a cornerstone of modern statistical modeling, particularly in regression settings where estimators are defined by minimizing an objective function comprising a loss term and a penalty on coefficient norms. Among such methods, the Lasso estimator (Tibshirani, 1996), which employs the l1 norm, achieves variable selection by shrinking a subset of coefficients exactly to zero, thereby inducing sparsity. Extensions such as the Group Lasso (Yuan and Lin, 2006) generalize this f-
ramework to structured settings, applying l2 penalties to predefined groups of coefficients to enable group-wise selection. While these methods excel at regularization, their uniform shrinkage mechanisms lead to over shrink also the signal, thus producing biased estimates of the non zero coefficients. The Adaptive Lasso (Zou, 2006) and its extensions address this limitation by incorporating data-driven weights derived from preliminary least squares estimates. Unlike its non-adaptive counterpart, w-
hich imposes uniform shrinkage, the Adaptive Lasso adapts penalization to individual coefficients, reducing bias and attaining oracle properties under suitable conditions. Similarly, the Adaptive Group Lasso (Wang and

For example, in a normal linear regression framework, let X be a matrix of covariates with p columns, y be the response vector of size n, and β the p-dimensional vector of unknown regression coefficients with y = Xβ + ε and ε ∼ N(0, σ2In). In these settings, the degrees of freedom can be thought as the number of “independent pie-
ces of information” needed to estimate the p unknown parameters. For simple least-squares estimates of β the degrees of freedom equal the number of variables p implying that each estimated quantity “consumes” one degree of freedom. This can be surprising, since differently from (random) subset regression, Lasso selects nonzero coefficients using the response variable so one would expect a higher value for the degrees of freedom. Nevertheless, this additional use of data is perfectly balanced with the a-
mount of shrinkage that Lasso applies to each coefficient. Note that this would not happen in subset selection, where the “best” subset of variables to use in a linear model is chosen accordingly to some criteria (see Tibshirani, 2015). Similar results were found for the generalized Lasso penalty (Tibshirani and Taylor, 2011; Chen et al., 2020) and for the more challenging case p > n (Dossal et al., 2013; Tibshirani and Taylor, 2012). This perfect trade-off between shrinkage and the use of the res-
ponse variable does not arise for the Group Lasso estimator, as discussed by Vaiter et al. (2017). In this work, the authors derive formulae for computing the degrees of freedom of the Group Lasso under both orthogonal and nonorthogonal designs, generally yielding values lower than the size of the active set (see also Vaiter et al., 2012). The concept of degrees of freedom underpins parametric inference and model selection: information criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Ba-
yesian Information Criterion (BIC) are defined by combining a model fit measure and the degrees of freedom (Säfken et al., 2024). Despite their advantages, adaptive methods lack a closed-form expression for their effective degrees of freedom. While such estimators exist for non-adaptive methods like the Lasso (Zou et al., 2007), adaptive variants resist analogous characterization due to their data-dependent weights, which introduce nonlinear dependencies between coefficients and penalties. Further-
more, the simplicity of the degrees of freedom formula for the Lasso estimator has inadvertently encouraged the misguided practice of computing information criteria for arbitrary penalized methods by substituting the size of the active set for the true degrees of freedom. This flawed approach risks overly optimistic model selection, often leading to overfitting and biased parameter estimates. In this paper, we solve this issue by deriving the first unbiased estimator of the effective degrees of fr-
eedom for the Adaptive Lasso within Stein’s unbiased risk estimation framework (Stein, 1981). Our estimator explicitly accounts for the adaptive penalty’s dependence on data-driven weights, regularization parameters, and coefficient signs. The proof

However, in Adaptive Lasso and related models, an additional term appears that does not vanish. The explicit derivation of this term’s gradient with respect to the data is obtained using matrix differentiation rules. Additionally, we re-derive the expression for the degrees of freedom of the Group Lasso, providing additional insights and charact-

Leveraging these results, we are also able to provide analogous derivations for the more challenging Adaptive Group Lasso estimator. By bridging this theoretical gap, our work enables principled model selection, risk estimation, and complexity-aware inference for adaptive penalized regression methods.

also on their adaptive weighting structure. When γ crosses a transition point γl, the adaptive weights—which themselves depend on y—induce nonlinear interactions between the retained coefficients and the regularization path. These interactions can amplify the sensitivity of the fitted model to the data, effectively “consuming more information” despite a sparser active set.

3 Empirical Assessment
In this section, we conduct two numerical experiments to validate and contextualize our theoretical findings. First, we generate synthetic data to empirically demonstrate the consistency of our theoretical results. Second, we analyze a real-world dataset to evaluate the utility of the estimated degrees of freedom in model selection. Notably, the use of our results achieves performance comparable to computationally intensive cross-validation while diverging significantly from the naive practice of -
equating the degrees of freedom to the size of the active set.
3.1 Synthetic data
We let n = 100 and p = 30 and generate a n × p data matrix X = (X1, . . . , Xp) where Xj ∼ N(0, In) for j = 1, . . . , p. We fix β = (5, −5, 5, 3, −3, 3, 1, −1, 1, 0, . . . , 0) thus having the last 21 coefficients equal to zero, and consequently let y⋆ = Xβ. For B = 10,000 replications we perturb y⋆ with independent noise generated from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance equal to a value leading to a signal-to-noise ratio equal to 4, obtaining yb = Xβ + εb. Estimation of β is obt-
ained with the estimators presented in Equations (4), (9), and (13). For the Group Lasso and its adaptive version, we grouped three consecutive coefficients leading to ng = 3 for g = 1, . . . , G and G = 10. For the Adaptive Group lasso the weight of each group equals the reciprocal of the l2 norm of corresponding least squares estimates. For each method, an appropriate -

4 Conclusions
We introduced a general framework for computing unbiased estimates of the degrees of freedom in penalized regression models, extending the foundational work of Zou et al. (2007). Our theoretical results underline that the common practice of using the size of active set as an estimate of the degrees of freedom is biased in many respect. This practice can severely distort inference since the size of active set and the true degrees of freedom are usually different, as highlighted by Janson et al. (20-
15). For the Adaptive Lasso, for example, we demonstrated that the correct estimate includes, in addition to the active set size, an adjustment term whose sign depends on the weights’ selection. Under default weighting schemes, this term is positive, leading to an inflation of the degrees of freedom relative to the active set size. In contrast, the Group Lasso exhibits a deflation effect due to its reliance on the l2 norm, reducing the degrees of freedom compared to the active set size. The Adapti-
ve Group Lasso presents a more complex interplay: both inflation (from adaptive weights) and contraction (from l2 shrinkage) coexist, making general characterization difficult. In addition to the analytical expressions for the degrees of freedom, we provide deep understanding of their local behavior as functions of γ. Specifically, we show that for the Adaptive Lasso (under certain conditions), these exhibit strictly positive but monotonic decreasing slopes within adjacent change points. For the G-
roup Lasso, we show that the associated degrees of freedom are bounded between the active groups and active coefficients, and their local monotonicity is intimately related to the notion of orthogonality. These findings advance the understanding of model complexity in penalized regression, with implications for model selection, risk estimation, and theoretical analysis of high-dimensional methods.
