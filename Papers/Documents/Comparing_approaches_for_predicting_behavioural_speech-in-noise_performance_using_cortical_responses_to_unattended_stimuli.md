ABSTRACT
The cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope is a phenomenon where the brain’s electrical activity, as recorded by electroencephalography (EEG) signals, fluctuates in accordance with changes in stimulus intensity (the acoustic envelope of the stimulus). Understanding speech in a noisy background is a key challenge for people with hearing impairments. Speech stimuli are therefore more ecologically valid than clicks, tone pips, or speech tokens (e.g., syllables) for assessing hearing. However, it -
remains unclear whether EEG responses to speech provide an advantage in predicting speech intelligibility. This study aimed to assess the ability of cortical responses to speech and speech-related sounds to predict behavioural speech-in-noise performance in listeners with normal hearing when they are not attending to the stimuli. Twenty native English-speaking adults with normal hearing (aged 18 to 40 years) participated in a speech reception task, listening to English Matrix sentences presented a-
t signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) of -15, -10, -5, 0, and ∞ (no background noise) dB, and then identifying the words they heard in the sentences. In the EEG experiment, the participants then listened to continuous speech, broadband noise modulated by the envelope of speech, and repeating short /da/ stimuli presented at the same SNR levels as in the Matrix test. For the latter, Auditory Late Response (ALR) was estimated from the EEG, and for the former, the strength of the envelopetracking responses -
was calculated. Cortical responses to all stimuli showed monotonic relationships with the signal-to-noise ratio at the group level and in most individuals, although there was considerable variability. EEG analysis in the delta band showed no significant difference in the number of participants with predicted speech reception thresholds (SRTs) within an error margin of 7 dB—the level at which SRT prediction is considered applicable—regardless of the type of cortical response used. In the theta band-
, however, SRT predictions based on cortical responses to continuous speech performed worse, showing a significantly lower number of predictions within an error margin of 7 dB compared to those based on cortical responses to modulated noise and the repeating /da/ sound. The proportion of individual SRT predictions with an error margin within 7 dB was, at best, 30 %. For people with normal hearing, cortical responses to continuous speech and modulated noise predicted speech-in-noise performance at -
the group level but not at the individual level, due to variability in cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope. Predicting the SRT on an individual level remains a major and clinically important challenge.
1. Introduction
In the wider society, perception of speech is essential for learning, communication, and socialising. A major challenge affecting the quality of life for people with hearing impairments, and even for people with normal hearing, is the ability to understand speech in a noisy environment. This problem can lead to, for example, difficulties in learning at school or social isolation. Speech perception refers to the ability to
perceive and understand speech (Heald and Nusbaum, 2014). It can be measured through behavioural tests, where subjects typically respond by repeating the word they hear or clicking on one of a set of choices displayed on a screen. It is usually quantified as the percentage of speech that a listener can recall in quiet or noisy conditions. The percentage of correct scores is then used to estimate the speech reception threshold (SRT), typically defined as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at which the-
 subject can correctly identify 50 % of the speech material (Plomp and

Since this type of measurement involves cognitive performance, such as attention, memory, executive function, and processing speed (Dryden et al., 2017), some groups of subjects (e.g., children or people with cognitive impairments) may not provide reliable behavioural responses. Difficulty in understanding speech-in-noise is to a large part dependent on peripheral hearing sensitivity. Conventionally, hearing assessment is primarily carried out using pure-tone audiometry (PTA). The -
result is then deemed to reflect the degree of hearing loss. However, the pure-tone stimuli used in the test are not very representative of the realworld challenges of speech perception and PTA does not reliably predict the SRT in individuals (Middelweerd et al., 1990; Smoorenburg, 1992). Moreover, PTA is also a behavioural measure, and so has the same limitation as the behavioural measure of speech intelligibility, in that children or people with cognitive impairment, may not be able to respond r-
eliably or at all (Hirsh, 1948). Auditory evoked responses (AERs) are objective electrophysiological responses, commonly obtained via the electroencephalogram (EEG) from electrodes placed on the scalp, that measure brain response to sound stimuli. The stimulus used in AERs can be as simple as clicks and tone pips, or more complex such as syllables, sentences, or continuous narrative speech. They can be an alternative for estimating hearing threshold (Stapells et al., 1995; Ronnberg et al., 2016) w-
ithout the need for behavioural responses. AERs have also been proposed as a possible predictor of behavioural measurement of speech intelligibility. The Auditory Late Response (ALR) to repeating short sounds, such as /da/, consists of peaks and troughs with latencies up to around 300 ms after the stimulus onset. The ALR is obtained by averaging the brain’s responses to each stimulus. The amplitude in the ALR decreases as competing noise levels increase, positively correlating with the decline in -
behavioural speech intelligibility with rising noise levels (Wong et al., 2008; Billings et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2016). Evidence suggests that ALR may reflect two main processes relating to speech intelligibility, which are auditory perception and cognitive activity related to speech discrimination in noise (Billings et al., 2013; Goossens et al., 2018). Studies have also shown the ability of the human brain to track the temporal envelope of sound; human brain activity measured by EEG synchron-
ises temporally with the stimulus envelope (Aiken and Picton, 2008; Ding and Simon, 2014). This phenomenon was found when using either amplitude modulated tones or continuous speech stimuli. It has been demonstrated that the cortical tracking of a continuous sound envelope is correlated with stimulus SNR: stimuli with lower SNR generate weaker responses, and speech-in-noise performance can be predicted using objective measures of cortical responses to continuous speech stimuli (Vanthornhout et al.-
, 2018). This type of response requires a modelling tool that relates the EEG and the stimulus over time, due to the continuous nature of the stimulation, as opposed to the isolated stimuli used in ALR, which involves averaging responses. Building on the work of Vanthornhout et al. (2018), Lesenfants et al. (2019), Verschueren et al. (2020) who demonstrated that the behavioural SRT can be predicted from objective measures of cortical tracking of the speech envelope, the current study focuses on wh-
ether cortical tracking of the speech envelope offers greater benefits (e.g., more accurate predictions of the SRT) compared to responses to other types of stimuli. In this study, we aimed to assess the applicability of objective measures of cortical responses to the continuous sound envelope and ALR in predicting the behavioural speech reception threshold (SRT) for individuals, even when no attention to the stimuli is required. A non-attending or passive listening task was also used in a study by-
 Vanthornhout et al. (2019), where the group level SRT prediction differed by 1 dB from the group behavioural SRT. The method used in the current study differed from the studies by Vanthornhout et al. (2018), Lesenfants et al. (2019), in which participants were required to answer questions about the content of the stimuli. The choice of non-attended stimuli comes with an inherent trade-off: while it excludes the important contribution of selective attention to speech-in-noise
processing, it offers practical benefits for specific clinical populations. This approach is particularly relevant for testing infants or children, where maintaining alertness is more feasible than controlling attention, and for individuals who cannot provide reliable behavioural responses. Understanding the predictive power of non-attentive measures helps define what aspects of speech processing we can assess in these populations. To be consistent with previous studies, the current study employed-
 the forward and backward temporal response function (forward and backward TRF) approach to quantify the cortical tracking of acoustic envelope (Crosse et al., 2016), which linearly relates the stimulus envelope to the envelope reconstructed (predicted or estimated) from the EEG (see Section 2.6 "Temporal response function (TRF) estimation” for details). This study employed subject-wise backward TRF modelling, consistent with the study by Vanthornhout et al. (2018), but in contrast to the study by-
 Lesenfants et al. (2019), which used subject-averaged backward TRF modelling. Two stimuli of varying complexity were used for the TRF approach: (1) continuous speech (the more complex and representative of speech-in-noise listening challenges), and (2) broadband noise with the same envelope as the continuous speech (but without spectral changes, and therefore unintelligible). Speech-modulated noise is uncommon in speech intelligibility prediction studies, but it has the potential clinical advanta-
ge of not involving cognitive processing related to language. This avoids potential confounding in (pre-lingual) infants, cognitively impaired people, or non-native speakers. The use of ALR to repeating /da/ was employed to compare whether a much simpler form of speech/sound stimulation, still sharing some properties of speech, would be sufficient to predict the SRT. One benefit of using the ALR, rather than acoustic envelope tracking, could be that time domain averaging is a powerful noise reduct-
ion method that is not appropriate when measuring cortical tracking of continuous stimuli. There is also evidence that the neural response to sound following pauses and with a sharp onset, such as in the ALR paradigm, produces large neural activity (Sussman et al., 2008; Lightfoot, 2016). There is thus some prior indication that ALR may be more robust than envelope-tracking responses, though a direct comparison has not previously been carried out to the best of our knowledge. The criteria for asse-
ssing performance in predicting the SRT from cortical responses to sound will be (1) the absolute prediction error between the SRT and threshold estimated from the objective measures from EEG response (2) number of cases where the SRT could not be predicted from the EEG within reasonable bounds and (3) the correlation between the behavioural and the predicted SRT. The findings from this study also provide insight into the use of cortical responses to speech and non-speech stimuli in the context of-
 objectively predicting behavioural speech-in-noise performance in individuals. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report on using cortical acoustic tracking using non-attended speech and speech-like stimuli in individual subjects in an attempt to predict SRTs. The work presents an original study to assess the potential for assessing individuals’ speech perception based on speech evoked potentials using a clinically feasible protocol. If the use of cortical tracking of contin-
uous speech and modulated noise envelopes shows no difference in SRT prediction performance, then the cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope may simply be a representation of speech audibility. This could imply that cortical tracking of speech is not very sensitive to central disorders affecting speech processing. There has been a suggestion that the cortical tracking of acoustic envelope may not reflect speech intelligibility (Ding and Simon, 2014). Evidence for this comes from a study by Ste-
inschneider et al. (2013) who found the encoding of speech and non-speech sound in both human and monkey. Not only was it found that the cortical tracking of acoustic envelope is non-speech specific, but it is also not specific to humans. Another concern is the difference in nature between objective and behavioural testing. Since many behavioural tests involve some

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Twenty native English speakers aged between 18 and 40 years old (mean 23 years) participated in this study (15 males and 5 females). All participants had self-reported normal hearing and confirmed to have hearing threshold below 25 dB HL between 250 Hz and 8 kHz using PTA. No participants had a history of neurological disorders. The study was approved by the University of Southampton Ethics Committee (ethics reference number ERGO: 52,472). All participants provided written informed consent prior t-
o the experiment.
2.2. Behavioural testing of speech intelligibility
The British version of the Oldenburg Matrix Test was used to evaluate the participants’ behavioural speech-in-noise performance following the protocol described in Kollmeier et al. (2015). Each Sentence in the British Matrix test contains five words with the fixed structure of “Name – Verb – Numeral – Adjective – Object” (e.g., Peter got three large desks). Each position in the sentence has ten alternative words which are randomly selected to form a sentence. Five signal-to-noise ratio levels for -
testing were 15, 10, 5, 0, and ∞ (without background noise or in quiet) dB. The Matrix sentences were presented at 65 dBA (at SNR = ∞ dB). For SNR = 0 dB, speech-shaped stationary noise presented at 65 dBA was added (giving an overall level of 68 dBA). For negative SNR the noise was kept constant at 65 dBA, and the level of speech was reduced according to the desired SNR levels. All auditory stimuli throughout the study were calibrated using a Bruel and Kjaer Type 4230 sound level calibrator (Næru-
m, Denmark). Stimulus presentation was controlled by a MATLAB script, with delivery through an RME Fireface UC audio interface and Etymotic ER-2 inserted earphones (Etymotic Research, Inc., Illinois, USA). This equipment setup was used for both the behavioural Matrix test and subsequent EEG experiments, though the stimuli themselves differed between experiments as described in the following sections. Each participant was presented with 20 sentences at each SNR. Scoring for each sentence was based -
on the number of correct words expressed as a percentage (word scores). The averaged percentage of correct words at each SNR was fitted with a sigmoid function as shown in Eq. (1) (Vanthornhout et al., 2019) using nonlinear least squares

2.3. Continuous stimuli
In this study, three types of sound stimuli were used: continuous speech, speech envelope-modulated noise, and /da/. We will first describe the continuous speech and speech envelope-modulated noise in this section. The /da/ stimulus used for ALR will be described later in Section 2.8 "ALR to /da/”. The purpose of using modulated noise is to emulate continuous speech by having a similar temporal envelope waveform and long-term spectrum, but it remains unintelligible. By having the same envelope sha-
pe in the speech and the modulated noise, one might expect that envelope tracking responses might be similar for
both, with differences arising primarily from time-varying spectral changes and cognitive processing with actual speech. The continuous speech stimulus was extracted from of part 1, chapter 2 and chapter 5 of ‘The Children of Odin – by P ́adraic Colum’, an audiobook read by a female narrator (available to download for free at https://librivox.org/the-children-of-odin-by-padraic-colum/). The original audiobook file sampling rate was 22,050 Hz in an mp3 format (energy band limited to 0 – 11,000 Hz) -
and was converted to a .wav file with a new sampling rate of 44,100 Hz. The amplitude envelope of the continuous speech was created by performing full-wave rectification on the broadband continuous speech and subsequently applying a low-pass filter at 100 Hz. All of these procedures were carried out using standard functions in the Praat software (Boersma and Weenink, 2001). The speech envelope-modulated noise signal, referred to as ’modulated noise,’ was generated by multiplying the amplitude enve-
lope of the continuous speech with a speech-shaped noise. The speech-shaped noise, serving as the carrier signal, was created by filtering Gaussian white noise according to the long-term average spectral shape of all continuous speech stimuli used in this study, with a bandwidth of 0–12,000 Hz. The same speech-shaped noise was also used as the background noise in the EEG experiment. The SNR levels used were the same as in the behavioural experiments. In the EEG experiment, the level of signal was -
fixed, and instead, the background noise varied according to the SNR. This SNR variation procedure is consistent with previous studies (Vanthornhout et al., 2018; Lesenfants et al., 2019) and ensures consistent neural responses to the acoustic envelope. All stimuli were calibrated using a Bruel and Kjaer Type 4230 sound level calibrator (Nærum, Denmark). Presentation of stimuli to the participants were controlled by a MATLAB script. Stimuli were presented through RME Fireface UC audio interface an-
d Etymotic ER-2 inserted earphones (Etymotic Research, Inc., Illinois, USA).
2.4. EEG experiment
EEG measurement was conducted after the behavioural test. EEG data were recorded using the BioSemi ActiveTwo 32-channel system (BioSemi, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The sampling rate was 2048 Hz. The EEG experiment consist of three experimental conditions varying in terms of stimuli: continuous speech, modulated noise, and /da/. Prior to the experiment, participants were instructed to avoid listening to the stimuli and pay attention to a muted documentary with subtitles presented on a screen. The cho-
ice of a documentary over other genre was based on other studies suggesting that movies where lip reading is possible can significantly degrade the quality of response measurement and that the auditory processing is not significantly affected while reading subtitles (Navarra, 2003; O’Sullivan et al., 2016). No questions related to the speech story were asked after the stimulus presentation as participants were asked not to attend to the stimulus. No questions about the documentary were asked eithe-
r. Attention to the stimuli could introduce a methodological bias in our measurements. Research has shown that when participants pay more attention to sounds, their brains show stronger tracking of the acoustic envelope (Vanthornhout et al., 2019; Reetzke et al., 2021). Since continuous speech is naturally more intelligible, participants tend to focus on it more easily, which could lead to stronger cortical tracking compared to other sounds. To make fair comparisons between different types of stim-
uli, we controlled for attention when measuring the relationship between cortical envelope tracking and SRT. In the first part of the experiment, the continuous speech and modulated noise were presented only in quiet at 65 dBA with a duration of 15 min for each stimulus. The backward temporal response function (TRF) approach (Crosse et al., 2016) was used to obtain the objective measures of EEG responses for the continuous speech and modulated noise conditions – as will be explained in detail late-
r in the ‘Temporal response function (TRF) estimation’ section. The second part of the EEG

2.5. Data analysis
No PTA results were used in data analysis; PTA was only used to confirm that all participants had normal hearing. The EEG data from each participant in all stimulus conditions were referenced to channels T7 and T8, leaving 30 channels available to be analysed. They were then normalised to give a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. EEG data were band-pass filtered using a zero-phase (non-causal) FIR filter (filter length was 6.6 times the reciprocal of the shortest transition band) over t-
he range of 1–4 Hz (delta frequency band) and 4–8 Hz (theta frequency band) and resampled at 128 Hz (Lesenfants et al., 2019; Vanthornhout et al., 2019). The sub-band EEG analysis was performed as these have previously been linked to processes of speech comprehension (e.g., understanding the meaning of speech and linking it to prior knowledge) and clarity of perceived speech (e.g., recognising words in background noise), respectively (Etard and Reichenbach, 2019). The stimulus envelope of the cont-
inuous speech and modulated noise for the TRF analysis were obtained using the Hilbert transform applied on the original signals at the sampling rate of 44.1 kHz. The Hilbert transform signal was then band-pass filtered using the same filter settings as in the EEG over the range of 1–4 Hz and 4–8 Hz, matching the frequency bands used in the EEG, and resampled at 128 Hz.
2.6. Temporal response function (TRF) estimation
This study utilised both the forward and the backward TRF approach for measuring the cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope. The correlation coefficient between the actual speech envelope and that reconstructed envelope from the multichannel EEG using the backward TRF (CORR) was the objective measure in attempting to predict the SRT using cortical responses to continuous speech and modulated noise. The forward TRF model weight (TRF-model) was used only to examine how the model weight, represen-
ting the extent to which the cortical response is positively or negatively related to the sound stimulus, is distributed across different time lags. No parameters from the forward TRF-model were used for predicting the SRT.

2.7. Statistical analysis for TRF approaches
For the forward and backward TRF analysis on cortical tracking of continuous speech and modulated noise envelope, the significance level for TRF-model and CORR was estimated using the bootstrapping method. This involved modelling randomly misaligned response and stimulus envelopes only from the quiet condition. The bootstrapping test was repeated 500 times (with different random delays between signals to remove any correlations) separately for the TRF-model and CORR within each individual, to obta-
in a null distribution of the bootstrapped TRF-model peak value and CORR. The true TRF-model when responses and stimulus are correctly aligned, is considered statistically significant when the peak value exceeds the upper or lower α = 0.025 of the null distribution. The true CORR is considered statistically significant when the correlation value exceeds the upper α = 0.05 of the null distribution. Friedman tests were used to test whether there were significant differences in CORR across the SNR le-
vels for the continuous speech and modulated noise stimulus condition. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon singed rank tests.

The mean behavioural SRT across 20 participants was 10.8 dB with a standard deviation of 1.1 dB, obtained by fitting a sigmoid to each individual’s median percentage of correct words and then averaging the SRTs. Fig. 7 shows the grand average ALR to /da/ at each SNR level in the delta and theta band. ALR to /da/ showed decrease in amplitude with decreasing SNR. Across the group there is a trend for the objective response parameters to

Fig. 9 highlights (in red diamonds) the points representing absolute differences greater than 7 dB between SRT and CT for each participant. Lesenfants et al. (2019) applied a generic backward TRF model (calculated using EEG data from all participants except the individual being tested) to each participant, whereas the current study applied subject-specific backward TRF. The purpose of Lesenfants et al. (2019) was to obtain the CORR using less EEG data. Aiming to enhance SRT predictions, we anticipated improvement through training data within individuals, however, the opposite outcome was observed. The CORR obtained through the generi-
c model might be less variable than the ones from the subject-specific model (Jessen et al., 2019). However, in some cases, the generic model may underperform the subject-specific model if the cortical responses across the cohort does not meet the homogeneity assumption (Di Liberto and Lalor, 2017). The selection of optimal EEG electrodes which generate significantly high CORR were also made in Lesenfants et al. (2019). The removal of EEG electrodes contributing less to the stimulus reconstruction-
 approach can significantly increase the CORR but may be challenging to optimise in individual recordings (Montoya-Martinez et al., 2021). Another factor affecting SRT prediction accuracy in this study is the exclusion of attention in the EEG experiment. While behavioural SRT tasks, such as the Matrix test, assess a listener’s ability to discriminate and identify sounds in noise—requiring attention and language processing—the non-attentive EEG approach focuses solely on whether the listener percei-
ves sound. Previous studies have demonstrated that cortical tracking is generally stronger under attention conditions, as attention enhances neural responses to target sounds while suppressing competing signals (Saiz-Alía et al., 2019; Vanthornhout et al., 2019). Although this limitation reduces the ability to capture attention-related cognitive processes critical for understanding speech in noisy environments, the non-attentive approach may still be practical for clinical populations, such as you-
ng children or individuals with cognitive impairments, where controlling attention is challenging. However, it does not provide a comprehensive assessment of speech-in-noise performance for the general population, where selective attention plays a crucial role in understanding speech in noise. In the condition using continuous speech, it is possible that participants constantly switch their attention between watching the documentary and listening to the story (Vanthornhout et al., 2019). Although,-
 the instruction was given for the participants to not pay attention to the story, it cannot be guaranteed that they followed the instruction throughout the whole session. This attention-switching behaviour can be reflected in the event-related potentials (ERP) waveform, where the amplitude and latency of the components in the waveform will be significantly different from that of the responses with no attention -switching task (Escera et al., 2001; Sussman et al., 2003). If the cortical tracking o-
f the speech envelope contains a succession of AERs (Nora et al., 2020), it may be continuously disrupted by this attention-switching activity. This might be one reason that the TRF-models from the continuous speech condition showed significantly lower amplitude compared to the TRF-models from the modulated noise condition (see Fig. 5B and D). In backward modelling, the attention-switching effect is also observed, with CORR at 0 and 5 dB in the theta band of the modulated noise condition being gre-
ater than that in the continuous speech condition (see Fig. 3B and D). As a result, this shift in the group-level CT brings it closer to the mean behavioural SRT at a more negative SNR level. The functional role of cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope is often suggested to be a combination between auditory processing of acoustic cues and cognitive abilities to combine the acoustic information to comprehend speech (Ding and Simon, 2014). The interaction between these two processes may cause c-
omplexity in interpreting the relationship between cortical tracking of acoustic envelope and speech comprehension. Song and Iverson (2018); Zou et al. (2019); Reetzke et al. (2021) demonstrated that attention may have more influence on the cortical tracking of acoustic envelope than the auditory processing. The current study minimized attentional effects by having participants watch a muted documentary with subtitles while ignoring the auditory
stimulus. Consequently, the measured cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope was likely driven primarily by acoustic processing. Reetzke et al. (2021) found that while attention enhances neural tracking of speech, this enhancement does not necessarily improve speech comprehension, particularly for non-native listeners. Similarly, Vanthornhout et al. (2019) showed that attention plays a crucial role in cortical tracking, particularly at higher SNRs. These findings, along with the current study’s-
 results, suggest that cortical responses to speech in non-attentive conditions may provide a more fundamental measure of auditory processing while failing to capture attention’s full influence on speech perception in noise. These observations highlight the need for research protocols that can disentangle acoustic and cognitive processing effects on EEG measures. Future studies should explore how attention modulates these interactions. Additionally, they should assess whether distinct neural marke-
rs can reliably separate auditory and cognitive contributions. Identifying such markers will be essential for developing methods that balance ecological validity with practicality for diverse populations. The cortical tracking of acoustic envelope in the delta band was suggested to be closely related to speech comprehension (Etard et al., 2019) and that SRT was predicted more accurately than in the theta band (Vanthornhout et al., 2018). The cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope in the theta -
band, rather than the delta band, was suggested to be more related to the clarity of the speech material. (Etard and Reichenbach, 2019). Etard and Reichenbach (2019) defined clarity of speech as the percentage of speech material that a native speaker can understand when speech is presented with background noise. In the current study, the cortical responses to speech and non-speech stimuli in the delta frequency band generate stronger responses than in the theta band, as indicated by the correlatio-
n coefficient between the actual and the estimated stimulus envelope. This agrees with previous studies reporting stronger responses to continuous speech in the delta band and more accurate SRT prediction than in the theta band (Vanthornhout et al., 2018; Lesenfants et al., 2019). However, the results from individuals do not fully support the notion that cortical responses in the delta band are more correlated with SRT than those in the theta band, since cortical responses to both natural speech a-
nd modulated noise in the theta band can be used to predict SRT. This may be due to the exclusion of attention task, so the cortical tracking of acoustic envelope only reflects the acoustic processing for responses to both natural speech and modulated noise. The results of the current study support the suggestion that cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope in the theta band is related to the clarity of perceived speech. According to Etard and Reichenbach (2019), speech clarity predominantly re-
flects the stimulus’s acoustic properties, particularly sound intensity variations captured by the acoustic envelope, which helps listeners segment words, phrases, and sentences. In the theta band, we found that cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope showed decreasing entrainment strength with lower stimulus SNR in both natural speech and modulated noise conditions. This trend aligns with the expected reduction in correlation as speech becomes noisier, suggesting that clarity may primarily rel-
ate to the acoustic envelope, which can be encoded in the EEG regardless of stimulus intelligibility to the listener. However, since this study did not involve attention tasks, it remains unclear whether this decrease also reflects a drop in speech comprehension. Overall, the results in the current study suggest that, when cognitive abilities are not heavily involved, cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope might mostly be related to the SNR of the stimulus. This finding applies to cortical tra-
cking of the acoustic envelope in both the delta and theta bands. TRF-models and ALR in the theta band exhibit remarkably similar waveform patterns and prominent peak locations (as shown in Fig. 5 Band D using all 30 EEG channels, and Fig. 7 theta band showing the response from the Cz channel only). This pattern resembles the P1-N1P2-N2 complex in ALR, or more specifically, the Cortical Auditory Evoked Potential (CAEP), which occurs at latencies of approximately

50–300 ms following stimulus onset (Gilley et al., 2005). Each component in the P1-N1-P2-N2 complex is associated with specific generators within the auditory cortex, and each generator serves a distinct role in auditory processing (Lightfoot, 2016). These components are primarily exogenous responses, meaning their occurrence is mainly, but not entirely, driven by the stimulus, in contrast to endogenous responses which are associated with cognitive processing. The implementation of a non-attendin-
g experimental paradigm in the current study may have contributed to the observed similarity between TRF-models and ALR in the theta band. This is particularly relevant as previous studies have reported that cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope in the delta band is associated with auditory attention (Attaheri et al., 2022; Van Hirtum et al., 2023). The separate analysis of TRF-models and ALR in the theta band may thus rule out attentional effects and more clearly reveal stimulus-driven respo-
nses. The similarity in patterns between TRF-models and ALR may therefore indicate that both reflect closely related auditory processing mechanisms that are predominantly stimulus-driven. The relationship between TRF-models and ALR in the delta band is more challenging to interpret, given this frequency band’s strong associations with attention, intelligibility, and other cognitive factors that were not measured in the current study. Based on the results shown in Fig. 5 (A and C) and Fig. 7 (delta-
 band), the waveforms of TRF-models and ALR in the delta band do not show clear similarities, unlike those observed in the theta band. The TRF in the delta band exhibited a strong negative peak at approximately 100 ms in the speech condition and at 300 ms in the modulated noise condition, whereas the ALR in the delta band showed a prominent positive peak around 200 ms. For a clinical application, the ALR to repeating /da/ may be more useful as they are faster to measure than cortical responses to -
continuous speech or modulated noise measured through the TRF. Based on the current study, within a shorter measurement time of 20 min compared to 55 min, the ALR to /da/ in the theta band provided similar accuracy for SRT prediction compared to cortical responses to modulated noise but was considerably more accurate than predictions using cortical responses to continuous speech, as measured by the percentage of predictions with an error greater than 7 dB. ALR may benefit from employing a repeatin-
g stimulus, as responses are generated using the coherent averaging technique. Measures of cortical responses using the TRF approach rely on fitting a linear model to the data, which can only be an approximation to the very complex neural processing of the stimuli and cannot fit the data well (as indicated by the low correlation coefficients found). It is thus prone to error (variability) from poor model fit, as well as the effects of random background activity. The ALR to repeating /da/ are based-
 on less stringent assumptions, which may partly explain why these simple stimuli performed better in some respects than the TRF approach in predicting the SRT. However, overall, all methods tested in this study are too inaccurate in predicting the SRT for individual subjects to be used in clinical applications.
5. Conclusion
This study demonstrated that it is possible to predict SRT using cortical responses to continuous speech, modulated noise, and /da/ when participants do not attend to the stimulus. However, the accuracy of SRT prediction was poor. The results showed that, at the group level, SRT predictions using EEG recordings were, at best, within 1 dB of the behavioural SRT and, at worst, 10.1 dB apart. Responses to modulated noise and /da/ provided a significantly greater number of individual SRT predictions c-
onsidered applicable (error within 7 dB) in individuals compared to cortical responses to continuous speech, particularly in the theta band. While this non-attentive approach might benefit specific populations where attention cannot be controlled, it would require complementary attention-based measures for clinical assessment of the general population. However, the method in its current form is not ready for clinical application due to inconsistent predictions and large
individual errors. Moreover, since SRT can be predicted from cortical responses to modulated noise, cortical tracking of the acoustic envelope may not specifically measure speech intelligibility in the human brain.
