From Pixels to Patterns: Decoding Smartphone Display Properties through Diffraction, Reflection, and Refraction
Mamatha Ramanjineyulu Maddur1, Hemansh Shah2, and Praveen Pathak1∗
1Homi Bhabha Centre for Science Education-TIFR Mankhurd, Mumbai, India and 2Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore, India
In this paper we show how students can measure optical features of smartphone displays through three experiments. Observing diffraction patterns from smartphone displays allows students to determine the Pixels Per Inch (PPI). Observing reflections within a smartphone display provides information about touch glass thickness and pixel layer properties. Finally, water drops are used as miniature lenses to see the magnified image of the pixels beneath. An enhanced theoretical model that covers both sm-
all and large droplets is provided.
I. INTRODUCTION
Students spend a lot of time looking at their phones. Why not give them a new way to look at them? Here we explore three optical phenomena of typical smartphones, namely, diffraction, reflection, and refraction. Smartphone, laptop, and TV displays produce distinctive diffraction and interference patterns, as noted in earlier studies [1–3]. In this work, we explore three related experiments using such displays: measuring pixel density (PPI) via diffraction, estimating touch glass thickness through reflection patterns, and refining water-drop lens analysis to account for thick lens effects. II. DIFFRACTION PATTERN OF PIXELS

Magnified view, as seen through a microscope, of the phone’s display is shown in Fig. 2(a) and (b). Here, the different colored regions differ in reflectivity and act as scattering centres. lting in interference patterns such as those shown in Fig. 2(c) and (d) respectively. V. CONCLUSION
Our exploration of smartphone display optics unveils intriguing connections between diffraction patterns, reflections, and lens behavior. The diffraction experiment enables teachers and students to measure the PPI of their phone and demonstrate the smartphone display’s potential as an optical grating. In some modern phones, the angled arrangement of pixels creates an X-shaped diffraction pattern, offering a unique challenge for students to explore. Reflection studies not only revealed the thicknes-
s of the touch glass and potential air gap but also highlighted the pixel display’s properties. Considering the curved shape and Snell’s law, the water drop experiment yielded experimental data consistent with our theoretical predictions. Auxiliary measurements of contact angles and characteristic parameters underscored the reliability of our methodologies. In principle, all three experiments can be performed on any other smartphone. Overall, these experiments provide accessible and educational in-
sights into the hidden optical features of smartphone displays. These insights are valuable for both scientific exploration and educational demonstrations.
