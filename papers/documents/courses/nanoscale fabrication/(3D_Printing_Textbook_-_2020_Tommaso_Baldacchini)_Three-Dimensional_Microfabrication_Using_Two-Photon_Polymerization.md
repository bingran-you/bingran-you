Foreword: Here, a small step toward a grand vision
“natura nihil facit supervacaneum” (nature creates nothing superfluous) et “multum in parvo” (much in little)
In the broadest interpretation, three-dimensional (3D) manufacturing/microfabrication embodies a number of material processing techniques where layer by layer or controlled self-assembly on a scaffolding is utilized to fashion a desired 3D structure. Upon cursory examination, 3D manufacturing appears to be an efficient means to make something, because regardless of the product shape, there is minimum waste. Only the amount of material necessary is used, no more, no less. The approach adheres to a -
manufacturing ideal where material properties can be varied or graded on a part, almost at will, and function can follow form from the nano to the meso scale. It has some likeness to biology where material is grown, cell by cell or in this case by form-function to form-function. In the most soaring of visions, a 3D manufactured product would not only have the necessary complex shape but also include properties for “sensing” the local environment, harvesting energy, and “communicating” its state. T-
his would be accomplished by the seamless integration of materials and functionality. We are far from realizing this vision, but recent strides in fields such as materials development and controlled process tooling bring hope that 3D manufacturing could impact the world much like the industrial and electronics revolutions. One particular area where materials development/characterization has been most congruent with process tooling is the use of controlled polymerization to fabricate 3D structures.-
 The technology (i.e., stereolithography) was the first to gain wide commercial acceptance in the genre of direct write additive manufacturing (AM) and mostly applied to fabricating prototype structures with feature resolutions measured on the order of 100 μm. This assembled work places its focus on a subset of AM technology, two-photon polymerization, where minimum linewidth and writing resolution of 9 and 52 nm, respectively, have already been achieved.
Two-photon polymerization (TPP) technology utilizes the high intensity that is available from femtosecond (fs) lasers to controllably breach a reactivity threshold in a polymer resin to induce polymerization and thereby form a free-standing structure. Complex structures can be formed by interfering laser beams, holography (i.e., continuous image projection) or by laser direct write. One advantage to the latter approach is the ease of fashioning both structure and site-specific defects. TPP technol-
ogy continues to achieve hallmarks for precision, resolution, and formed structure complexity because it addresses the issue from many different technical points, many of which are detailed in this assembled work. There is the desire to gain control of the photo absorption process

Brushing aside these technical achievements, critics commonly fault TPP technology for its impractical throughput for piece part fabrication. It is an unfair accusation made by critics who envision TPP being applied on the scale of many centimeters and larger. That is not the strength of the TPP technology. TPP will always be a single technique within the hierarchical 3D manufacturing schema, but it provides a key link. By virtue of the feature sizes that TPP technology can fashion, it provides a -

It is the ability to fabricate structures that can affect processes at the molecular and biological length scales and thereby derive a better understanding of how local disturbances affect process evolution. Nota bene: While microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) technology permitted the “reach” into t-
he microworld, it is NEMS (nanoelectromechanical systems) technology that actually “touches” it. Three-dimensional fabrication by TPP expands the micromanufacturing genre by permitting the near free-form fabrication of submicron/nanometer structures without the structural constraints imposed by silicon material processing.
For TPP to go beyond just the fashioning of prototype structures to the fabrication of viable commercial commodities, issues regarding process control, increases in piece part throughput and repeatability have to be addressed. One of the strengths of this assembled work is that it not only presents the state of art, it also addresses these critical issues, along with an assessment of current limitations. For example, approaches to making TPP faster are addressed directly (e.g., the implementation -
of multiple laser beams and parallel processing to the use of digital light modulators/spatial light modulators). There is also a look into the future with possible near- and far-term applications (e.g., structures within microfluidics/lab-on-chip, scaffolding to produce tissue mimics/models for administering patient-specific therapeutics for disease control and reduced remission) and an assessment of material processing technologies that can likely be merged/clustered with TPP to develop an integ-
rated (i.e., hybrid process) tool. While there are applications in which the desired structure can be elegantly fashioned on a desk top TPP tool, I believe TPP or its variant will find maximum market insertion when it can be merged among a cluster of 3D or AM manufacturing tools designed to manufacture macro scale objects, in which TPP is used to fabricate critical submicron structures at specific locations. Introduction
Introduction to the First Edition
Interest in three-dimensional microfabrication is driven by a number of advanced applications in fields as diverse as photonics, microfluidics, and tissue engineering to name a few. In all these research areas, the requirements for fabricating parts call for fast prototyping of self-supported objects with arbitrary complex geometries. Preferably, the manufacturing technology chosen to deliver this goal will allow the user to pick among a large variety of materials and to pattern them on scale leng-
ths that go from hundreds of nanometers to tens of centimeters. In works where the need is of “one-of-a-kind” threedimensional microstructure, processing time is typically not a priority. Otherwise, methods to accelerate parts throughput must be taken into consideration.
In the past 20 years, there has been a tremendous effort by a multitude of research groups in developing and improving direct writing technologies that are capable of manufacturing three-dimensional objects with high spatial fidelity and resolution [1]. Examples of these advancements can be found throughout the vast arena of writing techniques based on both energy beams (photon, electrons, and ions) and ink flows (dropletbased and continuous) where complex parts are created either by a layer-by-la-
yer or by free-form approach [2,3].
Laser-based direct-writing techniques are particularly attractive for fabricating threedimensional microstructures [4]. Indeed, depending on the laser excitation wavelength and whether or not the laser output is continuous or pulsed, lasers can be used to drive a wide range of physical and chemical processes, which are then the base for processing almost any materials. Furthermore, ongoing progress in lasers manufacturing is making these sources of lights more reliable, less expensive, and easier -
to operate, thus lowering the barrier of their use in academic and commercial applications.
Among laser direct-writing three-dimensional microfabrication techniques, twophoton polymerization (TPP) enjoys a unique set of characteristics. First, because spatial confinement of polymerization is an intrinsic property of TPP, no topological constraints are present in the fabrication of three-dimensional microstructures. Second, subdiffraction-limited feature sizes can be attained by employing laser intensities just above the intensity threshold at which polymerization occurs. Third, movable c-
omponents can easily be created without the use of sacrificial layers. And lastly, the carbonbased nature of the materials employed in TPP can be used as a chemical handle to fabricate structures with tunable physical and chemical properties such as hardness, shrinkage, index of refraction, and chemical specificity. Therefore, it is not surprising that TPP uses have rapidly grown in recent years. In a short amount of time, TPP has gone

Three-dimensional microfabrication using photopolymerization induced by a nonlinear optical process (multiphoton absorption) has been named and it is still named in different forms. Two-photon microstereolithography, multiphoton lithography, two-photon polymerization, two-photon microfabrication, and threedimensional direct laser writing are only few examples that can be found in the scientific literature. The first works in this field assumed that the number of photons involved in the excitation -
of photoinitiators was two. Hence, many of the original papers employ “two-photon” in their title. Based on a large number of experimental clues, it is evident now that the mechanism for generating the active species responsible for polymerization is not as straightforward as initially thought and that the order of photon absorption is two or higher depending on laser excitation conditions and type of photoinitiator used. The title of the present book uses the words two-photon out of tradition. It-
 does not imply that only two-photon-induced polymerization is

discussed; it is used to indicate intrinsically three-dimensional photopolymerization process based on multiphoton absorption.
The history of two-photon polymerization is interesting and it dates back to the first years of laser development. It was in fact 1965 when Rentzepis and Pao published an article in Applied Physics Letters with findings about the photopolymerization of styrene using ruby laser [10]. Since no sensitizers were used and styrene is completely transparent to the emission of the excitation source, the authors of this work concluded that the only plausible mechanism for the observed result was a two-phot-
on absorption mechanism. Rentzepis and Pao explain that “the present experiment represents the first known case of a multiphoton process terminating in a specific chemical reaction. It is to be expected that this is a general phenomenon and that other multiphoton photochemical reactions may also be induced and studied; photoinitiation of polymerization is, however, a particular suitable experiment in that chain propagation in free radical addition polymerization enhances the physical effects of an-
y radical created by laser radiation.” With the current knowledge of TPP, these words sound almost like a prophecy now. We need to wait 25 years since the paper of Rentzepis and Pao to find the first reports where polymerization induced by multiphoton absorption and three-dimensional microfabrication appear in the same place. A book chapter written by Cabrera, Jezequel, and Andre in 1990 discussed the fundamentals of a “multiphotonic” process to localize photopolymerization without a practical dem-
onstration; in this report three-dimensional microfabrication is foreseen by crossing two laser beams [11]. In the same year, a conference proceeding by Webb and Strickler showed experimental evidence of polymerization induced by the simultaneous absorption of two photons coming from the same laser [12]. The authors of this proceeding wrote that “two-photon excitation of photoinitiated polymerization with a sharply focused single beam allows microfabrication of complex structures of arbitrary form-
. By scanning the focused beam through a liquid polymer with a UV excited initiator it is possible to harden the polymer only at the focus thereby creating 3 dimensional objects formed with a resolution volume of the order of the optical wavelength.” The basic experimental methodology for performing TPP was described first in this paper and fundamentally used later on by the first adaptors of this technology. The microstructure produced using TPP by Webb and Strickler work was two dimensional; qui-
te ironic if one considers that this is probably the first TPP microstructures to be printed in the scientific literature. The first three-dimensional microstructure fabricated by TPP was presented in 1997 by Maruo, Nakamura, and Kawata in a paper published in Optics Letters [13]. It consisted of a coil with a diameter of 7 μm and a total length of approximately 34 μm. This paper is cited by many as the first report on TPP. Although it is the first article to reproduce a three-dimensional polymeri-
c microstructures created with this technique, I believe it is important to not forget the previous contributions. To end this journey in the history of TPP development, an important year is 1999. In that year, a research group led by Perry and Marder published

Introduction to the Second Edition
The opportunity for an editor to publish a book second edition is one not to lose. Not only in fact a second edition allows the authors to revise their chapters with up-to-date information, but it gives the editor the space to add new material. With this reasoning, I accepted the publisher offer to edit the second edition of Three-Dimensional Microfabrication Using Two-Photon Polymerization. In the new text, I have given special attention to subjects that deals with materials used in TPP and their-

1 Beyond photolithography: Direct-write microfabrication
Direct-write processes have evolved into viable techniques for printing functional materials over diverse types of surfaces for the digital fabrication of microelectronic devices. These simple, relatively fast, low-cost, and environment-friendly alternatives to traditional photolithographic processes have the potential to completely change the way micro- to mesoscale structures are presently fabricated. Direct-write by definition refers to any technique or process capable of deposition, dispensing-
, or processing different types of materials over arbitrary surfaces following a preset pattern or layout [1]. Direct-write techniques are not based on a single process but instead encompass a diverse, yet highly versatile set of processes extending over multilength scales. It is important to distinguish direct-write processes from traditional rapid prototyping or additive manufacturing (AM) techniques such as fused deposition modeling (FDM) and powder-bed melting or sintering 3D printing. Unlike -
these techniques, direct-write describes processes capable of precisely depositing or modifying functional materials on a substrate over digitally defined locations. Direct-write thus differs from traditional AM in its ability to write a wide range of materials, such as metals, ceramics, polymers, and organics with resolutions down to the submicron over any type of surface or substrate. Thus, in direct-write, the deposited material and the substrate become the part.
From a microfabrication perspective, there is an ever-growing demand for products that rely on a wide range of assembled microcomponents driven by the insatiable demand for portable electronics, sensors, and diagnostics for healthcare and the development of new autonomous systems. There is also a desire to implement all of these microcomponents in 3D geometries rather than traditional 2D designs, which pushes traditional methods such as lithography-based fabrication approaches to its limits. Here -

2 Introduction to nonlithographic microfabrication techniques
Direct-write processes rely on the dispensing, transfer or printing of discrete lumps of material known as voxels (i.e., volumetric 3D pixels) at predetermined locations on a substrate. These voxels serve as the building blocks for the lines, layers, and 3D structures required to fabricate a device. Thus the term direct-write is used to describe any process or technique that comprises motion control hardware and a discrete source of material or energy, operated in unison via computer control to ge-
nerate predetermined patterns. This definition is quite broad and covers a wide spectrum of methods for material transfer and processing over a surface across length scales that can range from tens of nanometers to millimeters and writing speeds that range from micrometers per hour to kilometers per second. Because of this wide operating space, precise classification of direct-write techniques is difficult at best and many techniques belong to a class of their own. Notwithstanding this, it is help-
ful to group the various techniques and methods developed to date based on the mechanism by which the transfer and/or processing of material takes place.
In general, all direct-write techniques transfer or modify voxels of material by four basic mechanisms: dispensing, flow, particle beam, or laser. 3 Laser-based microfabrication
To date, a wide range of processes exist for microfabrication purposes, with lithography reigning supreme. Despite their widespread use, many of these processes achieve limited success when employed in applications calling for complicated and high-resolution 3D microstructures with high aspect ratios and nontrivial geometries. In fact, many new technologies such as microfluidic devices, photonic crystals, micropumps, microactuators, micromotors, microturbines, and many other scaled-down assemblies-
 cannot easily be produced via lithographic techniques. New approaches based on direct-write concepts applied at much reduced scales need to be considered to expand the capabilities of microfabrication techniques to generate true 3D structures at the microscale and nanoscale. The basic premise of AM techniques, that is the fabrication of a design layer by layer, can be similarly applied to generate 3D microstructures. As a result, there is broad and rich spectrum of techniques currently available -
for achieving 3D micro-AM. Those techniques vary depending on the nature of the design, material choice, and target application of the component or part to be produced. For a comprehensive list of the wide range of micro-AM processes and techniques currently available, the reader is directed to the review by Vaezi et al. [12], since the aim of this chapter is to discuss laser-based microAM processes, in particular those known as laser direct-write or LDW.
From the earliest reports of light amplification by stimulated emission, lasers have been used for materials processing applications to modify, add, and subtract material from a surface or a bulk sample. The emission from a wide range of laser sources can be used to control or modify the composition, structure and even properties of single 3D volumes or voxels (as opposed to 2D areas or pixels) across length scales spanning the range from millimeters to nanometers. These processes are able to remo-
ve (i.e., laser micromachining), add (i.e., laser printing), and modify (i.e., multiphoton polymerization) different types of materials without physical contact between a tool (i.e., printing nozzle) and the material under process. Since these processes are able to create patterns in a serial or “voxel-by-voxel” fashion, they are referred to as direct-write as opposed to those that require masks or previously laid patterns (i.e., lithography, stamping, and self-assembly) in order to achieve the de-
sired design. Although laser-based direct-write (LDW for short) processes are serial by design (as opposed to the parallel nature of photolithography), their processing speed can be greatly increased by using high-power, high-repetition lasers and ultrafast beam scanning techniques such as polygon scanning mirrors. With LDW, the

3.1 Advantages of laser-based techniques for 3D microfabrication
Beginning with their operating principle, the result of the interaction of light with a material, LDW enables the localized and well-defined processing of many classes of materials in order to generate arbitrary structures. Since those interactions are well understood, and since the region illuminated with the laser beam can be controlled precisely, LDW techniques have shown great success in microfabrication applications. This ability to siteselectively modify the properties of a given material at-
 the micro- or nanoscale is one of the main advantages of LDW. To achieve similar site-selective control using a parallel processing technique such as lithography requires multiple masking steps, thus becoming less practical. As highlighted by Livingston and Helvajian, photolithography relies on the fact that all exposed areas to be processed receive an equivalent photon dosage or flux, while LDW takes advantage of the effect that each of the exposed areas can receive a different yet optimized pho-
ton flux [13]. This optimized photon flux can consist of a sequence of laser pulses each of which can have a prescribed amplitude, pulse width, wavelength and/or polarization. Such processing recipes allow precise control of the delivery of laser energy to each voxel in terms of spatial and temporal profiles. No other parallel processing or direct-write technique can approach LDW’s level of precision and selectivity in the means of delivery of the energy required to induce a change in the properti-
es of a voxel of material. This aspect is what gives LDW its unique capabilities in the areas of materials processing and photomediated materials modification for microfabrication applications such as microstereo lithography (the subject of the following chapter) and TPP (the subject of the rest of this book).

3.2 Laser micromachining
In laser machining, the fundamental interaction that leads to the removal of material can be athermal (ablation) or thermal (melting and vaporization) and depends primarily on the nature of the laser-material interaction, which itself is a function of the laser wavelength and laser pulse duration. For micromachining, athermal processes, with their reduced heat-affected zone (HAZ), are preferable and enable generation of features smaller than the diffraction limit [14]. The semiconductor and microe-
lectronics industries were one of the earlier adopters of laser micromachining. Driven by an ever-increasing demand for miniaturization, weight and power reduction and processing power and speed, subtractive LDW or laser micromachining has achieved great success in a wide range of microfabrication applications such as laser drilling of via holes for stacked or built-up printed circuit boards, photomask repair, marking, trimming, dicing, and patterning of flat panel displays and solar cells [15]. T-
he reason for the success of laser micromachining in these areas is the ability of LDW processes to achieve higher speeds, precision, and reliability, in fewer steps and in a more environmentally sustainable fashion than with conventional photolithographic processes. Other industrial applications of laser

micromachining can be found in the fabrication of nozzles for precision fuel injectors and inkjet printers and in the high-resolution cutting and texturing of stents and other implantable parts for biomedical applications [16].

4 Laser-based additive microfabrication
For those laser-based microfabrication applications that require the deposition or placement of material in a predetermined fashion, additive LDW processes such as LCVD and LIFT are two of the most successful techniques currently in use. The advantages of both of these techniques for microadditive manufacturing applications stem from the decoupling in their implementation of their energy and material sources. As opposed to other direct-write techniques, in both LCVD and LIFT, the sources of materi-
al to be printed (a precursor gas in LCVD and a solid or fluid in LIFT) are totally independent of the source of energy needed for printing. Consequently, both techniques are unaffected by issues such as contamination of the source material, clogging of the material delivery system, and/or limited ability to print different types of materials, which negatively impact many other direct-write processes.
4.1 Laser chemical vapor deposition (LCVD)
Both LCVD and LIFT have been used to fabricate 3D microstructures; however, LCVD is limited by the small amount of material that can be delivered to the location of interest by the use of gaseous metal-organic precursors; thus, its writing speed cannot exceed more than 0.1 mm/s [17] and must take place inside a vacuum to avoid contamination and dilution of the source precursor, which limits the ability of LCVD to generate patterns over an existing structure [18]. The decomposition pathways of the -
precursor gas in LCVD can be either photoactivated (photolytic LCVD) or thermal (pyrolytic LCVD). In either case, it is possible to achieve submicron 3D structures of a wide range of materials

4.2 Laser-induced forward transfer (LIFT)
LIFT uses laser pulses of sufficient intensity aimed through a transparent substrate coated with a thin layer of material to achieve discrete (or digital) material transfer in the forward laser direction. The transferred material can be collected on a separate substrate facing the thin layer. The straightforwardness of this approach led to many groups to try it with different types of materials and despite its inherent simplicity, LIFT exists in many different variations [19]. LIFT is compatible w-
ith virtually any type of material, and takes place under ambient atmospheric conditions, thus making it one of the most versatile digital microfabrication techniques developed to date. The LIFT techniques exemplify additive LDW, given their capability for generating high-resolution patterns without the need for lithographic steps afterwards [1, 19, 20]. In fact, the same equipment setup required by LIFT can be used for other LDW processes such as subtractive (laser micromachining), and modifying -
(laser sintering, laser annealing, etc.) [21]. What makes LIFT unique is its ability to laser transfer such a wide variety of materials with relatively high resolution conformal to the surface. For example, the LIFT process does not have a deleterious effect on the electrical, chemical, and even biological properties of the voxels of material forming the digital pattern. The ability to laser transfer functional materials and then process them or modify them in order to achieve the required propert-
ies and behavior is unique and represents one of the major advantages of laser-based microfabrication techniques.

5 2D microfabrication by LIFT
Since the initial reports of laser-transferred copper metal patterns by Bohandy et al. over 30 years ago [22], the use and development of LIFT has grown steadily. These simple yet powerful techniques employ a pulsed laser to locally transfer material from a source film onto a substrate in close proximity or in contact with the film, thus achieving the LDW of patterns independent of the type of surface. The source is typically a coated lasertransparent substrate, referred to as the target, donor, o-
r ribbon. Laser pulses propagate through the transparent donor carrier and are absorbed by the film. Above an incident laser energy threshold, material is ejected from the film and propelled toward the acceptor or receiving substrate. Translation of the source and receiving substrate, or scanning and modulation of the laser beam, enables complex pattern formation in three dimensions with speed typically limited by the laser repetition rate. 5.1 Printing of functional materials
Despite its successful application to the deposition of thin metal layers, the actual uses of the LIFT process are limited due to several shortcomings. In traditional LIFT, metal donor films are required to be deposited on the ribbon by conventional vacuum deposition techniques, which must be done ahead of time. Since these metal films tend to be very thin (a few hundred nanometers), the individual layers deposited by LIFT are similarly thin, thus limiting its application to lithographic mask repa-
ir and other niche areas. Moreover, during LIFT, the metals undergo rapid phase transformation during ejection from the donor substrate and subsequent transfer resulting on melting and solidification leading to the formation of interfaces between adjacent voxels. Such interfaces can have deleterious effects in the electrical transport properties of the patterned structure being fabricated. Furthermore, the melting of the transferred material must be taken into consideration when LIFT is performed -
under atmospheric conditions, because most metals tend to oxidize when melted in the presence of air, while the rapid quenching of the metal voxels once ejected can result in high intrinsic stresses between the transferred metal and the substrate. Similarly, traditional LIFT is not suited for the transfer of ceramics and other inorganic phases given the irreversible phase changes and decomposition that tend to be exhibited by these materials upon melting and solidification.

This is very critical since many types of materials, in particular complex multicomponent and multiphase systems can-

5.1.1 LIFT of nanoinks The use of LIFT for the deposition of high-quality functional materials requires the generation of structures comprising multiple voxels, adjacent or on top of each other, that readily merge to form a single, continuous pattern. Electrical interconnects provide a perfect example of this requirement as heterogeneous interfaces between voxels can degrade the overall conductivity. The application of LIFT for printing nanoinks is the enabling step that makes possible the printin-
g of functional materials without their degradation. This is accomplished by enabling the transferred material to remain fluid, whereby adjacent voxels on the receiving substrate will merge into one continuous segment. Fig. 1.1.3 shows a simple schematic illustrating the basic steps involved in LIFT of low-viscosity nanoinks.
In fact, LIFT of complex suspensions such as inks or pastes is very different from traditional LIFT processes since the materials suspensions are printed without direct vaporization. As shown schematically in Fig. 1.1.3, a small region of the laser-absorbing ink interacts with a low fluence (<100 mJ/cm2), laser pulse vaporizing a small amount of the solvent in the suspension. As the trapped vapor expands, it generates a bubble, which, upon collapse, ejects a droplet from the film toward the receiv-
ing substrate, where it is deposited with its original rheological properties intact. The results obtained with transfers of extremely laser-sensitive systems, such as buffer solutions containing biomaterials, proteins, and living cells [42], or electrochemically sensitive materials [43], confirm that most if not all of the transferred fluid does not interact with the laser pulse.
The uniqueness of the mechanisms during laser printing of rheological systems resides in the fact that they represent a new LIFT process based on the nonphasetransforming forward transfer of complex suspensions, inks, or pastes. This is made possible by the reduced shear forces required to dislodge and release the portion of the coating in the donor substrate illuminated by a laser pulse. In fact, under these conditions, LIFT can be carried out at lower laser energy fluences, thus effectively taki-
ng place without any ablation or heating of the transferred material. Given the diverse nature and large number of parameters affecting the laser transfer process for rheological systems, such a simple description cannot be expected to completely explain its behavior. 5.2 Printing of high-viscosity nanopastes for congruent transfers
A shortcoming of the laser transfer of fluids or inks is the generation of satellite droplets during transfer that result in debris formation on the donor substrate with deleterious effects to the achievable resolution. The use of ribbons thinly coated ( 1 μm) with high-viscosity (>100,000 cps) nanoinks or nanopastes has been shown to mitigate this problem [52]. This process, which has been termed laser decal transfer (LDT), allows the laser transfer of voxels congruent with the spatial profile of-
 the laser pulse. LIFT of congruent voxels can achieve feature fidelity and thickness uniformity comparable to lithographically patterned thin films [53]. The key to LDT is the use of thin and highly viscous nanoink layers in the ribbon, which can be sheared away from the transparent donor substrate with very low laser fluences, usually below 200 mJ/cm2, without deforming or breaking apart the shape of the thin layer. This new approach represents a significant advance in LIFT-based direct-write gi-
ven the improved spatial resolution (down to 2 μm), increased thickness uniformity (within 50 nm), sharper edge features, and minimal surrounding debris compared to traditional LIFT.
Fig. 1.1.5 demonstrates the fidelity of transfers achieved by LDT by comparing the resulting transfers by LIFT of two different types of silver nanosuspensions. 5.4 LIFT of biomaterials
LIFT of biomaterials such as biomolecules offers several advantages to commonly used biomicrofabrication techniques such as dot blotting, microcontact printing, and inkjet printing. The main advantages are due to the nozzle-free, noncontact printing capabilities that LIFT has to offer when processing all kinds of biomaterials in liquid-phase, such as biomolecules in aqueous solutions. Notwithstanding the fragile nature of most biomolecules, their interaction with the lasers used in LIFT is limited-
 since only very small volumes of the solution, which carry the biomolecules are evaporated in the process. An extensive body of literature exists detailing many different biomolecules printed by LIFT starting from work performed in the early 2000s at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory [42]. Several of the chapters in the book by Piqu e and Serra [19] and a recent review on the fundamentals and applications of LIFT [23] cover the subject in great detail. LIFT has also been used to print 2D and als-
o 3D structures of soft materials such as viscous hydrogels (alginates), which are of great interest for cell biology, printing of host structures for cell cultures, and more importantly the printing of 3D structures for scaffoldings needed for tissue engineering applications. LIFT of alginates for this purpose has been shown by Huang’s group at the University of Florida [63].
LIFT of cells, also known as bioprinting, offers many applications in biotechnology and medicine. For example, LIFT can be used to control the spatial arrangement of cells, which is of great importance for the study and understanding of cell response to their environment and/or other cells nearby. LIFT can also be used to print heterogeneous structures comprising different types of cells or cells with other biomaterials such as the alginates already mentioned to generate structures similar to tiss-

An example of what LIFT of biomaterials can do is shown in Fig. 1.1.6, which shows how LIFT can be used to print 2D patterns and multilayers of different types of cells such as fibroblasts and keratinocytes [64]. These LIFT-printed biostructures made layer by layer, which mimic the layered organization found in human skin, are being developed with the goal of printing skin substitutes for promoting skin tiss-

7 Parallelizing the LIFT process The use of spatial light modulators, based on commercial digital micromirror devices or DMDs, makes possible the dynamic adjustment of the size and shape of the laser pulse and thus the ability to print reconfigurable congruent voxels [68, 70]. The high refresh rate (kHz) of the DMD thus enables the printing of different patterns with each laser shot. In fact, by spatially adjusting the laser pulse used with LIFT, through a micromirror-based spatial light modulator-
, it is possible to print dynamically reconfigurable voxels. That is, each voxel can vary in size and shape from the previous one, thus allowing faster pattern generation and overall optimization of the sequence of printing steps required to generate a full design. Furthermore, it is a natural extension to use a DMD with LIFT of congruent

In addition, the DMD can “imprint” any bitmap pattern onto the laser beam, which is not entirely possible with an aperture. One example is the printing of grids. In fact, LIFT of congruent voxels combined with a DMD to achieve dynamic control of the spatial distribution of the laser pulse shows how LDW techniques are evolving from simple serial printing of drops to fully parallelized micro-AM technologies.

8 Summary
Among the numerous direct-write techniques available for microfabrication, laser-based direct-write or LDW is best suited for the production of 3D microstructures. These techniques allow the generation of patterns, structures, and devices not possible with traditional photolithographic processes. Furthermore, since the source of energy is decoupled from the material to be processed, LDW techniques are not affected by contamination or nozzle-clogging issues as opposed to other direct-write processe-
s. As this chapter highlights, LDW techniques can be subtractive, modifying, or additive in nature, depending on their implementation. This versatility is one of the great strengths of LDW and is what makes these techniques so widely applicable to almost any type of microfabrication application. In fact, the combination of various LDW techniques such as LIFT (additive) with laser micromachining (subtractive) in order to trim or adjust a printed pattern can be performed using the same basic setup a-
nd system. What this means is that LDW processes literally enable the operations of “writing” and “erasing” with a single tool on virtually any surface with any type of material. That is the power of LDW.

1 Introduction
Microstereolithography is a 3D microfabrication technology that is fundamentally different from the techniques commonly used in cleanroom environment for the manufacturing of microelectromechanical system (MEMS) components. Most microfabrication techniques evolved from the microelectronics industry and often use silicon wafers as substrate or carrier material, thin films of metals deposited by evaporation or sputtering, thin layers of polymers deposited by spin coating and patterned by photolithog-
raphy, chemical and plasma etching to generate various shapes. Microstereolithography is also a microfabrication technique, but it is related to rapid prototyping technologies, and more precisely to stereolithography (SLA), a technique patented in 1986 [1], allowing the fabrication of 3D components by layer-by-layer curing of a photopolymerizable resin with an ultraviolet (UV) laser. Microstereolithography is based on a manufacturing principle very similar to the one of SLA, but implements process-
 improvements that result in a far better resolution.
The first developments of the microstereolithography technique have been published in 1993, and a considerable effort has been first made to explore different ways to modify the SLA process to achieve a better lateral and vertical resolution. This resulted in a number of machines being developed by different research teams and using a very diverse range of components: different light sources, optical elements, irradiation configurations, etc. These machines can seem very dissimilar, but all of the-
m have in common the use of a space-resolved light-induced photopolymerization of a liquid resin to create small-size 3D components by a superimposition of a large number of thin polymer layers. If today, the word “microstereolithography” is commonly used to describe this technique, many designations have been used by the research teams who published the first reports, such as IH-process, optical forming, spatial forming, microphotoforming, microstereophotolithography, and 3D optical modeling. All-
 the processes that have been imagined can be classified in three main categories: - Scanning microstereolithography machines in which every layer of the object is made
by a vector-by-vector tracing with a light beam, tightly focused on the surface of the photosensitive resin.

2 Rapid prototyping and stereolithography
Rapid prototyping technologies, also called additive manufacturing technologies, are a group of processes allowing the rapid production of large-size models and prototypes, and their manufacturing layer-by-layer. Additive manufacturing processes are opposed to subtractive methods, such as CNC (computer numerical control) milling, turning, and grinding, where the object is built by cutting away material from a block and they are also different from formative methods, such as injection molding, impr-
inting, or casting, where the object is built using a mold in which material is solidified.
Additive manufacturing methods have in common that they rely on CAD (computer-aided design) for the design of the object to be built, then this virtual object is oriented in a way that will allow its manufacturing by the chosen method, sliced in a number of 2D layers by a dedicated slicing program (which may add supports if needed) and built layer-by-layer. Additive manufacturing technologies have the advantage to be able to produce objects with extreme geometric complexity and intricate details a-
s well as assembled components, in a relatively fast and simple process.

Rapid prototyping technologies had a strong impact on industrial product development as they reduce significantly the time to market in manufacturing. If the first domain of application of rapid prototyping technologies was the creation of components used for the physical verification of CAD models, for form fitting and for functional testing, many other applications have been found when these technologies became more affordable, including prototypes for design reviews, models for geology and arch-

SLA has been the first rapid prototyping method developed and commercialized, and it is still today the most used by the industry, as it is the fastest among additive manufacturing technologies, it is affordable, and the obtained parts have excellent surface finish even without additional operations. The typical resolution of SLA is in the range of 150 μm in the three directions of space, but as it is based on the superimposition of layers, resolution, precision and surface roughness are anisotrop-
ic. In particular, the vertical (along the build axis) resolution is related to the thickness of the superimposed layers, whereas the lateral (in-plane) resolution depends on the dimension of the light beam used to draw the shape of each layer, at the surface of the resin.
As microstereolithography is directly related to SLA, it is relatively simple to understand the choices that have been made to develop this process by studying how the resolution of SLA can be improved.

3 Improving stereolithography resolution
As the resolution of SLA is inherently anisotropic, different factors will affect its resolution in the vertical and horizontal directions.
3.1 Reducing the thickness of the layers
Even if the objects made by SLA are formed by the superimposition of layers, the thickness of the layer of fresh liquid deposited on the surface of the object being manufactured is not related to the vertical resolution of SLA process at all, and making thinner layers of fresh resin on the surface of the last layer manufactured does not allow to improve its vertical resolution. In fact, the vertical resolution of the SLA process is only related to the way the light penetrates in the photopolymeriz-
able medium: to make thinner layers, the light beam used to draw each layer on the surface of the resin should be confined to the surface and not penetrate too deeply in the medium. If this is not the case, and the light penetrates deeply in the medium, it will not only start the photopolymerization phenomenon and solidify the next layer of the object at the surface, but may also result in parasitic polymerizations in the already-polymerized layers of the object being built.

The first created microstereolithography machines were based on the scanning method. Two such machines were presented in scientific publications in 1993, one by Takagi and Nakajima [14] and another by Ikuta and Hirowatari [15]. Both machines are relatively similar: they use a scanning principle for fabricating the different layers of the objects and use a UV light beam focused on the surface of the resin through a transparent

window. The light beam is occulted by a shutter when a new layer is made or during positioning steps. The vector-by-vector fabrication of each layer is obtained using X-Y translation stages to move the whole photoreactor while focusing the light beam at a fixed location (Fig. 1.2.5A). These first two microstereolithography machines are described in more detail in a series of subsequent publications [16–18].
To obtain an improved lateral resolution compared to conventional SLA machines, these microstereolithography processes rely on a simplified optical system in which the light beam is fixed and tightly focused on one point. When moving the light beam with galvanometric mirrors, the focal point moves on a sphere, and dynamically focusing lenses are necessary to obtain a good focusing of the light on the flat surface of the resin. Removing the galvanometric mirrors and dynamic focusing optics results -
in a robust and simple focusing system, but X-Y translation stages are required to draw and solidify the shapes of each layer, making the process slow, especially if objects of high geometrical complexity are built.
The choice to constrain the surface of the liquid resin and perform the exposure through a transparent window has the advantage to avoid all problems related to spreading the fresh resin on the already polymerized part of the object being built and allows to create even layers of well-controlled thickness; however, it also has for drawbacks that the fabrication yields are very low because the adhesion of the structures being built to the window often causes their destruction [19]. Different variat-

To avoid the limitations related to polymerizing through a window, scanning microstereolithography processes where the polymerization takes place at the free surface of the photosensitive resin have been developed by many research teams. The first paper describing a scanning microstereolithography machine using a free surface technique was published by Zissi et al. in 1994 [20]. To create a layer of fresh resin on the surface of the object being built, the object was immersed in the resin tank and brought back close to the surface and time was spent waiting for gravity to level the surface. Even if this method used to spread fresh resin on the workpiece seems simple, the control of the thickness of the layer of liquid deposited is-

6 Microstereolithography processes having a submicron resolution
When manufacturing small components, it is possible to exploit nonlinear phenomena to build objects directly inside the resin. There are many obvious advantages in creating an object directly inside the resin rather than at its surface: there is no need to spread the next layer of fresh resin on the part being manufactured, which speeds up the fabrication process, there is no need to fabricate supporting structures nor to remove them once the object is built, assembled objects and feely movable co-
mponents can be made easily.
Various microstereolithography processes based on this principle were developed, based either on two-photon photopolymerization or on under-the-surface photopolymerization with a one-photon process. These microstereolithography machines can build very small components with a submicrometer resolution. The first machine based on the polymerization of the object inside the resin rather than at its surface was built to push further the limits in resolution of the microstereolithography processes. The resolution of scanning and projection microstereolithography machines is limited to a few microns in the three directions of space, but using the two-photon polymerization principle results in a significant improvement of resolution: the two-photon absorption requires that the combined energy of two p-

First, scanning processes were developed followed by projection techniques and finally submicron processes were invented. If only minor developments of the microstereolithography process have taken place in recent years, research has been done on the development of materials and applications. Similar to more conventional rapid prototyping methods, microstereolithography is first used for providing small polymer components for prototyping purposes, allowing to create rapidly devices and systems when conventional rapid prototyping technologies reach their limits in terms of size and resolution. Microstereolithography c-

This is not only useful for highresolution prototypes, but also for manufacturing small personalized components, such as hearing-aid parts fitted to the patient morphology.
The second main reason to use microstereolithography is the complexity of shapes that can be easily generated, leading to the fabrication of small and complex objects that would be very difficult to fabricate with any other manufacturing method. Being able to produce components that no other method can make is of course an asset as it allows to imagine new products and perform research on new concepts, but this aspect of microstereolithography is a double-edged sword, as such components cannot be -
mass produced by molding and if a few attempts have been made for fabricating multiple components simultaneously by microstereolithography, this technique is not yet suited for mass production. Consequently, these high-complexity high-resolution components are often successfully used to study new concepts in a research context, but rarely make it into products.
The research teams who developed microstereolithography machines demonstrated the possibilities of this technique by manufacturing components of high geometric complexity. 7.5 4D printing
4D printing is the process of fabricating through additive manufacturing methods, structures that can change of shape over time when a specific stimulus is applied. This change of shape can be linked to hinge-based folding structures, hydration of hydrogels, shapememory polymers, temperature-based deformations, and often require the fabrication of components using multiple materials of contrasting properties. Multijet modeling (also known as MultiJet printing or PolyJet technology) is often the pr-
eferred fabrication method for 4D printing as it is a multimaterial additive technology and allows mixing different resins to obtain the desired properties. It is also possible to use microstereolithography for the fabrication of 4D-printed components, with the advantage of its high resolution and using the large number of materials that can be shaped with this process. Different strategies have been imagined to obtain the 4D effect, either using single material solidification or multimaterial mic-
rostereolithography machines. - Various photopolymerizable resins demonstrating shape memory properties in a single
print were developed. Shape memory was demonstrated through fold-deploy tests with a thermal activation mechanism for various tailor-made resins [104–106]. - Temperature-responsive swelling hydrogel structures were fabricated by microstereolithography in a single print. By varying locally the applied fluence during fabrication, bilayer beams of different swelling characteristics were obtained. Their thermal actuation caused them to bend inwards to generate a gripping motion [107]. - Multimaterial -
microstereolithography machines were developed, allowing to exchange the photopolymerizable resin during the manufacturing process. Their use with different shape memory polymerizable resins allowed to perform complex actions, with different temperatures triggering sequentially the deformation of each individual polymer [106].

8 Conclusion
Microstereolithography is a technology that has seen its first implementations more than 20 years ago. It has been first developed in an academic context, with many research teams developing different concepts and apparatuses as well as formulating adapted resins dedicated to this technology. First, it has been used in a microsystems context, to fabricate

Microstereolithography

51

components that were not possible to be built with more conventional wafer-scale cleanroom technologies, giving an access to the third dimension to this field. However, it has only known a marginal success in the microsystems field due to the difficulty to integrate it with other microfabrication methods. Additionally, most complex objects made by microstereolithography have complex shapes and a 3D geometry and cannot be molded easily, making microstereolithography a manufacturing technology rathe-
r than a prototyping method.
Further developments of microstereolithography focused on the development of new materials dedicated to this technology. A variety of polymers and composite resins have been developed, as well as biocompatible and biodegradable materials. In recent years, only minor developments of the microstereolithography process itself have been performed, but many components that are extremely difficult to produce with other manufacturing techniques have been fabricated to investigate the fields of photonic c-
rystals and metamaterials, and there is a growing interest of the biomedical domain for this technology as it provides a rapid way to provide small components required for personalized medical devices, such as hearing aid components.
In the recent years, two new developments of additive manufacturing have emerged: 4D printing aims at adding functions that allow the fabricated components to evolve with time, resulting in components made by additive manufacturing that either change of shape or self-assemble themselves in given conditions. Bioprinting aims at printing living tissues and organs using additive manufacturing principles and is based on 3D printing biomaterials and cells. In both cases, the high resolution that can be-
 obtained with microstereolithography can be an asset to develop new applications.
Currently there are only a few microstereolithography machine providers on the market, and many machines are operated in an academic context. However, rapid prototyping technologies are currently gaining popularity as they are made more affordable and reach the general public. This will generate more ideas and new applications of rapid prototyping technologies that require high resolution and highly complex components may emerge, expanding the domain of application of microstereolithography.

Fundamentals of two-photon fabrication
John T. Fourkas
Institute for Physical Science and Technology, University of Maryland, College Park, MD, United States
1 Introduction The field of two-photon fabrication was born in 1997, with the seminal paper of Maruo et al. [1]. Since that time, the number of papers published on this topic each year has grown exponentially. More than 1500 papers on two-photon fabrication have appeared, and there are now more than 4500 citations to this topic annually.a This technology is used in a broad range of fields of science and engineering, and with the introduction of commercial instruments the range of applications is g-
rowing in leaps and bounds, as is made clear in later chapters in this volume. The reader is also recommended to the many excellent reviews that have been published previously on two-photon fabrication [2–18].

2 Nonlinear absorption
Typically, when light is absorbed by matter, the energy of a photon is resonant with an energy difference between an occupied electronic state and a higher electronic state (Fig. 1.3.1, top left). However, Go€ppert-Mayer predicted in 1930 in her doctoral thesis, and a year later in a publication [19], that it is also possible to absorb light when the energies of two photons collectively match the energy gap between electronic states in a material (Fig. 1.3.1, top right). In general, an n-photon absorption process can involve the addition of photons of n different frequencies. If the frequencies are different, the process is termed nondegenerate. However, the vast majority of multiphoton fabrication processes use photons of a single frequency. Furthermore, in fabrication schemes, degenerate multiphoton absorption typically involves only a single laser beam, which is the situation considered in the remainder of this chapter.
As shown in Fig. 1.3.1, in a typical two-photon absorption process, there is no state that is resonant with the individual photons. Rather, the first photon causes a transition to a virtual state, as is also the case in a Raman transition. The virtual state is composed of a combination of the far-off-resonance states of the atom or molecule. Because these states are far from resonance, the lifetime of the virtual state must be extremely short for the transition probability to be appreciable. Thus,-
 the second transition typically must occur within roughly 1 fs of the initial transition. In effect, for two-photon absorption to occur, both photons must be in the same place at the same time.
We can think of a two-photon transition as being a weighted sum of pairs of dipoleallowed transitions that take the atom or molecule from the initial state to the final state [33]. This picture has important consequences for the selection rules for two-photon absorption in atoms and in highly symmetric molecules, in which states that can be reached with single-photon transitions are two-photon forbidden and vice versa. For instance, the lowest state that is accessed efficiently in rhodamine 6G usi-
ng two-photon absorption [34] is higher in energy than the first excited singlet state [35] S1, and exhibits extremely weak linear absorption. Causing transitions to different excited states can have important ramifications for photochemical and photophysical processes. In the case of rhodamine 6G in methanol, for instance, the fluorescence quantum yield is 0.93 following linear absorption [36]. The fluorescence quantum yield is much lower following two-photon absorption at 800 nm [34]. Indeed, rh-
odamine 6G is an efficient generator of radicals upon two-photon excitation at 800 nm [37], even though it does not typically generate radicals following linear excitation to S1.
The nonlinear intensity dependence of multiphoton absorption is crucial for fabrication, in that this phenomenon allows absorption to be confined tightly in three dimensions if the excitation source is focused into a small volume. To see why this confinement occurs, we first consider linear absorption. The number of molecules addressed in a plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation of a beam of light is proportional to the area of the beam. The intensity in the same transverse plane, how-
ever, is inversely proportional to the area. Because the total population of excited molecules is proportional to the product of the intensity and the number of molecules, in the limit of weak absorption the same number of molecules is excited in each transverse plane of the beam (Fig. 1.3.1, bottom left). For two-photon absorption, the number of molecules excited in a transverse plane is proportional to the number of molecules (linear in area) multiplied by the square of the intensity (inversely -
proportional to area squared). Thus, the number

of excited molecules is inversely proportional to the area of the beam and, likewise, proportional to the transverse spatial intensity profile (Fig. 1.3.1, bottom right). Under appropriate intensity conditions, it is therefore possible to confine excitation to within the focal region of the beam.
Because two-photon absorption is mediated by a transition through a virtual state, this process is relatively inefficient. It is therefore extremely challenging to drive two-photon absorption with a classical, incoherent light source. Thus, the experimental demonstration of two-photon absorption was not achieved until after the laser had been invented [21]. Even then, two-photon absorption mediated by a virtual state (i.e., simultaneous rather than sequential two-photon absorption) was not amenabl-
e to practical applications in areas such as imaging and lithography, as the intensities required were so high that most samples would be damaged.
This situation changed when lasers with picosecond, and later with subpicosecond, pulse lengths became readily available. The peak intensities of ultrafast pulses can be quite high, but the duty cycle is typically on the order of 10À4 to 10À6 (or even smaller). Thus, high squared intensities can be achieved at low average powers, which help to prevent material damage. Two-photon absorption using ultrafast lasers first gained wide popularity as a means of performing 3D fluorescence microscopy [38].-
 The recognition that other photophysical and photochemical processes could also be localized using twophoton absorption spurred the rapid growth of the use of this phenomenon in 3D microfabrication and nanofabrication.
To create an object using two-photon fabrication, the laser focal point must be moved relative to the sample in a controlled, 3D manner. Many scanning methods exist, but they can be broken down into two rough categories: those that involve moving the sample relative to a laser focus that is fixed in space and those that involve moving a laser focus relative to a sample that is fixed in space. Each approach has its own advantages and disadvantages, and hybrids of both strategies can also be employe-
d. Strategies for scanning are discussed in more detail in Chapter 9. It is also possible to multiplex fabrication by using multiple beams simultaneously or by using masks [39–44]; see Chapter 4 for an in-depth discussion of the latter approach.

Having introduced the optical physics of multiphoton fabrication, we now turn to the materials aspects of this technology. The great majority of work in multiphoton fabrication has involved using light to render a material insoluble in the exposed region. A material whose solubility decreases upon exposure to light is known as a negative-tone photoresist [45, 46]. Negative-tone photoresists are used widely in photolithography, as well as in coatings and other technologies. Many of these materials -
can be adapted to multiphoton fabrication with relative ease.

We begin by considering radical photopolymerization, which is the most popular scheme for two-photon fabrication. Most traditional radical photoinitiators are aromatic ketones, with a prototypical example being benzophenone [47] (Fig. 1.3.2A). In Norrish Type I photoinitiators [48] (Fig. 1.3.2B), photoexcitation is followed by intersystem crossing, which creates a triplet diradical centered on the carbonyl group. This step is followed by homolytic bond cleavage to create two reactive free radicals-

A number of factors affect the efficiency with which two-photon excitation can initiate polymerization. The first factor is the two-photon absorption cross-section of the initiator. Larger absorption cross sections lead to more efficient initiation. In many typical applications, it is desirable to be able to use thick photoresist films, and so conventional radical photoinitiators usually have relatively small linear absorption cross sections so that light can propagate through the material without-
 significant attenuation. In the case of two-photon absorption, which occurs only in the focal region, absorption of the beam throughout the depth of the photoresist is not typically an issue. Thus, the design of new photoinitiators with large two-photon-absorption cross sections has been an effective strategy for creating materials in which two-photon polymerization can be driven at a low laser intensity [8, 49, 50].
The second critical facet of a photoinitiator is its quantum efficiency for the creation of radicals [51]. Ideally, every excitation event will lead to the production of radicals, but this criterion is not always met. Development of initiators with high two-photon absorption cross-sections, for instance, often requires a non-Norrish mechanism (e.g., electron transfer) to generate radicals. In such cases, the quantum efficiency of radical production can decrease substantially.
The third important aspect of a photoinitiator is the initiation velocity, which in effect is the degree of reactivity of the initial radicals that are created. Traditional radical photoinitiators have high initiation velocities [51]. Initiation velocity is typically a property that is sacrificed to some extent in creating molecules with large two-photon absorption cross sections. However, even if the quantum yield and initiation velocity were to each decrease by an order of magnitude, good molecu-
lar designs increase the two-photon absorption cross section by many orders of magnitude. Thus, increasing the two-photon absorption cross section is usually the preferred route to reducing the laser power required for fabrication.
The monomers that are most commonly used for two-photon radical polymerization are either acrylates (Fig. 1.3.2D) or methacrylates (Fig. 1.3.2E). Both types of monomer are relatively stable on the shelf (particularly if a radical quencher is added), but react rapidly in the presence of a high enough concentration of radicals. Acrylates are used more commonly than are methacrylates, as the former molecules are more reactive and thus typically can undergo two-photon polymerization at lower laser int-
ensities.
The monomers used for two-photon polymerization are typically polyfunctional, so that they become highly crosslinked upon reaction. This process converts a photoresist, which in two-photon polymerization is often a viscous liquid, into a hard, insoluble polymer with a high glass-transition temperature. If a liquid photoresist is not desirable, fillers can be added to turn the photoresist into a gel that will not move during fabrication [52]. Many different types of acrylic and methacrylic monomers-
 are available, so that properties such as solvent compatibility, hardness, shrinkage, adhesion, and even biocompatibility can be adjusted to the requirements of the end use.

If propagation were to proceed unchecked, exposure in one small region of a photoresist could cause the entire material to become crosslinked. However, two processes tend to halt propagation unless new radicals are being created continuously: (1) quenching occurs when a propagating radical encounters a molecule such as oxygen or a radical inhibitor su-

Termination and quenching, in combination with the intensity and exposure time, determine the size of the volume element (voxel) created when exposing a single position in the photoresist with the laser beam. Typically these effects are strong enough that crosslinking does not proceed far beyond the region in which the laser is focused in a two-photon photoresist [54]. 4 Direct fabrication in other materials
The ability to create 3D, polymeric structures with sub-100-nm feature sizes is an enabling technology for a broad variety of applications. However, there are many applications for which it is essential to be able to employ materials other than polymers. For instance, metals are desirable for many optical and electronic applications, biomolecules are desirable for many biological applications, and inorganic materials are desirable for many mechanical and optical applications. In this section, we g-
ive an overview of some of the different strategies that have been employed for two-photon-based fabrication incorporating materials beyond polymers.
In a negative-tone photoresist, the vast majority of the exposed material is converted to polymer. There are few other types of materials that meet this criterion. One notable example of such a material is the chalcogenide glass As4S6 [79, 80]. Upon two-photon exposure to light in the near infrared, this material is converted into As2S3. Subsequent wet-chemical etching can be used to remove the As4S6 glass while leaving the As2S3.
Another approach that can be used is to create a hybrid organic/inorganic material in which the organic portion acts as the photoresist. For instance, silanes with acrylate groups can be used to create photoresists that have mechanical and optical properties that are not readily accessible with acrylates alone [10]. The same type of strategy can be extended to other inorganic oxides as well [13, 81, 82].
Once deposited, photoresists are typically neat liquids or gels, and the percentage conversion in areas that have been exposed is quite high. The two-photon deposition of materials other than polymers usually occurs from a liquid solution or a solid solution of the material of interest. In this case, the conversion of the solution into the deposited material is generally much less than 100%. This situation introduces additional complexity into 3D fabrication. Because diffusion is required to repla-
ce precursor material that has reacted, the dwell time required for fabricating a voxel from a liquid is typically substantially greater than that for a photoresist, even when the two-photon chemistry in the liquid is highly efficient. Heating, convection, and bubbling of the solution can be detrimental to the fabrication of smooth structures. In the case of solid solutions, removal of the solid may cause the deposited 3D structure to collapse.
We consider two examples of materials that can be deposited from liquid or solid solutions: proteins and metals. Proteins were one of the first materials other than polymers to

Some of these problems can be addressed by depositing metals from solid matrices [90, 94–96]. This strategy has proven to work well for creating high-quality metal patterns on the surfaces of 3D structures. Fabrication of 3D metal structures within a polymeric matrix has also been demonstrated, although such structures can potentially collapse if the surrounding matrix is removed [90].

1 Introduction Free-radical photopolymerization is a widely used methodology of preparation of polymeric materials, especially where its unique features (spatial resolution, precise control of the process) are particularly important. This polymerization mechanism limits the range of the applicable monomers to those with olefinic double bonds. The industry is dominated by acrylate family (i.e., acrylates and methacrylates, structures I and II). A large variety of monoacrylates and multi(meth)acryla-
tes of low- and high-molecular weight is present on the market today. The properties of polyacrylates and polymethacrylates are different: in general, polymethacrylates are stiffer with higher glass temperatures, higher tensile strength, and low elongation, because the methyl group restricts the rotational mobility of the polymer chain (causes steric hindrance). Polyacrylates are usually soft and rubbery and display low tensile strength and high elongation.
The vinyl bonds on (meth)acrylate monomers react readily in the presence of radicals, and it is this fast rate of radical polymerization that makes (meth)acrylates attractive as compared to ionically polymerized monomers such as epoxides.

3 Effect of diffusional processes on propagation and termination steps
The elementary reaction steps in the free-radical linear (of monomers containing one double bond) and crosslinking polymerization (of multiunsaturated monomers) are the same and involve initiation, propagation, chain transfer, and termination, as described earlier. However, kinetics of network formation show also special features arising from the monomer multifunctionality.
3.1 Linear systems
The factor that determines the kinetic behavior is diffusion. The bimolecular termination is a diffusion-controlled process from the beginning of the polymerization. Diffusion control means that the observed termination rate coefficient ktb in the reaction (6), Scheme 2.1.1 and Eq. (2.1.2) is being dominated by the time constant of diffusion to form radical-radical encounter pairs (kdiff) rather than by the time constant of the chemical reaction itself (kchem) (diffusion-controlled reactions are g-
enerally characterized by kdiff ≪ kchem) [7]. Thus, the termination rate coefficient is a chain-length-dependent process since length of the macroradicals changes during the reaction, which results in differences in their mobility. For this reason, the ktb denotes an average value. Diffusion control means also that ktb is sensitive to various factors that affect the viscosity of the reaction medium, like conversion degree, the molecular weight, presence of a solvent or dissolved polymer, etc. [8].
One of the consequences of the diffusional limitations on the bimolecular termination is the gel effect (the gel effect occurs under isothermal conditions, whereas autoacceleration refers to nonisothermal conditions [9]; however, these terms are often not distinguished). In the linear polymerization, at the beginning of the reaction, the concentration of free radicals in the polymerizing medium is practically constant (so-called steady-state [6]) and the polymerization rate slowly decreases with c-
