BondBERT: What we learn when assigning sentiment in the bond market
Toby Barter1, Zheng Gao1, Eva Christodoulaki1 a, Jing Chen2 b and John Cartlidge1 c 1School of Engineering Mathematics and Technology, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK
2School of Mathematics, Cardiff University, Cardiff, UK {wu22796, ww24988, Eva.Christodoulaki}@bristol.ac.uk, ChenJ60@cardiff.ac.uk, John.Cartlidge@bristol.ac.uk
Keywords: BondBERT, Natural Language Processing, Bond Market, Financial Sentiment, Predictive Modelling
Abstract: Bond markets respond differently to macroeconomic news compared to equity markets, yet most sentiment models, including FinBERT, are trained primarily on general financial or equity news data. This mismatch is important because bond prices often move in the opposite direction to economic optimism, making general or equity-based sentiment tools potentially misleading. In this paper, we introduce BondBERT, a transformerbased language model fine-tuned on bond-specific news. BondBERT can act-
 as the perception and reasoning component of a financial decision-support agent, providing sentiment signals that integrate with forecasting models. It is a generalisable framework for adapting transformers to low-volatility, domain-inverse sentiment tasks by compiling and cleaning 30,000 UK bond market articles (2018–2025) for training, validation, and testing. We compare BondBERT’s sentiment predictions against FinBERT, FinGPT, and Instruct-FinGPT using event-based correlation, up/down accuracy-
 analyses, and LSTM forecasting across ten UK sovereign bonds. We find that BondBERT consistently produces positive correlations with bond returns, achieves higher alignment and forecasting accuracy than the three baseline models, with lower normalised RMSE and higher information coefficient. These results demonstrate that domain-specific sentiment adaptation better captures fixed income dynamics, bridging a gap between NLP advances and bond market analytics.
1 INTRODUCTION
Bond markets are fundamental to global finance, yet they remain comparatively underexplored in artificial intelligence research. Sentiment analysis applies techniques to quantify the tone or emotional score of textual data, converting qualitative news, reports, or social-media posts into numerical scores. By providing a quantitative measure of market mood, sentiment analysis has become an important tool for modelling and predicting trends across financial markets (Souza et al., 2015; Luna et al., -
2015; Pagolu et al., 2016; Shabani et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020). This approach has been shown to be particularly insightful in fixed-income analysis, where a growing body of research demonstrates a clear link between sentiment and the behaviour of bond markets (Pi ̃neiro-Chousa et al., 2021; Bartov et al., 2023; Erlwein-Sayer, 2018; Bakar and Hamzah, 2022). However, there are no tools specifically introduced for the fixed income market. This gap is critical

2 Literature review
Early approaches to financial sentiment analysis relied on lexicon-based methods (e.g., LoughranMcDonald), which count domain-specific positive and negative words to generate sentiment scores. Although easy to implement, these methods often fail to capture context, negation, and subtle linguistic signals (Du et al., 2024). Machine learning classifiers such as SVMs and logistic regression improved upon this by learning feature weights from labelled financial text, sometimes using lexicon outputs as-
 inputs. They are generally more accurate than pure lexicons, but these models still depend on manual annotation and often struggle with complex phrasing (Peng et al., 2024). On the other hand, deep learning models such as RNNs, LSTMs, and CNNs have further advanced the field by automatically learning latent representations that encode word order and context, yielding higher accuracy, but requiring larger datasets and high compute (Du et al., 2024). More recently, transformer-based models such as -
FinBERT have emerged as a benchmark for financial sentiment analysis. Introduced by Araci (2019),

Comparative studies consistently show FinBERT outperforms both the base BERT model and traditional machine learning approaches, largely due to its contextual embeddings and financial domain adaptation (Dataloop, 2025). Araci’s original work demonstrated significant accuracy gains even with limited fine-tuning data (-

While FinBERT has been widely applied to equity market forecasting (Zhang, 2025; Gu et al., 2024; Kulkarni et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024; Sreehitha et al., 2024; Rajesh et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025), its use in bond markets has been far more limited,-
 with only a handful of studies exploring this application (Makara, 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Talebi et al., 2025; Sontakke and Patil, 2024). Importantly, no variant of FinBERT has yet been trained or adapted specifically for bond-related text. In this work, we address this gap by employing the FinBERT model introduced by Yang et al. (2020) model and further adapting it for the analysis of bond market sentiment, as we will explain in more detail in Section 4. The development of FinGPT represents one-
 of the first systematic efforts to create an open-source financial large language model. The framework was introduced as a pipeline that automates large-scale financial data collection, with lightweight adaptation methods such as LoRA/QLoRA, and proposes reinforcement learning with stock prices for alignment (Liu et al., 2023). Its architecture was described in four layers (data, adaptation, evaluation, application), providing a foundation for customisable financial AI research (Yang et al., 2023-
). Building on the above, an instruction-tuning benchmark for FinGPT was proposed, showing that relatively small models (7 billion parameters) fine-tuned with finance-specific instructions can match or even outperform larger untuned models (Wang et al., 2023). This highlighted the need for instruction-tuned financial LLMs for sentiment analysis. Thus, we start seeing studies using FinGPT for predictive modelling. Liang et al. (2024) developed a dissemination-aware FinGPT for stock prediction, wher-

Another popular tool, Instruct-FinGPT, explores instruction tuning as a method to adapt generalpurpose LLMs in finance. This is achieved by converting small supervised financial sentiment datasets into instruction-style data and fine-tuning LLaMA7B, as shown in Zhang et al. (2023). Our work connects to research on decisionsupport systems, since BondBERT can be viewed as a perceptual agent module embedded within larger multi-agent financial architectures, where different agents handle forecasting, risk assessment, and trading.

4 Methodology
To address the absence of a bond-specific sentiment tool, we developed BondBERT by further adapting a financial domain language model for the fixedincome context. Specifically, we start from the FinBERT model introduced by Yang et al. (2020), which is domain pretrained on a large scale financial text (e.g., SEC filings and Reuters news) but not finetuned on labeled sentiment datasets. We fine-tuned this model on our bond-specific sentiment dataset to create BondBERT. For comparison, we use ProsusA-
I/FinBERT (ProsusAI, 2020) as a baseline, which is widely used in prior work and is fine-tuned on the Financial PhraseBank dataset (Malo et al., 2014) (general financial news sentiment) but not specialised for bonds. The training corpus for BondBERT comprised approximately 17,000 bond-related news articles spanning 2018–2021, internationally, covering macroeco
nomic, fiscal, and policy domains relevant to bond markets.4 These articles were first cleaned for duplicates, irrelevant topics, and insufficient content length, then aligned to market dates. Sentiment labels were generated by employing GPT-4.1-nano via the OpenAI API and evaluating different prompts designed to capture the expected impact of each article on bond prices.5 We used GPT-4.1-nano to generate sentiment labels efficiently, testing prompt formats until we identified one that produced co-
nsistent and financially relevant outputs. This was critical because general

The best performing prompt was concise and framed the model as a bond market analyst. This framing improved label consistency, ensuring that positive scores reflected bond price increases rather than general economic optimism. Finally, we asked the model to assign a sentiment score between −1 (strongly negative for bond prices) and +1 (strongly positive), with 0 indicating neutral. The final prompt, which was deliberately kept concise for better accuracy, was:
“You are a financial analyst. Return only one float between -1.00 and +1.00, indicating the immediate impact on the bond market prices. Negative means prices will fall; Positive means prices will rise.”
Using this prompt, we processed 17,000 articles for training, almost 2,000 for validation, and 2,000 for testing, in order to generate continuous sentiment scores in the range [−1, 1]. These scores served as the training labels for BondBERT’s supervised finetuning stage. These sentiment scores were merged with the article text using titles as unique keys to ensure accurate label alignment. Finally, it is worth clarifying that because both FinGPT and Instruct-FinGPT are not stand-alone architectures, we had to use LLaMA-2 as their underlying backbone. These models were released as adaptations of the LLaMA-2 family (7B and 13B), with LoRA adapters provided for FinGPT and instructiontuned checkpoints for Instruct-FinGPT specifically designed for this backbone. Although newer opensource backbones such as LLaMA-3 and LLaMA-4 are released, the FinGPT resources currently available are not -
compatible with them. Since our goal was to compare BondBERT against other financial models, we included FinGPT and Instruct-FinGPT in the evaluation despite their dependence on LLaMA-2. One drawback, however, of using GPT-based annotation is the potential risk of peek-ahead bias. Since GPT models are trained on large post-event corpora, they may inadvertently leverage knowledge of subsequent market outcomes when labelling historical articles. 5 Results
For evaluation, we adopted an event-based methodology to isolate impactful news. Sentiment shocks were defined as daily sentiment values in the top or bottom 10th percentile. When multiple shocks occurred on the same day, their scores were averaged. We chose to evaluate the two sentiment tools using article topics related to the UK bond market, such as “UK inflation”, “UK bond market”, and “UK economic outlook”, as shown in Figure 3. The figures present the Pearson correlations between sentiment s-
cores and UK bond returns across six economic topics, using a 10% daily sentiment shock, meaning using 10% of most negative and 10% of the most positive scores and a 7-day rolling window. As we see, neither FinBERT nor BondBERT in Figures 3a and 3b achieved consistent high correlations amongst all topics and bond instruments. However, we do observe clear directional differences. FinBERT correlations are predominantly negative or near zero (i.e., between -0.11 and 0.05), consistent with the more ge-
neral financial data on which it is trained, where positive macroeconomic news can imply falling bond prices. In contrast, BondBERT correlations are consistently positive across topics, with higher values, although it does show negative correlations for some instrument and topic combinations, similar to FinBERT. Moreover, FinGPT correlations in Figure 3c are mixed, with values clustering around zero and only limited alignment with bond price movements. This indicates that while FinGPT can capture -
some directional sentiment, its generalpurpose training in finance limits performance on bond-specific topics. For Instruct-FinGPT in Figure 3d, we observe that its correlations with bond returns are more stable and positive than those of FinGPT, and closer to the behaviour of BondBERT, so instruction tuning does seem to improve alignment with bond price movements. BondBERT, however, still achieves the strongest and most consistent correlations across topics.

6 Conclusions and Future work
This study introduces BondBERT, a bond-specific sentiment analysis model, and demonstrates its advantage over three other sentiment tools in aligning sentiment with bond price movements. We showed the importance of a transferable strategy for adapting LLMs to markets where sentiment dynamics diverge from equity assumptions, easily reproducible, and generalisable to other non-equity classes, such as commodities, derivatives, and real estate. Our results show that BondBERT reverses the wrong-sign bi-
as observed in equity-trained models and delivers more consistent forecasting improvements. The analysis is restricted to UK sovereign bonds, since the illiquidity of corporate bonds prevents a meaningful evaluation in that segment, and the current model is trained on synthetic sentiment labels generated by GPT-4.1-nano. While this approach is cost-effective and scalable, it may not fully capture domain expertise, and thus constrains the fidelity of the training data. Thus, we plan to introduce hu-
man annotation and semi-supervised refinement to improve the quality of sentiment labels beyond synthetic generation. Moreover, we aim to expand the dataset in both temporal and market scope, incorporating international sovereign and corporate bonds, with higher liquidity. These extensions will enable testing the generalisability of BondBERT and strengthen its potential applications in trading, risk management, and policy. While proprietary bond price data restricts direct replication, we aim to r-
elease our labelling pipeline,
prompts, and model to enable reproduction of core experiments. Beyond finance, we also highlight a broader AI challenge, including learning under weak effect sizes and noisy supervision, where sentiment often diverges from intuitive human interpretations. While BondBERT is not a standalone agent, it demonstrates how specialised perception modules can be embedded into intelligent decision-support agents for fixed income markets. Such modules could interact with forecasting and trading agents as par-
t of multiagent systems, extending the role of NLP in financial AI. In the longer term, the goal is to incorporate predictive models into an agentic-AI framework to provide bespoke investment advice to individual investors via a mobile application. For more details, see the artificial intelligence for collective intelligence (AI4CI) vision, described in Bullock et al. (2024).
