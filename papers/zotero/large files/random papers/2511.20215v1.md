Implications of the Four-Color Theorem on the Dynamics of N-Component Phase Separation
Michael Rennick,1 Xitong Zhang,1 and Halim Kusumaatmaja1, ∗
1School of Engineering, U niversity of Edinburgh, Edinburgh EH9 3JL, U nited Kingdom (Dated: November 26, 2025)
We study phase separation dynamics of N > 2 immiscible fluid components. In two-dimensions, as a consequence of four-color theorem in mathematics, there is a change in topological constraints for N = 3 and N ≥ 4 components. This leads to a suppression of hydrodynamics effects and we show that the diffusion-dominated coarsening dynamics for N ≥ 4 can be collapsed to a single master curve. The behaviour extends to thin three-dimensional systems as the fluid domain size reaches the smallest system di-
mension. However, by varying the fluid interfacial tensions, cloaking may occur, resulting in highly complex coarsening dynamics that can differ for each component. In full three-dimensional cases, the absence of four-color theorem (or its equivalent) means that the diffusive scaling law is only reached asymptotically for large N rather than at a certain critical N .
Spontaneous phase separation of fluid mixtures is important for a wide range of scientific disciplines, from the stability of formulated products [1, 2] and the design of solar cell active layers [3–5] to volcanic eruptions [6, 7] and structural colors in living creatures [810]. Extensive works on fluid phase separation both in two and three-dimensions have largely focused on two immiscible components, and it is well understood that there can be several different coarsening dynamics, including dif-
fusive, viscous hydrodynamics, and inertial hydrodynamics [11–14]. The importance of solid walls, including preferential wetting of one of the components, has also been examined [15, 16].
More recently there is a surge of interest in understanding fluid phase separation for three, four, and more generally N fluid components. For example, liquid-liquid phase separation in living cells leading to the formation of biomolecular condensates involves hundreds of different proteins and nucleic acids [1721]. The number of distinct immiscible components that spontaneously separate then depends on the interactions between the biomolecules [22–24]. Synthetically, recent advances in DNA self-a-
ssembly have allowed fine-tuning of biomolecular interactions leading to complex mesostructures, including the possibility to realize many immiscible fluid components [25–27].
To date, there has been some progress in understanding the thermodynamics of spontaneous phase separation of N fluid components [28–30], and the possible structures formed based on their interfacial tensions [31, 32]. However, their coarsening dynamics remains poorly understood, in part due to the lack of suitable simulation methods. Here, we address this gap by investigating the implications of the four-color theorem in mathematics [33, 34] in two-dimensions, and its absence in three-dimensions, -
to phase separation dynamics.
Employing lattice Boltzmann method that incorporates full hydrodynamics coupling, we investigate phase separation in two and three-dimensions with up to N = 8 components. Due to four-color theorem, in two-dimensions and in the absence of cloaking, we show that topological constraints for N ≥ 4 lead to strong suppression of coalescence hydrody
namics. In this case, we find a master curve to explain the diffusion-dominated coarsening dynamics. The suppresion of hydrodynamics is applicable to thin three-dimensional systems once the typical domain size reaches the system thickness, but is only valid asymptotically in three-dimensions as N → ∞. For the parameters chosen here, we clearly observe a scaling law of L∗ ∝ (t∗)2/3, as shown in Fig. 1(b). For N = 3, we find a foam-like structure in Fig. 1(a). As illustrated in Supplemental Movie 1, here, coarsening typically occurs via a combination of diffusive Ostwald ripening, in which larger domains grow at the expense of smaller ones, followed by hydrodynamic coalescence after a small domain disappears. Initially, both timescales are in competition, and the exponent is intermediate between -
2/3 and 1/3 (Fig. 1(b)). With increasing domain size, the time delay between Ostwald ripening events becomes the bottleneck to coarsening and hence we observe a full crossover to L∗ ∝ (t∗)1/3 diffusive scaling. In contrast, for N ≥ 4, the scaling law is dominated by diffusive scaling across the whole time domain, as shown in Fig. 1(b). As illustrated in Supplemental Movie 2, for N ≥ 4, a domain shrink is not necessarily followed by domain coalescence. This is corroborated by direct measurements (s-
ee Supplemental Material [42] Section III for our measurement methodology) of the coalescence probability for different N , as shown in Fig. 1(c). Coalescence events are significantly more probable for N = 3 than for N ≥ 4.
To explain this, we observe that the arrangements of fluid domains for N = 3 and N ≥ 4 are fundamentally distinct. Only planar maps in which every domain has an even number of neighbours are three-colorable [46], typically four, six or eight neighbours per domain for this work. As illustrated in Fig. 1(d), if there is an odd number of neighbours with N = 3, there will always be two domains with the same fluid component and coalescence will proceed. In contrast, for N ≥ 4, it is possible to have bo-
th even and odd numbers of neighbours. Due to the topological constraints imposed in a two-dimensional system, the four-color theorem allows fluid domains with at least four components to be arranged to avoid coalescence. This flexibility for N ≥ 4 allows the phase structure to avoid coalescence following Ostwald ripening by exploiting a change in the neighbouring phase topology, with a local switch from an odd to even structure and
vice-versa. In contrast, for N = 3 coalescence must always occur to find a stable even-neighbor configuration. Beyond four, additional fluid components do not fundamentally alter the possible fluid domain configurations, which explains the collapse in behaviour for N ≥ 4.

In three dimensions, there is no analogue for the four-color theorem and there always exists a possible configuration where fluid components can coalesce by extending into the third dimension. Consequently, we no longer observe a strict upper bound in the number of concurrent phases needed to arrest hydrodynamic coarsening (Fig. 2(a)). Systems with N = 5 and N = 6 follow viscous hydrodynamics with L∗ ∝ t∗ [12], with a gradual transition toward diffusive L∗ ∝ (t∗)1/3 scaling as the number of compon-
ents is increased further. While additional components do not impose new topological constraints, the probability of like phases coming into contact decreases asymptotically with increasing N , suppressing coalescence and increasing the role of diffusion in the coarsening process. We can emulate the topological constraints of a twodimensional system by confining the phase separation process to a thin film (Fig. 2(b-d)). For simplicity, here we calculate L in Eq. 8 using the circularly aver
aged structure factor of a single 67.5√N × 67.5√N slice through the domain. For the thin film case, coarsening initially follows viscous hydrodynamics until the average fluid domain size is larger than the film thickness, following which diffusion becomes the dominant effect and the curves collapse to the same L∗ = aN (t∗)1/3 line as the two-dimensional case (Fig. 2(b), additional results for other film thicknesses are included in Fig. S3 [42]). This transition occurs due to the initially complex -
three-dimensional phase morphology eventually becoming uniform in the confining dimension when the domains outgrow the film thickness (Fig. 2(c,d)). We next ask whether the four-color theorem is a necessary or completely sufficient condition to suppress hydrodynamic coarsening in two dimensions. We explore this by varying the fluid interfacial tensions and volume fractions. The detailed results are shown in Section VI of the Supplemental Material [42]. Importantly, we discover that the suppression-
 of hydrodynamic coarsening is consistent following the formation of interfacial triple points in two dimensions (or lines in three dimensional thin films) that stabilize the fluid domains. It is possible to configure the fluid interfacial tensions such that certain triple point combinations are no longer energetically favourable, if one or more spreading parameters Sij;k = σij − σik − σjk are positive. In Fig. 3(a), we show simulation results for N = 6 comparing non-cloaking and cloaking cases. T-
he surface tension and volume fraction configurations are given in Table S2 [42]. As before, the noncloaking case is dominated by diffusive coarsening dynamics. For the cloaking case, the interfacial tensions are such that the pink and purple fluids cloak the other fluid domains. These fluids are able to redistribute throughout the domain, and the entire mixture now coarsens hydrodynamically with L∗ ∝ (t∗)2/3 as highlighted in Fig. 3(b). By exploring the full phase space of spreading parameters, a wide variety of distinct coarsening dynamics and phase structures are possible. In summary, we have shown that the four-color theorem -
can impose strong topological constraints on the phase separation of N component mixtures in twodimensions. In such cases, the evolution of the characteristic length for N ≥ 4 follows a universal master curve governed by diffusion. However, the fourcolor theorem only provides a necessary but not completely sufficient condition for total hydrodynamic arrest. This also relies on energetically permissible triple points for long-term stability against hydrodynamics.

There is no equivalent topological restriction in 3D, but we recover effective two-dimensional behaviour by confining the system to thin films, with a crossover from hydrodynamic to diffusive coarsening once the average domain size exceeds the film thickness.
Our results raise new perspectives on how topological constraints can affect phase separation dynamics of many fluid components. Such systems may include liquid-liquid phase separation in biological cells and synthetic materials, such as block copolymers [47–49] and DNA nanostars [26], where the interactions between the building blocks can be systematically tuned. Future work should explore how surface wetting [50] affects possible phase topologies, as well as how complex fluid properties, includi-
ng viscoelasticity [51, 52], influence coarsening dynamics for many component mixtures.
