Abstract
Modern artificial intelligence systems make critical decisions yet often fail silently when uncertain. We develop a geometric framework for post-hoc calibration of neural network probability outputs, treating probability vectors as points on the (c − 1)-dimensional probability simplex equipped with the Fisher–Rao metric. Our approach yields Additive Log-Ratio (ALR) calibration maps that reduce exactly to Platt scaling for binary problems (Proposition 1) while extending naturally to multi-class set-
tings—providing a principled generalization that existing methods lack. Complementing calibration, we define geometric reliability scores based on Fisher–Rao distance and construct neutral zones for principled deferral of uncertain predictions. Theoretical contributions include: (i) consistency of the calibration estimator at rate Op(n−1/2) via M-estimation theory (Theorem 1), and (ii) tight concentration bounds for reliability scores with explicit sub-Gaussian parameters enabling sample size calc-
ulations for validation set design (Theorem 2). We conjecture Neyman–Pearson optimality of our neutral zone construction based on connections to Bhattacharyya coefficients. Empirical validation on Adeno-Associated Virus classification demonstrates that the two-stage framework (calibration followed by reliabilitybased deferral) captures 72.5% of errors while deferring 34.5% of samples. Notably, this operational gain is achievable with any well-calibrated probability output; the contribution of geom-
etric calibration lies in its theoretical foundations rather than empirical superiority over simpler alternatives. This work bridges information geometry and statistical learning, offering formal guarantees relevant to applications requiring rigorous validation.
Keywords: Calibration, uncertainty quantification, Fisher-Rao geometry, manifold geometry, probability simplex, geometric reliability scores, neural networks, neutral zones

1 Introduction
Modern AI systems make critical decisions in medicine, criminal justice, and autonomous systems, yet they provide no reliable mechanism to identify when predictions are likely wrong. A medical AI diagnosing cancer from imaging may output p = (0.51, 0.49) for malignant vs. benign with the same apparent confidence as p = (0.99, 0.01), despite the former representing profound uncertainty. This forced classification without uncertainty quantification leads to silent failures—the system appears to func-
tion normally while making unreliable predictions with potentially severe consequences.

This manifests as systematic overconfidence, where networks assign high probabilities to misclassified samples [2], and complete absence of uncertainty awareness [3].
1.1 Motivation: High-Stakes Applications
The deployment of AI in high-stakes domains reveals a critical gap: while these systems achieve impressive accuracy, they fail catastrophically when uncertain. Three domains illustrate the urgency: Medical imaging: CNN models classify CT scans for lung cancer detection [4, 5] and FMRI scans for brain disorders. These methods lack precision measures for individual predictions, yet clinical decisions require knowing when to consult human experts. Gene therapy: Adeno-Associated Viruses (AAVs) are cri-
tical vectors where misclassification of cargo types (Empty, ssDNA, dsDNA) can trigger immune responses in patients. Our preliminary work shows 16.45% error rates without uncertainty quantification. Social decisions: AI increasingly informs decisions about individuals in employment, education, and criminal justice [6, 7]. When humans are the decision-making unit, misclassification costs are socially significant and often irreversible. In each domain, the cost of misclassification vastly exceeds th-
e cost of deferring uncertain cases to human review. Current methods provide no principled mechanism for this deferral.
1.2 Existing Approaches and Limitations
Existing post-hoc calibration methods—temperature scaling [1] and Platt scaling [8]—apply univariate transformations that treat probability vectors as unconstrained Euclidean points, ignoring the simplex constraint P
i pi = 1. These methods achieve good calibration on average but provide no instance-level uncertainty quantification. Uncertainty quantification approaches such as conformal prediction [9] provide coverage guarantees but produce set-valued predictions that are difficult to interpret. Bayesian neural networks [10] and deep ensembles [11] require significant computational overhead, making them impractical for many real-time applications. Neutral zone methods have shown promise in binary classificati-
on. The r-power framework [12] identifies ambiguous predictions through hypothesis testing but extension to multi-class problems requires new theoretical machinery, as the binary hypothesis testing framework does not generalize directly.

4 Empirical Validation
4.1 Experimental Setup
4.1.1 Application Domain
Adeno-Associated Viruses (AAVs) are critical vectors for gene therapy, delivering therapeutic genetic material to target cells. Misclassification of AAV cargo types—Empty (no genetic payload), single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), or double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)—can trigger immune responses in patients or result in manufacturing losses exceeding $500,000 when contaminated batches require destruction. Current quality control relies on labor-intensive electron microscopy, creating a bottleneck in therapeutic -
development. We applied our geometric calibration framework to a convolutional neural network trained on electrical signals from solid-state nanopore measurements [21, 22]. Previous work using rpower neutral zones on binary AAV classification achieved approximately 50% error capture while deferring 20% of samples [12]. Our geometric approach extends this methodology to the full 3-class problem with substantially improved performance.

Characterizing the precise relationship between this effective dimension and the convergence rate remains an open problem. Additionally, the softmax Hessian structure (Lemma 2) contributes favorable curvature in the directions most relevant to classification, while regularization ensures uniform strong convexity across the full parameter space. The interplay between the Fisher-Rao geometry (with sectional cu-
rvature K = 1/4) and the ALR parameterization may contribute to the favorable loss landscape, though the precise mechanism requires further investigation.

5 Open Problems and Future Directions
This section outlines research directions extending the geometric calibration framework. We organize by theme, progressing from theoretical extensions to practical questions and connections with related methods.
5.1 Curvature-Based Error Bounds
The positive curvature K = +1/4 of the probability simplex suggests tighter error propagation than Euclidean space. Specifically, geodesic triangles on positively curved spaces have angle sums exceeding π, and comparison theorems (Toponogov, Rauch) bound distance distortions. We conjecture:
dFR(p, q) ≤ 2 tanh−1 ∥ ALR(p) − ALR(q)∥2
C (18)
for some constant C > 0, with the sublinear tanh−1 growth suggesting tighter bounds than linear Euclidean propagation. The intuition is that the ALR transformation “stretches” distances near the simplex boundary, while the spherical geometry “compresses” them, potentially yielding favorable cancellation.
5.2 Distribution Shift Robustness
Under covariate shift Q ≪ P with bounded likelihood ratio dQ/dP ≤ M , we aim to prove:
|EQ[dFR] − EP [dFR]| ≤ p(M − 1)2 + 2M · DKL(Q∥P ) · π (19)
using the bounded diameter diam(∆c−1) = π. This would provide guarantees for calibration transfer across related domains (e.g., training on one imaging modality, deploying on another).

6 Conclusion
We have developed a geometric framework for uncertainty-aware classification that bridges information geometry and statistical learning theory. The framework comprises two stages: geometric calibration of neural network probability outputs via ALR transformations, followed by reliability scoring and neutral zone construction for principled deferral of uncertain predictions.
22

Main contributions. Our central theoretical result is Proposition 1, which establishes geometric calibration as the natural multi-class generalization of Platt scaling—a widely-used binary method that previously lacked principled extension to c > 2 classes. This fills a gap in the calibration literature by grounding multi-class calibration in the intrinsic geometry of the probability simplex. Supporting this framework, Theorem 1 provides consistency guarantees at rate Op(n−1/2) via M-estimation t-
heory, while Theorem 2 yields concentration bounds for reliability scores with explicit sub-Gaussian parameters. These bounds enable practitioners to determine required validation set sizes before data collection—a practical benefit for applications where labeled validation data is expensive.
Empirical findings. Validation on AAV classification demonstrates that the two-stage framework achieves substantial error reduction: 72.5% error capture at 34.5% deferral rate, reducing automated decision errors from 16.8% to 6.9%. Importantly, our comparison with alternative calibration methods (Table 5) reveals that these operational gains arise primarily from the neutral zone mechanism itself, which is applicable to any well-calibrated probability output. Geometric calibration achieves competit-
ive empirical performance while providing formal guarantees that simpler methods lack.
Limitations. Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, empirical validation is confined to a single application domain with c = 3 classes; the framework’s behavior in high-dimensional settings (c ≫ 3) and under distribution shift remains to be demonstrated. Second, the evidence for faster-than-n−1/2 convergence (Remark 11) is suggestive but inconclusive, and Conjecture 1 on Neyman-Pearson optimality lacks rigorous proof. Third, the cross-validation variance (Section 4.8) indicates that op-
erating point performance exhibits meaningful uncertainty at n = 310.
Future directions. The open problems in Section 5 outline extensions of this work. Most pressing are: (i) rigorous investigation of Conjecture 1, potentially via explicit characterization of the likelihood ratio under Dirichlet perturbations; (ii) distribution shift robustness bounds for calibration transfer across related domains; and (iii) integration with the r-power methodology [12] for distribution-free neutral zones in multi-class settings. The connection between Fisher-Rao geometry and opti-
mal decision theory suggested by Remark 6 merits deeper exploration.
Broader context. As AI systems increasingly inform high-stakes decisions in medicine, criminal justice, and autonomous systems, principled uncertainty quantification becomes essential. This work contributes formal foundations for one approach to this challenge. The theoretical guarantees established here—consistency rates, concentration bounds, and the principled connection to Platt scaling—may prove particularly valuable in applications requiring rigorous validation documentation, where empirical-
 performance benchmarks alone are insufficient.
