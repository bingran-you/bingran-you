UNORTHODOX ALGEBRAS AND THEIR ASSOCIATED UNORTHODOX LOGICS
HANAMANTAGOUDA P. SANKAPPANAVAR
Abstract. This paper grew out of our investigation into a simple, but natural, question: Can “F → T ” be different from “F” and “T”? To this end, we introduce five “unorthodox” algebras that will play a major role, not only in providing a positive answer to the question, but also in their similarity to the 2-element Boolean algebra 2. Yet, they are remarkably different from 2 in many respects. In this paper, we will examine these five algebras both algebraically and logically. We define, and initi-
ate an investigation into, a subvariety, called RUNO1, of the variety of De Morgan semi-Heyting algebras and show that RUNO1 is, in fact, the variety generated by the five algebras. Then several applications of this theorem are given. It is shown that RUNO1 is a discriminator variety and that all five algebras are primal. It is also shown that every subvariety of RUNO1 satisfies the Strong Amalgamation Property and the property that epimorphisms are surjective (ES). It is shown that the lattice of-
 subvarieties of RUNO1 is a Boolean lattice of 32 elements. The bases for all the subvarieties of RUNO1 are also given. We introduce a new logic called RUN O1 and show that it is algebraizable with the variety RUNO1 as its equivalent algebraic semantics. We then present axiomatizations for all 32 axiomatic extensions of RUN O1 and deduce that all the axiomatic extensions are decidable. The paper ends with some open problems.

2. Preliminaries
In this section, we recall some useful results regarding De Morgan semiHeyting algebras. Observe that all the five algebras mentioned above have semi-Heyting reducts. Semi-Heyting algebras were introduced by the author in 1984-85 as an abstraction of Heyting algebras. The first results on these algebras, however, were not published until 2007 (see [37]). An algebra L = ⟨L, ∧, ∨, →, 0, 1⟩ is a semi-Heyting algebra if the following conditions hold:
(SH1) ⟨L, ∧, ∨, 0, 1⟩ is a lattice with 0 and 1, (SH2) x ∧ (x → y) ≈ x ∧ y, (SH3) x ∧ (y → z) ≈ x ∧ [(x ∧ y) → (x ∧ z)], (SH4) x → x ≈ 1.

6
A semi-Heyting algebra is a Heyting algebra if it satisfies:
(x ∧ y) → x ≈ 1.
We will denote the variety of semi-Heyting algebras by SH and that of Heyting algebras by H. Semi-Heyting algebras share some important properties with Heyting algebras; for instance, semi-Heyting algebras are distributive and pseudocomplemented; the congruences on them are determined by filters and the variety of semi-Heyting algebras is arithmetical (for more such properties, see [1, 2, 3, 37]). A key feature of semi-Heyting algebras is the following [3]: Every semiHeyting algebra ⟨A, ∨, ∧, →, 0-
, 1⟩ gives rise naturally to a Heyting algebra ⟨A, ∨, ∧, →H , 0, 1⟩ by defining the implication x →H y as x → (x ∧ y).
The following lemmas will be useful in the sequel.
Lemma 2.1. Let L = ⟨L, ∧, ∨, →, 0, 1⟩ be a semi-Heyting algebra. For a, b, c ∈ L, a ∧ b ≤ c if and only if a ≤ b → (b ∧ c).
Lemma 2.2. [12, Corollary 3.9] Let L = ⟨L, ∧, ∨, →, 0, 1⟩ be a semi-Heyting algebra. For a, b ∈ L, a →H b = 1 if and only if a ≤ b.
3. The variety RUNO1
In this section we define, and investigate, an important subvariety, called “RUNO1” of the variety of dually hemimorphic semi-Heyting algebras.
Definition 3.1. An algebra A = ⟨A, ∧, ∨, →,′ , 0, 1⟩ is a semi-Heyting algebra with a dual hemimorphism (or dually hemimorphic semi-Heyting algebra) if A satisfies the following conditions:
(E1) ⟨A, ∧, ∨, →, 0, 1⟩ is a semi-Heyting algebra, (E2) 0′ ≈ 1, (E3) 1′ ≈ 0, (E4) (x ∧ y)′ ≈ x′ ∨ y′ (∧-De Morgan law).
We will denote the variety of dually hemimorphic semi-Heyting algebras by DHMSH. An algebra A ∈ DHMSH is a dually quasi-De Morgan semiHeyting algebra if A satisfies the following: (DQD1) (x ∨ y)′′ ≈ x′′ ∨ y′′; (DQD2) x′′ ≤ x.
The variety of dually quasi-De Morgan semi-Heyting algebras is denoted by DQDSH.
An algebra A ∈ DQDSH is said to be De Morgan if A |= x′′ ≈ x. An algebra A ∈ DHMSH is said to be unorthodox if A satisfies the axiom: (0 → 1)′ = 0 → 1.

4.2. Properties AP, SAP, ES.
Amalgamation Property (AP, for short) is an important property for varieties to have. We refer the reader to [23] for its history and its connection to algebraic logic (see also [21]). In this subsection we wish to investigate whether V(Ai), i = 1, 2, · · · , 5 have AP, SAP and ES.
Definition 4.13. By a diagram in a class K of algebras we mean a quintuple ⟨A, f, B, g, C⟩ with A, B, C ∈ K and f : A 7→ B and g : A 7→ C embeddings. By an amalgam of this diagram in K we mean a triple ⟨f1, g1, D⟩ with D ∈ K and with f1 : B 7→ D and g1 : C 7→ D embeddings such that f1 ◦ f = g1 ◦ g. If such an amalgam exists, then we say that the diagram is amalgamable in K. If such an amalgam exists and satisfies the condition: f1(B) ∩ g1(C) = f1circf(A), then we say that the diagram is strongly a-
malgamable in K. We say that K has the Amalgamation Property (AP, for short) if every diagram in K is amalgamable. K has the Strong Amalgamation Property (SAP, for short) if every diagram in K is strongly amalgamable.
Remark 4.14. Since f and g in a diagram are embeddings, we can, without loss of generality, simply think of a diagram ⟨A, f, B, g, C⟩ as a triple ⟨A, B, C⟩ such that A ⊆ B ∩ C. Accordingly, the definition of (AP) can be restated.

Consequence Relations and Logics:
• A consequence relation on F mL is a binary relation ⊢ between sets of formulas and formulas that satisfies the following conditions: for all Γ, ∆ ⊆ F mL and φ ∈ F mL : (i) φ ∈ Γ implies Γ ⊢ φ, (ii) Γ ⊢ φ and Γ ⊆ ∆ imply ∆ ⊢ φ, (iii) Γ ⊢ φ and ∆ ⊢ β for every β ∈ Γ imply ∆ ⊢ φ.
• A consequence relation ⊢ is finitary if Γ ⊢ φ implies Γ′ ⊢ φ for some finite Γ′ ⊆ Γ.
• A consequence relation ⊢ is structural if Γ ⊢ φ implies σ[Γ] ⊢ σ(φ) for every substitution σ ∈ Hom(FmL, FmL). (σ[Γ] := {σ(α) : α ∈ Γ}).
• A logic (or deductive system) is a pair L := ⟨L, ⊢L⟩, where L is a propositional language and ⊢L is a finitary, structural consequence relation on FmL.
• Logics in a Hilbert-style:

7.2. Algebraizability of a logic.
Algebraic Semantics for a Logic:
Let ⟨L, ⊢L⟩ be a logic and K a class of L-algebras. K is called an algebraic semantics for ⟨L, ⊢L⟩ if ⊢L can be interpreted in ⊢K in the following sense: There exists a finite set δi(p) ≈ εi(p), for i < n, of identities with a single variable p such that, for all Γ ∪ φ ⊆ F m and each j < n,
Γ ⊢L φ ⇔ {δi[ψ/p] ≈ εi[ψ/p], i < n, ψ ∈ Γ} |=K δj[φ/p] ≈ εj[φ/p],
where δ[ψ/p] denotes the formula obtained by the substitution of ψ at every occurrence of p in δ. The identities δi ≈ εi, for i < n, are called defining identities for ⟨L, ⊢L⟩ and K.
Equivalent Algebraic Semantics and Algebraizable Logic:
Definition 7.1. Let S be a logic over a language L and K an algebraic semantics of S with defining equations δi(p) ≈ εi(p), i ≤ n. Then, K is an equivalent algebraic semantics of S if there exists a finite set {∆j(p, q) : j ≤ m} of formulas in two variables satisfying the condition: For every φ ≈ ψ ∈ EqL,
φ ≈ ψ |=K {δi(∆j(φ, ψ)) ≈ εi(∆j(φ, ψ)) : i ≤ n, j ≤ m} and {δi(∆j(φ, ψ)) ≈ εi(∆j(φ, ψ)) : i ≤ n, j ≤ m} |=K φ ≈ ψ.
The set {∆j(p, q) : j ≤ m} is called an equivalence system.
A logic is algebraizable (in the sense of Blok and Pigozzi) if it has an equivalent algebraic semantics.

The following definition is well known (see [32, page 179]).
Definition 7.2. [32] Let L be a logic in a language that includes a binary connective →, either primitive or defined by a term in exactly two variables. Then L is called an implicative logic with respect to the binary connective → if the following conditions are satisfied:
(IL1) ⊢L α → α.
(IL2) α → β, β → γ ⊢L α → γ.
(IL3) For each connective f in the language of arity n > 0, α1 → β1, . . . , αn → βn, β1 → α1, . . . , βn → αn
⊢L f (α1, . . . , αn) → f (β1, . . . , βn).
(IL4) α, α → β ⊢L β. (IL5) α ⊢L β → α.

Definition 7.3. (Rasiowa) Let L be an implicative logic in L with →. An L-algebra is an algebra A in the language L that has an element 1 with the following properties:

10. CONCLUDING REMARKS
In the preceding sections we saw:
(a) V(A1, A2, A3, A4, A5) = RUNO1.
(b) RUNO1 is a discriminator variety and its first-order theory is decidable. (c) The algebras Ai, i = 1, 2, · · · , 5 are primal. (d) Each of the varieties V(Ai), i = 1, 2, · · · , 5 has the SAP and ES. (e) The lattice of subvarieties of RUNO1 is a Boolean lattice consisting of 32 subvarieties of RUNO1. (f) Equational axioms for all subvarieties of RUNO1. (g) The logic RU N O1 for the variety RUNO1. (h) Axiomatizations for all axiomatic extensions of RU N O1).
The items (b), (c) and (d) above imply that the algebras Ai, i = 1, 2, · · · , 5 share some strong properties with the Boolean algebra 2, from which we can conclude that each of the algebras Ai, i = 1, 2, · · · , 5 (and their logic) could be a formidable competitor to the Boolean algebra 2 in computing. Computer chips based on three-valued logic of any (or all in parallel) of Ai, i = 1, 2, · · · , 4, or based on four-valued logic of A5, could be more useful in future, since they would have more ex-
pressive power than the binary chips.
